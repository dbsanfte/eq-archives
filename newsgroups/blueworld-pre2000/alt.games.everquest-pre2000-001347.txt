	I honestly didn't mean this to be insulting, although I may
have worded it in a way that there was no way for you to take it other
than an insult.  You did, though, snip where I said that I was merely
using it as a metaphor to describe what a person feels while playing a
single person role-playing game.  And I stand by that metaphor as
being very descriptive and useful.   Maybe I shouldn't have directed
it solely towards you, but I thought it was clear that it was intended
to be a description of a universal phenomenon.  If it wasn't, I
apologize for any offence you took. 
	As far as missing your point, I think I understand it very
well.  You think players should have the power to enact change in what
should be *their* world.  After all, they are the ones who inhabit
that world.  I understand this, and I think that, ideally, this would
be great.  But I also think that it is an impossibility in an online
role-playing game.
	My point was (which, if any point was missed, I think it was
this) is that a *multi-player* role-playing game, which we have both
agreed people play to feel empowered, by its nature cannot give any
power to *any* players.  This is simply because the moment people are
able to secure the power necessary to enable change, there will be
other people who *lose* that power.  And if people feel that they are
unable to attain power, for whatever reason, they simply won't play
the game.  Sure, some people will see it as a challenge to try and do
whatever it takes to get the power, but many others, unable to
immediately escape to a world where they are "powerful", will just
quit playing.  Hell, if they wanted to deal with a world where there
are lots of achieving go-getters who they can never hope to equal (or
don't particularly care to equal), they'd go back to the office.  A
lot of this comes down to one of the most fundamental questions  that
plagues games like UO and EQ.  How do the designers balance the game
for people who play maybe an hour or two a night or every other night
and for those people whose lives basically revolve around the game (of
which I know a few - people who play upwards of 60 hours a week)?  The
easiest way is to make it so that *nobody* can change anything, and
leave it up to the players to empower themselves.
	Basically, single-player games and multi-player games both
have their strengths and weaknesses.  Single player games are able to
provide one with a tangible sense of empowerment.  *I* killed the big
bad wizard, and *I* saved the kingdom from certain doom.  Of course,
you do this all by yourself, and only you get any pleasure out of it -
hence, the appropriateness of my comparing it to a form of emotional
masturbation.  Multi-player games, on the other hand, so that everyone
can feel like they are powerful, are not able to give *any* power to
*anyone*, for to do this would be to exclude *someone* and to leave
them feeling like the powerless schlep they are in real life and from
what they are trying to escape.  What they do offer, though, is the
setting, and other people with whom to *pretend* whatever you want
(well, to a certain extent).  Get a group of people together, pretend
Dundee or Magnus is king, and by golly, they *are* king (btw, did you
ever become king, Dundee?  I know there was a movement for it).  This
may not be as satisfying as Dundee or Magnus actually *being* king,
via the game mechanics, but it's the best we can hope for in a
multi-player game.  As far as being able to change things such as
wiping out a threat of monsters, again, if one group of players wipe
those monsters out, then what is left for the next group of players to
wipe out?  And why should the first group of players be the ones who
got the glory of wiping out that spawn of monsters, and not the
second?  By their nature, the tangible aspects of a multi-player game
must not change.  Any changes that happen must take place in the minds
of the players, and nowhere else.  Obviously, that bothers you, but it
doesn't really bother me that much, and I'll still exclusively play
multi player games from now on.  I personally prefer my delusions to
be in the plural rather than the singular ;)  
	So, in closing, all I have to say is that, yeah, it'd be cool
if players could change stuff, and if we truly did rule the world in
which we play.  But that would mean *a lot* of people would be
excluded.  Hence, to keep it fair, *nobody* can be included.  I know
it kind of sounds like some nightmare totalitarian regime where
everyone is forced to be equal, or maybe some wacko plan so that
everyone has good self-esteem and says "I *am* special*,  but, oh
well.  Just so long as the designers don't start saying "all avatars
are equal, but some avatars are more equal than others," I'm happy
with the current situation.  Although, if you think about it, this
thing kind of thing already happens when GM's in UO "bless" houses and
such.  Hmm...interesting.  Oh well, I just thought of this, and I'm
not about to explore it right now - so if someone else cares to do so,
be my guest ;)
