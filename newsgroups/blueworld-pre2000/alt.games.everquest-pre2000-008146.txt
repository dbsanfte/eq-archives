Why as a matter of fact, yes!  Well, sort of.  Passed the NY bar exam
in July and I'm *still* waiting to be sworn in -- currently, I'm
scheduled for late May/June.  Damn bureacracy....

But I digress.  You are correct that the burden of proof in civil
matters is considerably lower than in criminal matters.  Generally,
the former only requires a "preponderance of the evidence," e.g.,
"more likely than not," while the latter requires proof "beyond a
reasonable doubt."  This is why it was easier for the Goldmans to get
money damages from OJ than it was for L.A. to successfully prosecute
him.

But the more crucial issue is this: what criminal law has Microsoft
conceivably violated by including this "back door"?  None that I can
think of.

If a company suffered actual damages as a result of the exploitation
of the back door, a suit against Microsoft would not be outlandish.
As a matter of civil tort law, Microsoft is responsible for the acts
of its employees when they are acting within the scope of their
employment (a theory called "respondeat superior," if you dig Latin).
Like any company that produces a defective product that injures
others, they can be required to pay damages when that defect causes
harm to someone.  But criminal prosecution?  Not hardly.  That just
won't happen, because no criminal law was broken.
