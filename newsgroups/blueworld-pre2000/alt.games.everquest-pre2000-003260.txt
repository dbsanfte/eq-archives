Reclaiming the Swastika

Tibor Krausz

(January 3, 2000) Malaysia, a  multiethnic country of Malays, Chinese
and Indians, with a dominant Muslim  majority, has a bad name for
government-sponsored anti-Semitism.  So it was with some anxiety that
I  recently entered the country. The  ubiquitous swastikas did nothing
to put  me at ease.

In the city of Penang on the picturesque  island of the same name, I
felt like a  character from Art Spiegelman's Holocaust comic strip
"Maus" - lost in a  maze of swastikas. The damn things were all over
the place. In windows.  Over the entrance of a hospital. On Chinese
banner signs. At the point of  pagodas. Even as lines of decoration
under the eaves of houses. All this in  a country that doesn't admit
Israelis; which only a few years ago almost  declared war on the
neighboring city-state of Singapore for defying its  "order" not to
host Yitzhak Rabin on a state visit; and whose just reelected  Prime
Minister Mahathir Mohamad blames Jews for everything from the  recent
Asian economic meltdown to homosexuality in Malaysia.

It was more than enough to make me paranoid. I felt I had "Jew"
written  all over me in bold, accusing letters. When street vendors
and rickshaw  drivers pumped me with their routine "Where are you
from?" as a ploy to  see if I was a well-padded Westerner or some
less-moneyed type, I was  certain they had sensed the Jew in me and
were only shopping for  confirmation. They were ready to beat me up,
for sure.

I sought shelter in a Buddhist temple complex - but it was the worst
possible place for escape, the very den of the swastika. The ornate,
rainbow-colored, dragon-ornamented buildings looked as if they had
been  turned into Adolf Hitler's summer residence. Gigantic swastikas
perched  smugly atop the various temples. Swastikas revolved in
spinning wheels  around the complex. They reposed on the breasts of
armies of Buddha  statues. And one swastika stood on a 20-foot pole in
the center of the  enclosure, fitted with light bulbs for
post-twilight illumination.

I was clearly not the only one getting shivers in the tropical heat.
"This  swastika is good, not bad like yours," a Malay temple guide,
curiously  named Yacob according to his badge, explained to a
flabbergasted and  uncomfortable-looking middle-aged German couple, as
I loitered nearby.  The swastika, he told them, is an ancient Eastern
sun symbol of  universality, standing for the energy of life, the gate
of heaven, and the  Buddha's footprint. It had been a sign for peace,
happiness, and  prosperity for millennia before Adolf Hitler
expropriated it for his Nazi  Party. (The Encyclopaedia Britannica
elaborates: "It was a favorite symbol  on Mesopotamian coinage, it
appeared in early Christian and Byzantine  art... and in India it
continues to be the most widely used auspicious  symbol of Hindus,
Jainas and Buddhists.")

The Eastern swastika, Yacob pointed out, stands on one of its bent
arms,  not on a point like the Nazi Hakenkreuz. "It also brings you
luck," he  added to the two German tourists. Well, not if history is
anything to go by.  But the Germans seemed more relaxed by now.

Then I saw the real thing. This time there was no mistaking it: It had
"NAZI" blazed below it. It glared back at me from a bumper sticker on
the  back of a motorcycle, the implication being that the driver was a
real bad  guy. He was competing in a crazy midnight motorcycle race
through the  near-deserted streets of Penang. I was pleased to see him
left behind in the  dust.
