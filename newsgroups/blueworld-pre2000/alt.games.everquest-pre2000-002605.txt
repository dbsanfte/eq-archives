Hurm, well, many of the better muds have never reached the end of
their lifespan. I don't think three years is a realistic target--it's
too short. :) MUME, Gemstone, Medievia, and of course, most of the
MUD2s, anyone? On Legend, we did a pretty good job of controlling this
deflation, by having strict standards for the allowable ranges of
stats for creatures of a given level, and by having an inheritance
system so that creating creatures was a matter of overloading or
altering a basic template that met the difficulty criteria. And lots
and lots of playtesting and upgrading. It's worked for over five years
now...

I think that ultimately, the answer, as it is for most everything
involving online games, will in some manner involve:

- do it without human admin intervention

>Agreed, but again I submit that if the budgets aren't there to support maintenance

Oh, absolutely there must be a budget for "maintenance teams that keep
the games interesting." Online games of any type which do not have
this die messy deaths. The question then becomes, given said budget,
the demands of the playerbase, and the creative desires of the team,
where do you spend the effort--correcting deflation, adding new
features altogether, extending the elder game, improving the newbie
game in hopes of reducing churnout of novices, etc etc. Always a
juggling act.

>Aren't you just talking about algorithmically keeping pace with deflation as
>opposed to having game designers manually doing it?  I'm not sure we're at the
>point where this could be programmed, and even if it could, I doubt the program
>could even come close to competing against a hand tuned world in terms of providing
>compelling, fun, and interesting content. 

I don't know either. :) That's why I said it was a thought experiment.

I do think that over time, we will move towards less and less
hand-created content in these games as the scope required for the game
grows and the audience increases. Leveraging player-contributed
content and algorithmic and/or "smart tools" content generation is
going to be very important and companies drive towards better profit
margins and larger audiences. A matter of bang for the buck: where
does the hand-tuning help the most, and where can you get away with
fractal map generation, or algorithmically defined creatures?

>  There's quite a big difference between
>algorithmically increasing the speed and quantity of enemies in a Pacman maze and
>algorithmically generating quests, story lines, and adjusting the difficulty level
>of dungeons and outdoor areas in a MMORPG.  I'd rather allocate programming
>resources towards providing my game designers with better and better tools such
>that they could 'hand tune' the world more quickly, allowing them to be creative
>and yet still efficient.

That's certainly the state of the art right now. It will be
interesting to see what happens over time as various pressures are
brought to bear on the industry.
