Let me add to that by saying that EQ does not allow much advancement without
combat.  In UO, I had a wandering healer who, even though the healing skill
was broken, was able to carve out a niche for himself without having to kill
anything.  In fact, he didn't even own a weapon.  (He owned NOTHING.  The
bank was full of bandages.)

In EQ, this is almost impossible.  Level caps on skills mean that a pacifist
healer cannot exist as there are no significant non-combat methods of
advancing.

Not to say that I'm switching back ...  I love EQ.  Any advancement system,
level or stat driven, must be properly implemented if it is to be
successful.  UO's system, when I left, was getting better but still suffered
under the oppressive weight of people who'd exploited it over the year it
had not improved.  If it's worth anything, my opinion is that a new game's
advancement system should be very tight, and perhaps loosen as time goes on
and potential exploits can be identified.  It should not start so loose, as
UO's did.

So, UO's mechanics may be designed to assist roleplaying to a certain depth,
but those mechanics have also always been easily exploited by folks whom
some have accused of ruining the game.  Roleplaying "communities" (PaxLair
on Ches., for example) have to contend with a lot.  EQ's mechanics (races
aside) might not assist in roleplaying as much, but the atmosphere does not
yet stifle it.  In fact, it can be quite successful.  And I'm starting to
see "communities" develop, but player-made "towns" are an impossibility now.

Both games offer combat and PvP to one degree or another.  Good thing in EQ
is that you don't lose all of your possessions every time you die.  Looting
of your corpse is limited or impossible, depending upon your flick of the
PvP switch.

EQ, once you learn the interface, is, in my opinion, less frustrating to the
newbie.  And UO can't touch these graphics which, while no Half Life, are
pretty nice.
