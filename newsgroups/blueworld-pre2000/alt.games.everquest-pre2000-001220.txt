   The 'archetype' system is exemplified by the White Wolf games (Mage/
Vampire/Werewolf/Changeling/Wraith/etc.).  In each, your character chooses
an archetype, which gives the character certain abilities and limitations,
and then the character has broad freedom in choosing additional skills.

   I label 'archetype' games as a class system, because they force you to
make clear choices at the beginning that cannot be completely undone.
Although a Nosferatu might try to emulate a Ventrue, he'll never quite
make it.  :)  Similarly in EverQuest, you could take a paladin, boost its
wisdom and have it specialize in healing, and call it a halfway point
between a paladin and a cleric.  The idea is to allow a complete continuum
of characters, but with several fixed starting points.

   Pure skill-based systems give the player no starting point; you can be
whatever you wish.  This is good, if you're truly interested in 
role-playing and willing to create a realistic character, with the flaws
and weaknesses all realistic characters have.  

   However, this system breaks down, on-line and on paper, as soon as you 
start to play with the guy who wants to be able to do everything.  The
powergamer isn't interested in realism or even role-playing, and he'll
create characters who look like cookie-cutter versions of the 'best' 
character possible in the game system.  These people are not much fun to
play with...and I'll tell you, sad as it is, they outnumber us.  :/

   Skill-based systems are fine in mature, friendly playing groups.  I've
yet to have a successful paper RPG campaign in a skill-based system when
the group included people I did not know well.  And on-line gamers are
not generally referred to in terms such as 'mature' or 'friendly'.  In
my opinion, skill-based systems are not suitable for on-line RPG gaming.

   But again, this *is* all just a matter of taste...
