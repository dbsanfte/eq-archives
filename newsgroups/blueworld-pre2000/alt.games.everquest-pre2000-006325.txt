Philip Brown wrote:
>
> it is not a normal scenario that you present someone a
> contract whrein you are the recipiet of the service (or whatever).
> Generally the provider offers the contract to the user.

That asymmetry is what i wish to correct.

> They don't need to be equal parties - merely one or more parties
> receiving something in return for consideration in terms of an
> agreement.

I distinguish equal (in rights) from identical (in all particulars).
In contractual arrangements, i'm firmly of the conviction that
all signatories are equal in rights.

> No - they need not physically sign it under some circumstances as
> mentioned above.  Remember that verbal contracts are enforceable in
> Australia - the main point about something being signed is that it's
> pretty good proof of intent and acceptance.

Yes, whereas disputes over verbal contracts are often
one person's word against another's.

> > ... how many contracts offered by a company
> > do _not_ have a signature of an authorised officer
> > representing the company?  Any?
> 
> Plenty.  How often do people sign a document offered to them that is
> "terms and conditions" or "acceptable use policy" or whatever. ...

What if i edit that before signing?

> As I said, signing just makes things easy to prove,
> but aren't needed to make it valid (in Australia at least,
> things vary in different jurisdictions of course).

What then is to prevent a company from "deeming" that
i've agreed to a contract that i've never seen or
heard of?  I might not even have dealt with the company
in any way, direct or indirect.  They might take my name
from the phonebook then assert that i've agreed to a
contractual arrangement.  If the law does not require
_my_ signature, the company can easily forge documents
by the truckload "confirming" that i agreed.
