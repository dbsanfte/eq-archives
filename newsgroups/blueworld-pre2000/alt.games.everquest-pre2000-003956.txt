This is a rather annoying thread, and yet in some ways interesting because it
touches (very peripherally) on medical ethics.  When I hear the term
"euthanasia" I think the most current use of the word refers to medical
euthanasia.  This might just be my bias being in the medical field, as it
might be the bias of a historian to think of another use of the word.

If by "mercy killing" you refer to medical "euthanasia", then I personlly
would not equate that with "random mindless killing".  Current medical ethics
dictates that if appropriate treatment is given to easy pain and suffering and
secondarily results in the death of the patient, it is ethically acceptable.
It is called something like "secondary effect".  The most common example of
this situation used in discussion is a terminal cancer patient in extreme pain
and not expected to survive more than a short time (hours to days).  Maximally
safe doses of morphine have been reached and the patient is still in extreme
pain.  Increasing the morphine in an attempt to afford the patient more
comfort even though there is a risk of respiratory depression is an ethically
acceptable treatment.  I don't have my Code of Medical Ethics handy, but I
have taken enough medical ethics courses to know this is fairly accurate.

There are those who may disagree with this idea, and ethics change, but it is
the current ethical standard in medicine.
