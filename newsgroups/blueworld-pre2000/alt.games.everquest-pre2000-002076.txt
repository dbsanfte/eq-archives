>Why do you predict lower retention rates overall? 

Ha sto do with the nature of the playerbase attracted to such things,
and the typical play pattern evolution of different types of players.
Social types tend to evolve into hermetic, insular cliques, which
migrate out of the game with great ease once their ties are completely
with each other and not with the game itself.

Achiever types, powergamers, tend to evolve into either socializers,
or turn to PvP as a new ladder of advancement. As long as this ladder
remains enticing, they will remain with the game.

Basically, retention is driven by the ladders that remain to be
conquered in the game, and by the level of ownership in the game that
players have. So for example, we have very very clear statistics that
those who own houses keep paying for UO for much longer than those who
do not, because of ownership. And those who are involved in the game's
politics, PvP, and social structure, tend to remain longer as well, as
opposed to just playing casually from time to time, or who just pop in
for adventures with their friends.

>Roleplaying doesn't have to involve conflict.  What conflict is there

>is just as limiting to roleplaying as artificially restricting them not

>play (i.e. that of combatant).

This is the crux of the argument. In mud design circles it is phrased
as "Marion's Tailor Dilemma" after Marion Griffith, who runes the
!Overlord Project to foment roleplaying in muds. The formal statement
of it is:

---
Suppose Marian is (role)playing a tailor and in the game that is
a feasible profession. She learns the requisite skills and enjoys
her work, designing clothing for other players, and the opportunity
it provides to talk with many other players.

Along comes Boffo who doesn't like Marian, Tailors in general or is
just in a bad mood. He attacks, and kills, Marian, loots her shop
and leaves her to pick up the pieces.

The question now is: who should protect Marian from this? Marian
herself, being a tailor, has neither the skill nor the interest
in learning to fight and should arguably not be bothered with it.
---

This is not a resolved dilemma in online game design. The flip side is
that protecting Marian involves eliminating the expression of the
conflict (in fact, it usually involves pushing that dislike into
verbal harassment, which then requires human admin intervention to
resolve).
