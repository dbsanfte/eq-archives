    I'll save you the trouble, Bunboy.  McGautha was the Supreme Court's
opinion that permitted states to allow juries unfettered discretion in the
imposition of the death penalty, while in the subsequent Furman opinion a
plurality of the Court partially overruled McGautha and required states to
give juries some sort of guidelines in imposing the death penalty.  Both
cases were responding to the question of jury nullification (or juror
nullification as it is sometimes called).

    The point is that nullification is not so much a principle as it is a
phenomenon.  Particularly in criminal cases, where the prosecution has very
limited rights of appeal, and virtually none at all after a full blown jury
trial, juries can and occasionally do completely ignore the law and decide
to acquit the defendant because, for example, they believe the law under
which the defendant is being prosecuted shouldn't be enforced, or they
believe that the mandatory punishment doesn't fit the crime.  And there's
not much of anything the prosecution or the judge can do about it.  The
prosecution can't appeal the verdict, nor can the judge throw out the
verdict, without violating the constitutional guarantee of trial by jury or
the prohibition against double jeopardy.  And it does happen--witness the
fact that Kevorkian was acquitted not once but twice under Michigan's
assisted suicide law even though the undisputed facts plainly demonstrated
that he had violated that law.  But those two juries apparently decided that
the law was idiotic and refused to convict him.

    Whether or not juries do or should have the right of nullification is a
fertile ground for debate--better suited for another group than here (we are
way off-topic by now).  But it does happen, and (God forbid) unless and
until the Constitution is altered to abrogate the right to trial by jury and
the prohibition against double jeopardy, it will continue to be implicitly
sanctioned by the Constitution.
