A Note on the SOJ (Stone of Jordan) Economy:
So... how and why was the ring, known as the Stone of Jordan, selected by the masses as a universal trading currency? Why not perfect skulls? Why not rare items themselves? Of the many reasons, 6 tend to stand out as to why this occurred:

I): Stones Are Rare: It is rather difficult for the average player to acquire a great deal of stones. Even with sniffers, ultra-uber ww barbs, and mass % to find magic items, stone finding still takes time. Even the most dedicated gamblers have a difficult time getting more than one stone every 2 hours. 1 in 33 rings viewed will be a unique or rare. Unless VERY low-level characters are used, (which has it's OWN subset of problems), rings appear in about 1 in 5 trading screens. Multiplying that by the chance a unique item will generate, 1 in every 165 gambling screens will contain a Stone. (This is of course, is the average, a pattern that only becomes apparent when you consider thousands of people in the equation. Your luck may be considerably worse, or a little better). This makes them rare, and hence, valuable.

II): A Stone Has Pre-Requisites: The pre-requisites of having both a nagelring and a manhold heal, a requirement that most new players are totally unaware of, makes stones un-likely to drop from monsters or chests. Further compounding this issue, there are enough stones out there, that their presence in games (especially past normal mode), further reduces the chance of the ring generating. Thus, only those individuals specially rigged to find them are acquiring stones. A controlled amount of them are being found, like any currency, which keeps the value relatively constant.

III): Stones Have Dramatic Powers: The effect of a stone on any character is noticeable, particularly spell castors. +1 to all skills, 25% to mana and 20 to mana, as well as an appreciable amount of lightning damage.... No other ring (currently) can replicate (or even have) most of these skills. Thus, the item has value to all classes - Sorceresses and Necromancers because they can WEAR it, Amazons, Barbarians, and Paladins, because they can TRADE it with the latter classes.

IV): Stones Are Small: Occupying only one inventory slot, a mule character can carry many dozen's of stones, or a barbarian in the field can have a few tucked away for surprise barter goods. While some uniques are worth MORE than a Stone of Jordan, few as covienently sized. The item carries the most worth for its size. (Note: The eye of etlich at 6% life steal is worth significantly more than a stone, but it is simply too rare to become a trading standard. Stones are common enough to circulate as currency).

V): Stones Are Not Random: They have set powers and abilities. Other than it's palette, one stone is exactly like the other, which differs, from say, the totally random eye of etlich. A stone is stable, and a player understands exactly what he is getting. When there is no randomness to an item, people can agree on it's worth, rather then fighting over it's mods.

VI): The Players Have Ascribed Value To Stones: Another note, more important than the others, is the fact that the Diablo 2 trading community as a whole has accepted the worth of a stone. A large part of the mystique surrounding the Stone was generated upon release of the game, and the mysterious ring was netting REAL world prices of over $150.00 (US). The aura stuck, even after Geoff Frazier and Jarulf publicly revealed the necessity of possessing the other unique rings before a stone would generate. Once stones started to proliferate, they were not longer considered priceless, and they began to enter the trade market. It was not a great leap for people to start ascribing a stone value to powerful rares, which evolved into the system we have today. No other item has achieved this. An attempt was made with skulls, but those people were by and large laughed at. Skulls, while valuable for their transmuting properties, are too common, and their benefits, a RANDOM chance of a decent rare item, make them expendable, viewed on par with a full rejuvenation potion. Valuable, but clearly, not worth a decent rare weapon, in any number. (Literally hundreds of skulls would be necessary to acquire a high end rare. Additionally, the perception in the trading community surrounding all gems has never been positive. This stigma sticks, even in light of the new horodric cube formula. Perhaps the expansion pack will change this, adding more diversity to the game, but until then, this system will probably stand.


How To Read This Guide:
To interpret the value of an item listed, you are provided with several categories, which you can combine to find the general value of a rare item in stones. (This list has a "fudge" rate of +/- two stones). The first info given will be a brief description of the sub-category the object resides in. Next comes the MINIMUM requirements an item must have to be stone worthy. These are always preceded by roman numerals. (Example: I):, II):, III), etc...). Unless the item meets these requirements, it is probably NOT worth stones. (Exceptions do exist). The third chart will show a list of the modifiers, (referred to as "mods"), most requested by the players of battle.net. These powers and abilities enhance the value of the item by the number of stones listed. Letters always precedes the respected modifiers table. (Example: A):, B): C):, etc...). The next list contains the items at their base value. Remember - all listed items are assumed to have met the minimum requirements. If an item does not, it is probably also not worth spending time trying to trade it. (Again, there are always exceptions). Certain special rules exist for determining the value of a rare, but these are documented with an *, and a footnote below the sub-section, so finding them should not be difficult. The final catagory proceeds the items themselves, and is a brief list of the character classes most likely to be interested in that particular item. It is often difficult to convince a necromancer of a 150+ executioner's sword's worth , or to impress most barbarians with a rare +2 wand. This catagory is designed to help the truely inept find their target audience. If I have made this sound more complex than it is, simply browse through the document, and it's proper use should become pretty obvious. Hope I didn't confuse ya... on with the guide......

The Rule of Three:
There is one final factor when calculating an item's value in stones. This is a law, known as "The Rule of Three." Simply put, this guideline takes into account that certain items are worth vastly more when they posses a select set of powers, which play off one another in such a way as to exponentially increase the item's value - more so than the list would otherwise indicate. Thus, if an item has any three powers from the Rule of Three list, (Rule of three mods are Highlighted on the Respected Modofiers Table, with a #), the item's total value, (after all mods have been calculated), is multiplied by 2.5.

Exceptional Weapons:
When looking for a weapon, never forget the most important fact: It's all about the damage. Without great damage, no item, regardless of its powers, will be worth much. The fighting classes need high damage to best capitalize on life and mana steal gear, and will scoff at weapons lacking the ability to lay down some extreme pain. Several things set the base value of an exceptional weapon:

I): Having all three of the damage modifying prefixes/suffixes. (Kings, merciless, and slaughter).
II): The popularity of the item in question. A lance for example, is worth much more than a gothic axe.
III): The item must usually be the pinnacle of its item type, i.e. the most potent exceptional version in the weapon group. Example: An Executioner's sword will trade well, but a Dacian Falx probably won't.

Unique Items:
Unique items, since they always have set values, form the base currency of the battle.net trading world. A Stone of Jordan is considered "one dollar" and the lesser uniques often take on the role of change. A few of the rarer uniques are actually worth more than a stone, but this is due mostly to the difficulty in getting them, as gambling costs are prohibitively high. (Particularly on armor). Please note: Although a specific number of a certain item may be WORTH a stone, it is doubtful that 10 treds of cthon will ever net you a stone. Most of these objects serve the purpose of change or bartering leverage. Cheaply gambled uniques, while useful, will not hold much value on their own. Set Items
FAR more common that rares, set items are generally not worth trading. However, certain items are potent enough to be of obvious use to any character, and thus, retain at least SOME worth. Useful set items are more often used to enhance a trade, or as a leveraging tool, rather than a point of interest. It is EXTREAMLY unlikely that anyone will trade a stone for any number of rare items. They are garnish, like parsley, no nutritional value, but they look pretty on a plate.

Socketed Items*:
Rarely are socketed items considered of value on the "stone" market. The relatively weak powers offered by most gems severely de-valued socketed gear, which is a shame. It was a wonderful concept, reminiscent Final Fantasy VII, and had it been made useable past normal mode, giving players the ability to heavily customize their gear would have greatly added to the game's fun factor. But such is not the case - hopefully the LoD expansion pack will address this grievous wrong. However, despite the apparent weakness of socketed items, there ARE a few, (particularly socketed exceptionals), that can actually command a decent price when trading.

Gems:
Offering to trade gems, post 1.05c, would have caused a trader to become the laughing stock of the channel. Gems, as a general rule, are too common, and the powers they offer too weak to have any real trading backbone. However, version 1.4 introduced a new horodric cube formula: (6 perfect skulls + 1 rare item = a new, randomly generated rare item.) This new capability has added immeasurably to the value of skulls. Diamonds and topazes are somewhat valuable as well, but only as "leverage." It is extremely doubtful that any number of these two gems would trade for a stone of jordan. Skulls MIGHT trade for stones, although, stand alone, this is also unlikely.
