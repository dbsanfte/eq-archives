Newsweek Does EQ...
Newsweek released an article entitled Farewell, Hack-and-Slash? by Erika Check that talks about RPGs and specifically EQ. Here are some highlights:
THEIR MISSION IS TO KILL, and forget about parents, teachers and homework. And the cycle never ends: when one level ends, another begins at the click of a mouse.

At least that’s what some parents and politicians say. They may not know about a growing countertrend in gaming circles: titles that depart from the shoot-’em-up model to promote positive social values. One such game is EverQuest. Released almost two years ago, it’s a vast online universe of more than a quarter-million players exploring, chatting and making friends.

EverQuest is a traditional role-playing game in many ways. Combat is still an integral part of it, but bloodshed is not the ultimate goal, and the teen-rated game isn’t gory. Here, characters interact with each other, defeating enemies, trading items or giving advice. “The attraction of online games is people, and lots of them,” says Carl Fenley, a 31-year-old gamer. “Online games, like EverQuest, are kind of like the bar Cheers—it’s always open and everybody knows your name.”

That’s no accident. Unlike traditional games that have simply been adapted to the Net, EverQuest forces players to rely on each other. “We were looking for how to make role-playing games better, and the best focus was creating cooperative play,” says Jeff Butler, a producer at Verant Interactive, the San Diego-based maker of EverQuest. At the start of the game, players choose specialized characters. Each character has different skills. For instance, shamans can heal other players, and warriors are formidable fighters. Half elves are more intelligent than dwarfs, but dwarfs are stronger. No type of player is superior, so different players often form teams to profit from each other’s skills. Some teams, called “guilds,” are coalitions of players who “quest” together and may consider other guild members friends. Guilds are small communities within the whole. Fenley started his own guild with friends he met through the game. That guild now has 70 players, plus a Web site and message board where members talk outside the game.

EverQuest players cooperate in other ways, too. Once you buy the software, you connect to the game’s servers via the Internet (a credit card gets you a user name and password). Next, you create characters, choose a starting world and begin your quest, learning spells and killing creatures to gain experience or items, like money. You can then buy advanced training sessions, weapons and other items, which allow you to fight bigger enemies, gain experience and become more powerful. Thousands of players meet in the online worlds, appearing and disappearing as they log on or off from their own computers. New gamers—a.k.a. “newbies”—may feel bewildered, but usually get help if they ask for it. Experienced players might even save others in tough situations. “I love the social dimension of EQ,” says 36-year-old Virginia Carter, from Brevard, N.C. “It’s like a huge convention—meeting people from all over the world, and perhaps some even in my neighborhood.”

Verant’s pro-social strategy isn’t just a touchy-feely novelty. It makes good business sense. “To prolong the longevity of the game, you don’t want to promote people playing alone for a few hours and then quitting,” says Butler. Especially since EverQuest relies on a $10 monthly subscription fee. So far, Verant’s strategy seems to be working. Butler says more than half the players who buy the game keep playing. That makes 285,000 active subscribers. Fans say the social focus is key to the game’s success: “It will be a long time before any other games can offer the challenge I get from playing against real—and thus unpredictable—people I meet online,” says Jennifer Bowen, a 28-year-old mother who’s been playing EverQuest since its inception.

For now, massively multiplayer online games are a drop in the software industry’s $6.1 billion bucket. The genre is still new—another popular title is the classic Ultima Online—and the games require an immense start-up investment and constant upgrades. But these games might offer a glimpse of the future. As virtual worlds become more compelling, our networked lives may become as real as—or more real than—our physical ones. After all, says Fenley, “you can scream at the TV until your throat is sore and have no effect whatsoever.” But in the online universe, everyone can hear you scream.

No Attack Command For Pets
One player asked how come pets havent been given a No Attack command yet and Alan replied:
Well, primarily because I haven’t proposed such a thing to the team yet. And the primary reason that I haven’t done that is because I haven’t seen them in several days, we’re still on vacation.

But let me ask you this: Should pets really be that easy to control?

My personal answer would be no. As it is now, pets are a challenge. And they are very, very strong. The drawback to that strength is that fact that they aren’t too bright. Giving the caster more control over his pet might make them too good...

More About EQs Revamping
When Brad said they were reworking some zones that concerned some players saying it "scared" them. Brad responded with:
It doesn’t scare me at all. Yes, it’s possible that we’ll make some of those places harder. It’s also possible that we’ll go the other way and make them more accessible for the lower level crowd... Right now we don’t know. We might add more locks, traps, quests, etc. But we’ll pay close attention to the availability of dungeons for all level groups.

I have a love of those underused zones as well. I often like to hang out in those places just to avoid crowding.

In any case, we’ll be keeping a good sense of the overall game whenever we make changes.

December 28, 2000
More On EQ Plans
Brad added a second post later to comment in more detail on his coming EQ changes:
Yes, I forgot to mention we are still planning on getting to the point where we consider the classes balanced. We are targetting the first or second quarter 2001 for this.

Also, a general comment I forgot to make, which is pretty exciting:

Currently, the EQ Live/Expansion teams have almost as many people as the original EQ development team! Thanks to the continued success of EverQuest in general and our two expansions specifically, we can easily justify a very large team to continue working on the game. From our perspective, EQ has a VERY long life ahead of it and SOE/Verant is very committed to support and enhancement.

In fact, the EQ Live/Expansion teams are actually larger than some of the teams working here on new MMOGs. I think it's important that our players are aware of all this, that while we are working on several other premium games (Planetside, Sovereign, Star Wars Galaxies, and a couple of unnannounced titles), EQ is still just as important (if not more important at this time, since it's generating income ) and has just as large (if not larger) of a team and committment behind it as any of our other titles.

Thanks both to a very experienced team (I think we have more 50+ players on the Live/Expansion teams than ever before), full of both original EQ dev team members as well as all sorts of newer members that have joined us from all over, as well as the enormous amount of great ideas and feedback we get from this board and elsewhere, we've all sorts of exciting plans for this game in the future!

Where is EQ headed?
Brad comments on the direction he plans to take EQ:
Here's the rough plan:

1. Finish up last minute Velious tuning.
2. Massive re-vamp of underused dungeons in the old world, implementing Kunark/Velious style loot/quests/mob spawning/etc. This will include both high level and mid level zones.
3. Add at least 2 free zones, probably focussed on Odus.
4. Begin work and planning for the next big thing (yes, I'm purposely being vague here, for now).

Also, you should see more trade skill upgrades, more interface upgrades, general engine upgrades, etc.
