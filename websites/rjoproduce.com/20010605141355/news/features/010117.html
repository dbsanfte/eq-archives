Growers face another cold night

Rolling blackouts also are a risk as temperatures fall toward 25.

By Robert Rodriguez
The Fresno Bee
(Published January 17, 2001)

Central and southern San Joaquin Valley citrus growers hoped Tuesday to make it through another night of subfreezing temperatures as forecasted lows were expected to reach 25 degrees.

The National Weather Service in Hanford issued a freeze warning Tuesday afternoon, and another one is possible today. Overnight temperatures Tuesday evening were expected to dip to between 25 degrees to 32 degrees on the Valley floor with durations of one to four hours.

Agriculture officials said Tuesday morning's cold temperatures caused little damage, if any.

"We squeaked through all right," said Dennis Plann, Fresno County deputy agricultural commissioner. "Now we are taking a look at what is coming."

The season's icy weather prompted some citrus growers to flip on frost-protection tools, including wind machines and irrigation water.

Tulare County ranks as the leading citrus producer in the state, generating a $349 million crop in 1999.

Gary Kunkel, assistant Tulare County agricultural commissioner, said growers could see some cosmetic damage with temperatures as low as 24 degrees, regardless of the duration. Once the temperature drops that low, any moisture on the orange rind will freeze, causing rust-colored stains.

This time of year, growers keep a close watch on their groves, monitoring temperature gauges and using frost-protection devices such as wind machines and irrigation water.

Grower Bill Ferry Jr., monitored his Tulare County groves all night Monday, running about a dozen of his 60 wind machines in areas with the coldest temperatures.

The towering machines pull warmer air closer to ground level while irrigation water is pumped into the fields from deep wells, where it emerges at nearly 70 degrees. Farmers say both methods can provide a three- to four-degree cushion of protection.

Although Ferry expected another sleepless night Tuesday, his concern was compounded by the threat of losing power.

The state's utility regulator issued a warning Tuesday that electrical power reserves were reaching critical levels and users were urged to cut back to avoid rolling blackouts.

A farmer's worst nightmare during this energy crisis is to lose electricity while operating wind machines or pumping water. Ferry estimated that about 30% of the industry's wind machines run on electricity, as do nearly all the water pumps.

Valley legislators want to grant citrus growers an exemption from rolling blackouts when the temperature drops below 32 degrees.

"If they do have blackouts, we hope we are as far away from that as possible," Ferry said.

Pacific Gas & Electric Co. officials acknowledge that agriculture is in a unique situation but say they can't grant an exemption from rolling blackouts. Such action can only come from either Gov. Davis, the Public Utilities Commission or the Legislature.

Christy Dennis, PG&E spokeswoman, also said that while the company wants its customers to conserve during peak hours, agriculture may not be able to do so during a hard freeze.

"Agriculture is the livelihood of the Valley, so there are some exceptions," Dennis said.
