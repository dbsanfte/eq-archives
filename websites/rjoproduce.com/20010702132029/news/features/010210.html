Bitter year for Sunkist

Ex-leader's exit, revenue dip mark growers' meeting.

By Robert Rodriguez
The Fresno Bee
(Published February 10, 2001)

VISALIA -- Riverside grower Cal Knauer can tell you how lousy the 1999-2000 citrus season was: He didn't make a dime.

"I did my job, growing some of the best navel oranges," said a frustrated Knauer. "But I am financially broke."

Knauer made his sobering point Friday during Sunkist Growers' annual meeting held at the Visalia Convention Center. More than 1,000 grower-members of the marketing cooperative attended the event.

While Knauer's situation may have been the extreme, cooperative officials acknowledge that last year's citrus harvest was dismal.

Total revenues were just less than $847 million, down 2% from the previous year. And payments to growers also dropped 3% to $682 million.

Sunkist's acting president, James H. Mast, attributed the poor showing to several factors including poor quality, retail consolidation and foreign competition.

Spain has increased its supply of clementines, putting it in competition with Western navels, and Valencia oranges now have to contend with summer deciduous fruit as well as Australian navels.

In a move praised by some growers, Mast also announced that Sunkist would no longer market lemons from Argentina, a citrus rival whose entry into U.S. markets was opposed by several citrus industry groups.

Organizations, including the Exeter-based California Citrus Mutual, fought Argentina's imports because of the potential for bringing in harmful pests or diseases.

Mast said Sunkist agreed to market a portion of the Argentine crop as a way to "handle some of the competitive volume."

But after revaluating the agreement recently, Sunkist chose not to continue.

Pierre Tada, president of Limoneira Co., believes the deal went sour after American consumers rejected the foreign lemons. "I think they just didn't buy it," Tada said.

Riverside grower Knauer also pressed Sunkist officials to explain the sudden departure of former chief executive officer Vincent Lupinacci, who left four months ago after serving 21/2 years with the cooperative.

Mast and board chairman Al Williams stressed that Lupinacci was not forced out.

Williams, did say however, that members of Sunkist's executive committee had a "minisession" with Lupinacci just before his resignation to talk about the cooperative's most serious challenges.

Williams said Lupinacci told him he was leaving for personal and health reasons.

"Maybe, it was the pressure of the job," Williams said.

Growers such as Charlie Rudd of Ojai weren't happy with Lupinacci's departure. "He had a lot of ideas and these guys running things don't change easy," Rudd said. "But something has to happen, we can't continue the same way."

Fellow Ojai grower Dick Burgess agreed, saying that Sunkist -- the nation's most recognizable brand of citrus fruit -- must work harder at regaining consumer confidence in its products.

Mast is aware that one of the cooperative's major challenges is to increase the visibility and value of Sunkist fruit. He highlighted several marketing promotions for U.S. and Canadian markets that include vintage labels on bagged Sunkist oranges, a Volkswagen giveaway and new decorative crates.

"In order to survive and profit, we are going to have to stand out with our products and our performance," Mast said.

"And that one word -- Sunkist -- can make a difference."
