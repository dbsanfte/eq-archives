This is our Out Of Character etiquette that all players are expected to observe. It contains our express description of what is considered in good taste and the penalty for non compliance.

Posting is the most powerful arena to display the skill of the Pirate. The exploits of our Crew are always intended to become larger than life. Their eternal struggle to show clever bravado helps them intimidate more powerful foes into submission. From raiding party spoils to the daily life of the veteran sailor, all have their importance in creating the full swashbuckling stage. Feel free to post any in character (IC) accounts on the boards. The more the better, tell the realms and your mates tales of quests, challenges, PvP victories or encounters, etc.

Pirates of Neverwinter POSTING POLICY Since posting is a major activity for The Pirates of Neverwinter, the following policy has been designed to let members know what sort of posting is generally encouraged and that which is frowned upon by the leadership of the Guild. Fortunately, the guidelines are quite simple:

  • Posts that violate most general community standards are strictly prohibited. If a board is public, common sense dictates that situations and conversations be acceptable to a general audience.
  • Members are to refrain from the type of post that is generally referred to as "talking trash". In other words, insults that are out of character, provocative, or generally in poor taste are to be avoided. Conflict with other guilds is allowed, but please keep all such posts in character, tasteful, and meaningful. For example:
    POOR POSTS:
    "Shut up you wuss!" "Shaddup ye landlubbin' wuss!" (An accent doesn't make it O.K. )
    Better POST:
    "Many were the illusory visions of a Reaver too long at sea. Dolphins became leviathans, manatee appeared as mermaids and foul mouthed phantoms became real people. So it was that Greyden touched the torque at his neck, its magic clearing his mind and dispelling the false image."
  • Members are to refrain from replying to any posts that do not meet our own standards. Acknowledging such posts only encourages the antagonistic author to post more of the same.

    The goal of these guidelines is to keep our Guild and Role-Play folders free of the insults and trashtalk that fills so many of the other guild's folders. These posts add nothing to the role play of the game, and more often than not simply generate bad feelings. We maintain that it is possible to have conflict in character without generating it out of character, and anyone who reads our folder regularly can see that we prove this time and time again.

    Pirates of Neverwinter WRITER'S GUIDE Participating in shared fiction is one of the most interesting activities of the Pirates of Neverwinter. New stories are posted in the guild folder constantly, and many of these stories are shared efforts. One story line may have two, three, or even a dozen different authors, all contributing their own particular style and characters. The plot is usually not decided in advance. Instead, each author posts whenever he or she sees fit, and makes whatever plot advances or twists seem appropriate at the time. The end result is a living, breathing story whose conclusion nobody can predict.

    The process itself is quite simple. An author (usually, but not always, a crew member) starts a story and leaves it incomplete. Another member then adds to it, advancing the plot. This process continues until that chapter or encounter is complete. Most authors use their Reaver character as the center of their own particular portions of the story, though this is not mandatory. Many authors make use of NPCs (characters that do not have any player counterpart within any game or are supporting roles) to advance the plot. Some examples of NPCs in the past have included a halfling butler named Nubbs, a weasel faced Informant, and a Nemesis Captain determined to destroy the vessel, _Wicked Wench_. Some NPCs have even become major players in a story line, (Kiko was originally an NPC created by Venimeux) sometimes more active than the author's own character.

    Of course, any attempt at shared fiction which involves several authors has its pitfalls. Since the stories are not planned out in advance, often one author's contribution will drastically alter the plans of another. Sometimes an author will have to discard several pages that were written off-line after reading the latest additions. While this is all part of the fun of shared fiction, a certain amount of discretion is advised when writing additions. To this end, the following guidelines have been devised to help new authors enjoy this facet of PRoW. Please keep in mind they are only guidelines, not hard and fast rules. Still, any author should consider carefully before discarding them.

  • 1) NEVER KILL ANOTHER PLAYER'S MAIN CHARACTER WITHOUT THEIR EXPRESS CONSENT!! This is the most sacred guideline of shared fiction. Most players are very fond of their characters, and it simply is not acceptable to kill them off in a story without their permission. Keep in mind this permission is not likely to be given, unless the player plans to retire that particular character for good. This is the one rule that should never be broken for any reason.

    2) Never inflict serious harm on another player's character unless you have their consent, or offer a clear and immediate "cure" within the story. Most players who involve their characters in a story line wish to keep that character active within the story. Hence, any maiming, dismemberment, etc. is usually an unwelcome turn of events if it happens without their permission. However, if you take certain steps this can still be an acceptable plot twist. For example, should you decide that a certain character should get hit with a devastating spell, you might mention in passing that he or she was near a temple at the time of the atrocity. Therefore, a quick trip to the local healer could cure the character handily, allowing that individual to continue in the story unimpaired. Note that cuts, bruises and other wounds are quite acceptable to hand out, even rather nasty ones.

    3) Portray other players' characters respectfully. Don't make another players' character, guild, etc. look foolish or inept in your story telling. This, of course, is not relevant if the other player is intentionally playing a foolish or inept character. In that case, have a ball. A safe guideline is to simply make minor references to other character's actions. Don't make any major decisions for them or have them take any major actions. The result will often be the other player reading your post and thinking "My character would NEVER do that!" When in doubt, ask.

    4) Respect other authors' NPC's. NPC's, while usually not as important as players' characters, are still to be treated carefully by those authors who did not introduce them into the story line. When an author introduces an NPC, it's usually for a good reason that may not become clear for some time. Any major changes to that NPC (like death) could drastically alter the course of the story, possibly ruining that author's plans. Some extra leeway may be taken in this area, however, as ruining an author's plan is not necessarily a bad thing, and most authors are fully aware that once an NPC is introduced he or she becomes fair game for other authors. In other words, you are free to make alterations to another author's NPC's, but do so with discretion.

    5) Remember your own character's limitations and mortality. While we all like to play the hero, remember your character is a mortal being with limitations. Don't try to solve every problem yourself. Powerful magic usually comes at a high price. We teleport back and forth, healing and swallowing potions in a matter of seconds in game and forget that it should take hours or even days to recuperate from fighting and spellcasting. Your character's strengths are all the more dramatic after his or her weaknesses have been examined as well. If your character tends to solve every situation single handed, this means other characters will be stuck as second stringers most of the time. There's plenty of glory to go around, so let others share in it. Also, failures can be just as interesting to read about as victories.

    6) Be consistent with your character's in game abilities. The shared fiction of Pirates of Neverwinter functions as a sub-activity of various gaming worlds. Therefore, all the "rules" of the game or world apply. Keep your character's actions in keeping with their class. Rely on his or her talents to deal with problems and challenges. Each class will have its own particular spells or skills for dealing with a situation. Non fighters usually rely on spells or stealth instead of brute strength. Also, remember that most magic takes time. This should be accounted for in any writings.

    7) Don't resolve complex situations too quickly. When writing about an event that is large in scope, such as a major battle, let it evolve slowly. Shared fiction is a slow process, and should be treated as such. Allow every author a chance to get involved, and leave them openings to weave a new thread into the story. A single battle could take dozens of posts to resolve if there are many characters involved in it. If you end it too abruptly, some characters may not get to see any action at all.

    8) Maintain the plausibility of the story at all times. This is more of a general writing guideline than a guideline about shared fiction, but it couldn't hurt to mention it. When a situation is finally resolved, resolve it in a believable fashion. Don't rely on divine intervention, magical items that had not been mentioned previously, or the like. If you can't find a believable way out of a tough situation, suck it up and take it like a Pirate. The other authors won't allow anything too terrible happen to your character. (If for some reason it does, revenge is always fun and makes for great tales!) Everything should be clearly plausible within the laws of magic and combat in your current universe. Mundane travel takes time, even though we jog from the local market to the outer land in minutes where it should take hours, days.

    9) Keep In-Character vs. Out-of-Character knowledge consistent. Apparently the reader is aware of the entire plot unfolding, of the inner thoughts each character explores and many other things. Sometimes the irony of the who knows or does NOT know certain things is what drives a plot or subplot. Each individual character does not usually possess such knowledge, or if they can even glimpse such things, it should be explained and perhaps even compensated by risks or negative consequences. Unless your character is psychic, clairvoyant, possesses super-human vision and or hearing, many things will be unknown to them. Take time to discover these things and explain how your characters learn them.

    10) Ultimate Etiquette and Planning. Once in a while you may find someone to plot, plan and write with, sharing ideas makes for great tales, but be patient. If you anticipate a response from a particular crewmate or character, dropping them an e-mail to give them the Heads Up isn't a bad idea. If you are planning a particular RP, write up a bullet point of the MAJOR events that will occur. Let people choose one event to write, or decide on a signal for the other character to begin. Don't be upset if others come into your storyline unexpectedly as well, encourage them to join. But show them your plan quickly lest they divert your story far from its original course. If you have an idea or a plot twist that involves your own character, strike while the iron is hot! Many times the story will change more quickly than you thought, and opportunities don't always last. Some of the best stories have come by sudden unexpected events. While you may have a definitive close or direction to your storyline...it doesn't mean an end. Remember that you can come back to a buried treasure...or venture again for a special magical ingredient or creature. There are many storylines that need time to be fulfilled, the birth and growth of children...the return of lost family or enemies. Your story doesn't have to be continuous to be exciting or well written.

    --- Once again, all of the above (except for #1) are guidelines. There may indeed be times when it is appropriate to ignore one or more of them, but do so carefully. Also, don't hesitate to E-mail other authors with questions, possible plot twists, etc. etc. Some storylines work best with some rough planning in advance, however vague. Most importantly, have fun and don't be afraid to get involved! Most authors like nothing more than having new writers jump into their storylines and muddy the waters a bit. If you're feeling a bit uncertain, E-mail them and ask beforehand. Odds are you'll get nothing but encouragement.
