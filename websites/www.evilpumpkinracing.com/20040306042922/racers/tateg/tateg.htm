The Little Things

Tah-Tay Goookin #256E

BIR September 12-14, 2003

 

There are lots of little things that have to come together if I’m going to have a good race weekend. Time and money seem to have the largest effect on these little things, so it is these two formidable obstacles that must be overcome. Rather than simply giving in to such obviously solid barriers, I prefer desperately trying to force things to work. (Ever try to throw a potato chip? Ed.)

 

Money

I had no money for another set of race tires for the September races, but I figured my slicks from the previous weekend would be OK for a few more races. Sure, they’re not legal for the Supersport race, but I’m hardly a threat to the top five riders, so who’d bother protesting my finish? Well…uh…my teammates might if I finish ahead of them, but I’ll take my chances. Plus I still have a credit card that’s below the credit limit, so I could just put all the racing fees on that beautiful little piece of plastic. Of course, I lost it just a few hours into my race day, resulting in some mad scrambling and cell phone calls to the bank to cancel the card. Then suddenly there was a blaring, horrible mispronunciation of my name over the PA system (Tah-Tay.....Goookin.....), asking me to report to the front gate. Turns out I’d left my credit card at the gate, where I first used it. Great! Call the bank back, reactivate the card, and the "no money" issue disappears. Poof ! Well, at least for a few weeks…

 

Time

Unavoidable time constraints reduced my racing weekend from the normal Friday through Sunday to just Saturday. I had National Guard obligations on Friday; pure patriotism here folks! (OK, OK – and need for another paycheck). I had family obligations in Philadelphia on Sunday, which meant sitting on an airplane for hours going somewhere I didn’t want to go. So I’d miss practice on Friday and the traditional 5-hour endurance race on Sunday, but I was determined to still get at least one full day of racing in on Saturday. Heck, I could pack the bike and all my stuff into the pick-up on Thursday night after work (which basically means move the entire contents of the garage into the relatively small bed of the pick-up), defend our country on Friday (conduct a covert assault in Afghanistan a nd…er…uh…well…truthfully, upgrade an SQL Server database from version 6.7 to SQL 2000 - try to keep your clothes on, ladies.), drive up to Brainerd on Friday night, race on Saturday, drive back to Minneapolis on Sat urday night, and catch the 5 AM flight for Philadelphia on Sunday morning. It’s a plan! The only big downside would be missing the Saturday evening dinner with the Sage Homes/Jenspeed racing team (hey, they were paying for dinner!). Perhaps I’ll get another opportunity to leech a free meal off of them – no, wait…I got screwed out of that again at our last Jenspeed Training. Right, Commander JenSpeed?

 

Races

With the money and time problems solidly addressed through credit and sleep deprivation, it was time to race. During the Supersport race, I was determined to get a good start. There’s always so much for my busy brain to keep in conscious memory that I rarely get it all right. Such as:

    • shut the helmet visor
    • line up on the starting grid so you don’t rear end the bike in front of you in case his start sucks
    • sit forward to get weight on the front wheel
    • get the left toes under the shift lever so you’re ready to shift
    • creep the clutch just barely to the engagement point
    • rev it up to about 7000 rpm
    • use FULL throttle at the green flag and only modulate the clutch (yes, sometimes I forget to go to full throttle. Sigh.)

But this time, my synapses were firing with full spark, and I was actually able to get a great 1st gear launch at the start. I mean I really got a good launch. I was so distracted with my front-wheel-skimming monster start, I forgot to shift to 2nd gear in time. After a couple of seconds of the rev-limiter stopping any additional acceleration, I realized what was going on, and made the shift. That bunch of bikes I launched past in 1st gear, all passed by me in 2nd gear. I suppose Dr. Dave’s dark cloud of entropy looms over anyone even close to him. (You make your OWN cloud, dude. Ed.)

Now, in my mind, I had to "make up" for my mistake at the start by late braking past a few bikes in turn 3. So I went truly deep into the turn, then grabbed a truckload of brakes. The good news is that I passed 2 or 3 bikes on the brakes. The bad news is that there was at least one other person braking really hard, to the point of locking up his front tire and crashing. He and his bike were now bouncing through a pack of other racers. "Don’t worry, crash-boy is way over on the inside of me and not really in my path. Keep your line," I told myself confidently. Unfortunately, his bike knocked another guy’s bike out from under him, and now crash-boy #2 was skidding on his back RIGHT IN FRONT OF MY FRONT TIRE!

This sort of thing is somewhat distracting when it’s live and just 4 feet away.

I thought I was already braking hard, but the situation proved to me that I could brake even harder. It still wasn’t enough, and I was about to ride right up and over the middle of the skidding, helpless victim and spare him the trouble of ever paying for a proper vasectomy. Just when I was considering if I’d be better off to ride over my prey while I was on the brakes or off the brakes, he saw his own peril and cleverly rolled left and was clear. Whew.

The only other noteworthy item during the Supersport race was my poorly behaving suspension. It felt like the front end was "skipping" on bumps, especially on corner exits, which made the bike want to "tank slap" on me. I could make it better by weighting down the front end while cornering, but that was very difficult and distracting. I finished the race in 9th place, knowing I had to look at the suspension later. Back in the pits, I also noticed that when I spun my front wheel, it seemed like the brake pads were dragging too much on the brake disks. I enlisted Dr. Dave to help me work on this problem and my suspension issues, hoping he might have some good insight into them.

Dr. Dave is kind of good at fixing things.

Sure enough, the Good Doctor™ pointed out that I had too much brake fluid in the reservoir. The fluid level window is misleading, because of the angle the reservoir when mounted on the clip-on handlebars. So I had mistakenly overfilled it with fluid, which didn’t show up as a problem until I put new brake pads on. It was not a big deal to fix (It just involved sucking, which we all know how to do... Ed.), and my brakes were quickly back to normal.

Now, back to the suspension. This is a much trickier task, and I don’t really understand what to do to fix different symptoms (it actually requires some knowledge). After a certain period of beating my head against the ground and hoping to have some miraculous suspension enlightenment, Rich "The Great Pumpkin" Nelson gave me some help. He began measuring static sag, sag with rider, etc. at both the front and the rear of the bike. Apparently it’s really important to get your static sag set up correctly as a starting point for suspension setup. Mine was way off. Too much preload in the rear and not enough in the front. While I was able to adjust my Fox shock at the rear, the forks do not have preload adjusters. Time to buy some aftermarket fork preload adjusters, I suppose. I also could not remember how I setup the adjustments on my cartridge emulators in the forks, but the consensus seemed to be it should be at "3 turns". I could not get that verified, much less changed with the time I had in the pits. Rich also noted that if by "weighting down the front" I could make the bike behave better, I should raise my rear shock ride height in order to put more weight on the front wheel. Wow. That’s logical. I thought that adjuster was for adjusting the shock for really short or tall people. Unfortunately, you basically have to hang the bike from the ceiling to disconnect the shock and then make the adjustment, and again, I just didn’t have time to do that. It seemed pointless to start changing damping adjustments when I couldn’t get the baseline of static sag right on the forks, and when I couldn’t adjust the ride height. I decide to just deal with it as-is. This winter I’m going to have to buy some parts and do some studying about suspension. Time and money again!

During the Superbike and GP races, I was able to post times in the 1:56 arena, and finished somewhere around 10th in the Superbike, and 15th in the GP race. Rich showed me that I have some work to do if I’m going to beat him; he’d draft passed me on the straight and continue to put distance between us on the infield. Sheesh. I got passed on the long Brainerd straight by several people in the mid-pack of the Lightweight class, but generally was able to give them a run for it on the infield. Unfortunately, I’ve been plagued with not being able to use the high-speed draft effectively against others like they do to me. I can only get alongside the person, and never really finish the pass. Some of it may be due to being down on power – several people use cheater cams etc, and several people are on the new SV650 which makes a few more ponies than the 2000 version that I have. But I think it might be a bodywork thing also (since it couldn’t be a lack of talent!). Could the sleek body work really make a difference in aerodynamics versus the naked bike like I race? Regardless, both races amounted to great dicing in the infield part of the track, followed by me getting passed on the straight. The only other variable from lap to lap in the races was whether or not I was able to keep the throttle wide open through turn 2. If I initiated the turn properly, I could keep ‘er pinned; if I was off just a little, I’d have to ease off just enough to tighten the turn (and just enough to get passed by whomever was right behind me) before pinning it again.

It was great fun racing for that one day, but I then quickly had to pack up and leave. Too bad, because I really would have liked to run the endurance race on Sunday. Oh well, maybe next year. Until then, I have my "to do" list:

    • buy and install preload adjusters on the forks (money and time)
    • adjust the static sag on the forks (time)
    • set the cartridge emulator damping nut properly (time)
    • adjust the ride height on the rear shock (time)
    • buy bodywork (money)
    • paint bodywork (money)
    • install bodywork (time)
    • increase power to get on par with new SVs (money)

I see a pattern here. It seems painfully obvious that I’ll continue to need more money and time if I have any chance of getting ahead of all the little things. I’ll try again, but my benevolent boss keeps sticking to his core positions, which are:

  • Need does not justify raises
  • You can try talking to HR about more vacation time - Good luck

Look at them, Smithers. Goldbrickers.... Layabouts.... Slug-a-beds! Little do they realize their days of suckling at my teat are numbered…

But don’t fret, ladies and gentlemen. I just heard that "someone from Minnesota" won the Powerball lottery, and I have a ticket in my wallet. Hmmm…I wouldn’t have to work, so the "no time" issue gets solved. I’d have plenty of resources to address this "no money" issue. It’d be perfect! But there is the threat of Dr. Dave’s universal equilibrium. If his countless years of higher education (like Bluto in Animal House?) give his theories any merit, I’d then develop severe arthritis or some hard to pronounce and incurable ailment that would not allow me to race, thus offsetting the lottery success and maintaining nature’s order. Or maybe he’s just full of shit – you make the call.

HEY – NO CRASHES?

Tate Guckin #256E

BIR July 18-20, 2003

ROAD TRIP! Dr. Dave and I decided we’d leave for the Friday practice really early on Friday morning, thus saving a little money in hotel costs for the weekend. So, at 5:30am we were in my driveway loading bikes, gear, and our sleepy asses in to the truck. Sheesh – there’s just way too much stuff to bring up to the races. It’s almost to the point that the two of us and our race gear cannot fit in one pickup truck and trailer. We have:

  • 2 large coolers, stocked solid with beer and munchables
  • 2 tool boxes of near-infinite mass
  • 2 sets of leathers/helmets/racing undies, etc.
  • Spare parts for 2 SV 650s
  • Spare parts for 1 GS 500
  • Front and rear stands
  • Assorted partygoods, etc, etc.

Not to mention the three race bikes and one pit bike! But, after some creative packing, we’re ready to roll.

Just as we finished loading, Dave, being the conscientious truck owner he is, decided the check the truck’s engine oil before our journey. Good thinking. Next thing I know, I’m watching the Good Doctor repeatedly slamming the hood of the truck. Seemed odd. Then again, after enough road trips with Dave you kind of get immune to odd behavior (ask him why he was yelling at the pavement at Deal’s Gap, NC sometime). But when I realized this odd behavior was the only thing stopping us from leaving, I had to ask. "What the hell are you doing?" As it turns out, a spring in the truck’s hood latch had broken, rather than one in Dave’s head like I had assumed.

Broken spring, hood latch, and Ford part number for the F-150 Hood Latch Assembly. (Goddam ungrateful POS! ed.)

So…unpack the truck, get the toolboxes out, disassemble the front of the truck, and remove the bad part. Now what? It’s 6:30 am, and the local Ford dealer doesn’t open until 7:00. And what are the odds that they will even have that spring? But, we went there on the hope that some motivated parts guy would show up just a little early to work, and we got lucky. "Thanks Parts Guy at Midway Ford!" It turns out you get to buy the whole assembly, not just the spring (hey – it’s not my money, so I didn’t much care). Just a little more work installing the new part, repack the truck, and we were on the road by 7:30. Good thing that all worked out, because we probably would have just skipped the race weekend rather attempt to unpack the truck and repack it all in mine. Anyway, I sure hope Dave learned his lesson about buying trucks from shady characters…(i.e., People named Tate ed.)

So, perhaps it doesn’t really save much to go up to the races really early the morning races start, rather than going up the night before. However, I always feel like I’m in the Twilight Zone when I’m at hotels in the Brainerd Area. They seem even weirder than the hotels in Arkansas (really!). For example:

Hell, I turned left – cooler, suitcase, and Dave in tow.

And then there’s the hour or so of unwinding in front of the TV with a drink (or two) before going to sleep. Whew – at least they do have remotes for the TVs. Wait. What the …

Note the PC (previous channel) button. How useful is this when you can only go up or down one channel at a time? Think about it…

Lightweight Supersport

So, logistics aside, it’s time to race! The Lightweight Supersport race started out great. Dr. Dave and Billy and I were in a tight pack, and we were ready to show each other who the real man is. Dave has always been very aggressive on the brakes in turns 3 and 9. However, he has planted his face into the dirt in turn 9 enough that he doesn’t often play that card any more, and I know that his beat-up shoulder cannot handle HUGE braking in turn 3. So, my plan was simple (insert ‘Dr Evil’ type laugh here). After all, none of us are actually going to win anything; it’s just the fun of dicing with each other that’s the whole attraction of racing. So, just a couple laps into the race, I stuff on the inside of Dr Dave fairly hard on the brakes in turn 3, and … uh … hmmm … watch him drive straight off the track. "Don’t watch him! Don’t watch him! Don’t watch him!" I was able to force my focus from his problems and actually make the turn, although it was kind of disappointing to kno w that he was not going to be there to play with. That’s OK; I still have Billy to race with. But … well … gee … sure looks like Billy and his bike racked up against the hay bales in turn 8. Sigh. Once again, I was too far behind the front pack, and too far ahead of the trailing pack to really have much fun.

 

Lightweight Superbike

Time for new tires. I can always count on Mason Racing Tires to have fresh Michelin Pilots ready to mount at the CRA races. It’s very convenient. But, since this weekend was the first time I didn’t actually call him to verify tire availability, Murphy took the opportunity to punish me for taking anything for granted. What’s that? Mr. Mason says all he has is slicks? Mongo never tried slicks. Mount those bad dogs up!! After one practice session on the slicks, it felt like they were gripping waaaaay beyond what DOT can. I had a decent start in the Superbike race, and was grinning ear to ear with the newly found traction I had with the slicks in the infield. Not sure what malady Dr. Dave and Billy had, since I never saw them that race, but I sure had fun with #146. Still don’t know who he is, but it was the same thing lap after lap after lap. He’d draft past me on the straight, and I couldn’t get him back. I tried like hell, but could never get the draft slingshot to pull me completely past him. However, I did have a great deal of success late braking him in turn three. So that’s how it went the entire race. About half way through the race, there was some excitement between turns four and five. Apparently, someone had a crash of good enough quality to separate the gas tank from the bike, and this tank was just laying there in the track right on the racing line late in the exit of turn 4. It definitely tested the seal of my backside O-ring. Even after the tank was removed from the track, I could not get comfortable going full speed through there since there still was a puddle of gasoline (that’s my excuse, and I’m sticking to it!)

 

Lightweight Gran Prix

The GP race was really another iteration of the Superbike race. Early in the race, I was hanging with some of the faster guys, but then my "friend" on #146 showed up. Somehow he and I were tangled with each other too much, resulting in the two of us losing the fast pack. This time, I was very determined to return the favor of the high speed drafting of him somewhere in the high-speed part of the track. A couple of times I was almost able to get side-by-side with him as we leaned in to turn 1, but at that speed I kind of wimp out unless I am SURE I have the pass. There sure is a touch needed on the throttle to back off just a little – I kept backing off too much while conceding the line to him, and then he’d suddenly be 10 bike lengths ahead of me. *&^%$#@! After several laps of this, just before turn 1, I was in that same familiar position of almost having the pass. I wondered if lifting my derriere off the seat a little would help the aerodynamics and give me just 1 or 2 m ore miles per hour. No. That resulted in a wicked tank slapper at full speed (you’ll be glad to hear the trusty O-ring still held back leakage!). But by the time I got control back, he was a long ways ahead of me. I was able to catch him on the infield and then continue with the same trading of position between him drafting me on the straight and then me late braking him in turn 3 for the rest of that race.

Time to Upgrade

BIR, June 20-22, 2003

It was time to upgrade the race bike platform from a GS500 to an SV650, and I had about 6 weeks before the next race weekend. No problem. I once learned a semester’s worth of Abstract Algebra in 4 days (well…learned it well enough for a C, anyway), so how hard can this be? It’s not like I’m trying to figure out women or something…

So the order was sent to Paul Jensen of Jenspeed Racing (www.jenspeed.com) and I waited for the parts to arrive. I learned a lot about the world of aftermarket parts. ALMOST NOTHING FITS OR WORKS RIGHT!!

  1. Forks: The settings for the preload are determined by how large a spacer you cut from a piece of pipe, and the damping is set by a small screw inside the forks. So set it now, and then disassemble the forks and cut more pipe bits to make adjustments?!? I was saved this frustration by Brad Frey from Two Bee’s Tuning since he called me with the settings that he’s found to work best.
  2. Shock: Why the heck would you make an aftermarket shock for an SV that isn’t threaded for the same lower mounting bolt? Given my anorexic supply of nuts and bolts, I now have 1 English bolt, and the rest of the bike is metric on the SV.
  3. Digital Tach: Since the stock tachometer is also part of the stock speedo, I needed a tach and opted for the digital one. Sure would have saved a lot of iterations of installation attempts if the Japanese translator for the installation instructions wouldn’t have skipped the day when his English class talked about "positive" vs. "negative".
  4. Shift light: Worked perfectly. Of course, I just spliced the tach wire to connect to the light, so I never checked the instructions…
  5. Braided Brake Lines: You’d think someone would have checked the thread pitch of the brake line bolts on the calipers to make sure they’re right, but nnnnooooooo. Also, if you’re ever race prepping an SV, be sure to give some thought into whether you order parts for an SV or an SV-S, since once you put clip-ons on your SV, the length of brake line needed is more like that of an SV-S. I now have brake line poking out from behind the sides of the number plate, like arm pit hair on a French woman poking out from behind the edges of her tank top.
  6. Rearset Foot pegs: There’s no way anyone tried mounting these at the factory where they’re made, because the shift shaft drags on the frame. But, with a little help from the handy-dandy Dremel I was able to make a properly sized spacer. On the other side, I just had to accept the face that there was no way to mount the return spring on the brake pedal. I … uh … guess I don’t need it?
  7. Frame Sliders: Ok, Goober – don’t send instructions for which of the sliders mounts to which engine mounting bolt or anything (yes, the 2 sliders are different sizes, and there are different engine mounting bolts they could mount to).
  8. Number Plates: In my attempt to control cost, I opted to not buy full bodywork, and just mount number plates. So about 5 days before racing, I began looking. Guess what? Nobody carries them in stock, and Lockhart-Phillips "discontinued that part number". I finally found some on the web for way to much money.
  9. Temp Gauge: Worked perfectly.
  10. Bellypan: Good luck finding a bellypan that can contain fluid. I found one that at least could be modified to contain fluid, but then it turns out that the mounting bracket doesn’t fit with an M4 exhaust. Dr Dave Johnson, his grinder, and countless hours of bending metal finally resolved the mounting problem.
  11. Exhaust: First, I removed the old one, which was way more difficult than I expected. Once that was done, I opened the box for the new one, and the directions said "Do not remove the stock rear cylinder header pipe". Ooops…too late. Glad I wasted that effort.

Anyway, I need to thank Dr Dave, Dr Dave’s grinder, Clint Lee, Paul Jensen, Brad Frey, and my credit card for their help with the SV project. I never would have made it in time without their help.

Now it’s OFF TO THE RACES!

Friday’s practice was great fun! The SV runs about 20mph faster on the top end than the GS did, so it took me quite a while before I was keeping any real speed through turn 2. But after a while I was having great fun dicing with teammate Billy Hewitt. I was enjoying the good brakes on the SV, and had fun stuffing Billy in turn 3 a few times (comes back to haunt me later). Ok, I’m ready for the races.

During the Lightweight Supersport race, I had a wonderful launch at the start, and was running closely behind the "fast guys" through the first lap. "Wow, this is great". But then I hit a false neutral during my downshift in turn 10. Since I tend to shift poorly even on the street, I knew that soon after the false neutral, there was a good chance that the transmission would find the gear on its own with a big "clack!" soon, so I held in the clutch to keep that from messing with my rear wheel during the turn. Problem was, now I can’t raise my left hand to warn the other riders that I was now traveling at a really sucky speed. So I just tried to hold a predictable line and went way to the outside of turn 10, and once the mob had passed me I searched through the gearbox until I found one that would work. I could see Billy and Dr Dave way ahead, and I knew what my mission was. With just a couple laps to go, I had the pleasure of surprising Dr Dave by passing him. "Gue ss who’s back, Dave?" Once again, I was having great fun racing with someone. Then, we got passed by a couple of really fast Novice riders (bastards!), so I tried to pick it up a notch so they wouldn’t get away from me. Coming into turn 9, before we were even braking, they passed an Orange Shirt on the right and the good news is I saw some room on the left of him where I could also pass. Bad news is that the Orange Shirt moved left as those ahead of me passed him on the right, perhaps out of some misplaced effort to be safe. My passing lane was now shifted solidly into the grass. Traveling at race speed on the grass is tough business. I had the presence of mind to not grab front brake in the grass (been there before, don’t want to do that again), so I was working the back brake. Each time it felt like it was sliding to the side, I’d ease up and wait for the bike to straighten out. However, I did not have the presence of mind to remember to pull the clutch in, so the motor stalled, and I had no w ay to get out of the back tire slide I was in. Hello, HI SIDE! The bike vaulted me into the air, and I landed on my right shoulder and head. I tumbled forever, and then got up and sat behind some trees wondering what that ringing sound was.

The SV took the crash really well, all things considered. The frame sliders worked wonderfully, but the tank took a hell of a hit. It was kind of depressing to see how wrinkled, scratched, broken, and dirty the once pretty machine had become. But I had the same experience sometime in my early 30’s looking in the mirror after a shower, so this was certainly not something that was going to be too depressing since it can be fixed.

So, with a new helmet in hand, I was ready for the Superbike and GP races on Sunday. In the Lightweight Superbike race, I had some weird type of petit mal seizure or spasm or something while waiting for the green flag, and unconsciously jumped the start. I stopped within just a few feet, but then the flag went and I wasn’t ready yet to go again. So the entire pack took off without me. Most of that race I was just racing myself. Not much fun. But with all that time by myself out there I realized that I needed to make a gearing change. The 44 tooth sprocket was too tall, so I made a mental note to up that to a 45 for the next weekend.

During the Lightweight GP race, I was stalked incessantly by Billy Hewitt. Although I know you shouldn’t do it, I kept looking behind me to see if I was getting away from him. No. As the last lap started, I was wondering where he was going to make his move. He obviously had plenty of time to figure out where he is faster than I am, and would make his move there. It just sucks when you know it’s coming. Sure enough, just before turn 2, he drafts past me and I couldn’t get back in his draft and return the favor. "But I’ve been able to out-brake him coming in to turn 3 every time", I though to myself (insert evil grin here). Unfortunately, Billy’s a quick learner and it was not to be. He blocked the inside line, and was plenty good on the brakes. He did the same thing through turns 4 – 8. Often when you are protecting against the pass really aggressively, you aren’t really going through turns as fast as you would otherwise. So when I saw he was protecting the inside pass again in turn 9, I took a new strategy – corner exit speed. I got on the brakes relatively early, and set up for an early apex and early acceleration (the whole time really really hoping there was no one waiting to pass me, since it would have been easy to take the inside line on me with this approach to the turn). Hey! It worked! I was able to carry much more speed exiting the turn and I passed him. In a rare display of calmness, I managed not to get too psyched out in turn 10 and keep my position ahead of Billy. I’m the man! I OWN … 8TH place. Or … 10th … or … 12th … whatever it was. Ok, so I’m really not "the man". Nonetheless, it was some of the most fun I’ve had racing.

Lessons Learned:

  1. New riders wear orange shirts for a reason. As Dr Dave put it, "I wouldn’t have tried that outside pass on an Orange Shirt. You can’t count on them to behave reasonably." But truthfully, the fault lies with me for picking the passing line that I did – It’s the passing rider’s obligation to safely make the pass, and I didn’t.
  2. Novice? Expert? It doesn’t matter. There are some very fast novice riders (my hat’s off to you!), and there are a few slower expert riders.
  3. Fellow Evil Pumpkin Rich Nelson (aka, the Great Pumpkin) is busy prepping his new SV for racing. Once he joins Billy and me, and once Dr Dave has heeled a little more, there’s going to be some great racing (at the back of the back?) between us.
  4. As our benevolent sponsor, Eric of Sage Homes (www.mysagehome.com), stated in a note left on my banged up SV tank, "Fans love a good crash". Glad I was able to add to your enjoyment of the weekend.
  5. Apparently there were some pictures taken of me while I was taking a nap. No, really! I was asleep! So if you see these pictures defaming me, know that I was asleep and Photoshop was most likely involved.

Well, I’m off to see the bodywork guy to see what he can do with my tank and tail section. We’ll talk again after the July races.

I really enjoy riding motorcycles

BIR, May 2-5, 2003

A person spends an awful lot of his time doing things out of obligation, but a motorcycle ride is solely for you. It doesn’t matter if it’s trail riding, motocross riding, street riding, or roadracing – it’s always just you and your machine having fun with your buddies. But life has an incessant and wicked sense of humor, which requires an all-too-often HUGE dose of cycle maintenance, which I really do not enjoy at all

It was my turn for that misery while I was putting my engine together just before the May 2003 CRA races at Brainerd. After several trips to the machine shop (long story, don’t get me started), the GS500 engine was ready for assembly. Teammate Clint Lee was very gracious in his services in assisting me in assembling the motor in his basement, and explicitly warning me to "be sure an cover the openings before trying to put the pistons on, otherwise you’ll drop the lock ring into the hole." OK, so noted. At least for the first piston. I forgot on the second piston, and dropped the cursed ring into the great abyss. I actually did a good job of not introducing his children to a level of foul language that one only really embraces after a military tour. After an eternity with an extend-o-magnet, I was able to fish it out of the bottom end of the engine, and was referred to as a "lucky shit." Funny – didn’t really feel lucky. Fine. Get over it. Then two of the eight cam cap bolts also decided to strip the threads. A week later, I guess I can see how Clint thought this was so funny.

Now, the bike is running and IT’S OFF TO THE RACES! Dr Dave accompanied me to the races, although he still has an injured flipper so he would just be practicing and not entering any races. So we left really early on Friday morning to get up for a day of practice. Since I didn’t race during the first race of the year, it really took me an entire day of practice before I was able to ignore all of the voices in my head and really ride like a person should ride on a race track.

After a day of practice, it was time to relax over a beer or two. But, again, it’s never that simple. I met a lady, who I will call Ducati (748cc set of jugs) who was justly proud of her new set of … uh … rebuilt twins. I handled myself very properly . While the rebuild on the set was a tribute to some physician’s countless hours of study, it was nonetheless awkward to be evaluating them in the presence of her husband. But hey, in the name of medicine, I obliged.

During Saturday’s races, I learned that teammate Paul Jensen really knows how to ride the hell out of a bike – pissed me off getting stomped by a relative newby to roadracing, but he’s a good guy so I’ll forgive him. I also learned that I don’t want to keep racing a GS500. Too much maintenance, and I’m just tired of dealing with trying to make a bike that was never meant to be raced behave properly on the racetrack (OK, and Paul doesn’t have one yet so I won’t have to get beat by him if I have one). So, stay tuned while I try to convince Chase that I deserve a higher credit limit to buy an SV650.

Racing Lessons Learned in May:

  1. Just because no one is closely behind you at lap 3, if you relax the pace you will definitely give up 2nd place by lap 7.
  2. If you feel an odd tugging on your arm at 100+ mph, don’t panic – it’s just Bruce McGee pulling your arm for fun.
  3. The stock GS500 rear shock sucks while racing. Paul Jensen referred to my bike as the "pogo stick" after watched my bike wallow around in turn three during the 17-lap trophy dash race – it was scary
.

Well, I’m off to find an SV650 – hell, the kids can find their own way to pay for college … and food … an clothes …

June 23-25th, 2000

Although it was the second race weekend for the CRA, it was my first racing of the year since I had scheduling conflicts with the first CRA weekend. There's a first time for everything... (June 25th - 27th)

This is my first contribution to the evil pumpkin site, although Dave Johnson (aka Dr. Dave, aka Super Dave) and I have been hanging around the pumpkin patch for about a year now trying to pick up tidbits of wisdom on this whole racing thing. I missed the first race weekend, so I was really fired up for the second weekend. I was going to try out the advise of Reg Pridmore who, the week before at the CLASS cycle school, had talked so matter-of-factly about trail braking well into the start of a turn. It made me realize that I had been braking quite early going into turns (that explains why Dr Dave passed me each time coming up to turn 3). After a week's worth of experimenting with that on my VFR during the daily commute at the cloverleafs of hwy 36 and Snelling, I was ready to give it a try on the track.

Practice sessions Friday and Saturday morning were not very helpful for "practicing" this since there were always a herd of bikes with terribly different speeds at every turn (practice sessions definitely need to be broken up better between different types of bikes). But I was ready for the Ultralight Supersport Sprint race to let 'er rip. Unfortunately, the clouds that were threatening rain all day let loose around lap 2 of the race. I was right on the Dr's rear tire when someone else stuffed inside of me on turn 10. Too bad for him, he low sided right next to me. Being the humanitarian that I am, I chose to avoid running over him and his bike and ran off the track. By the time I got back on track I was left in the dust by the leading group. 6th place finish for that race. Damned rain - still no REAL good experimenting with this late braking thing.

The track had dried up pretty well by the time I was at the starting line for the Trophy Dash. I was feeling pretty good during the race until, you guessed it, the rain started again. I did enjoy the front row seat to another low side in turn 4 by the guy directly front on me, but still there's no getting around the fact that I don't like racing in the rain. I was just grumbling to myself about that, when the race was red flagged. Apparently there was a getting to be quite a pile of crashed bikes on turn 10, so they ended the race early. Somehow, I was 1st in my class when it ended. WooooHoooo! Still, I had not really had a chance to really feel like I had good track time.

Sunday's Ultralight Superbike race found cooler but dry weather. After a typically poor start for me (hate it when I miss 2nd gear!) I was towards the back of the pack heading into turn 1. It took me a couple of laps to get past the slower bikes, and then just focused on passing the bikes I came up on one at a time. The late trail braking thing works GREAT!!! No one ever passed me by out late braking me, and I got passed several people by doing it to them. Thanks Reg! I was reeling in the last guy in front of me, when I noticed the white flag. Only one lap left to get past this guy. I didn't catch up to him until turn 7, and couldn't pass him in 7 or 8. Coming up to turn 9 I decided to only go down 1 gear rather than 2 and try to take turn 9 very fast so I could have high exit speed and pass him down the following straight. Don't try this at home, boys and girls. I was able to get the bike back on the track from the grass with both tires still underneath me, but just barely. OK, that was scarey...nothing wrong accepting 2nd place.

Finally, the Ultalight GP race. I felt decent through that race, but I also had it in the back of my head that it sure would be nice to go home from the weekend with me and my bike in tact. So I wasn't overly aggressive that race. Had loads of fun, no near crashes and finished 4th. Almost had 3rd, but it took a serious late brake in turn 10 to get in front of the guy, and then I was going to hard into the turn to get on the gas until very late. He just passed me right back exiting the turn. Oh well, I had to try it.

Overall, a very good weekend for me, specially when compared with Dr Dave's mechanical woes, Brian's crash, etc. I still haven't seen lap times for all the races, but I did see my best lap during the Ultralight Superbike was a 2:05.6 which is a personal best. It was over 3 seconds faster than the best lap by the guy who won the race, which just goes to prove how poor my starts are. My GS500 is still bone stock except for upgraded fork springs, so I was frustrated by people leaving me on the straight after I'd worked so hard through the infield to make up time on them. I'm going to have to get more power out of that bike if I'm going to have any luck fending of Dr Dave and the bike that will be born from mating his two GS500's.

Sunday evening muster for the pumpkin GS500's showed Dave with 2 broken bikes (and a beat up checkbook), Brain with a scraped up and oil leaking bike, Mark feeling exhausted after only his second race weekend ever, to the point of not running the last ultralight race, and me feeling good about my first race weekend of the year (yeah...that probably means my turn is coming for a very troubled weekend).
