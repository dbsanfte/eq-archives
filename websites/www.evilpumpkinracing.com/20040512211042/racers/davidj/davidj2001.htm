This weeks Theme: Collision!

Fortune smiles upon the hapless motodweeb sometimes, and this was my week. Weather was a perfect 70 to 80 F at BIR, and I had two bikes (the GS 500 and SV 650) that required only gas and throttle twisting for the whole weekend. This is in stark contrast to my other outings (e.g. see "Tales of Fire and Metal Shavings"). This was also my first outing since my promotion to "Expert". My impression is that there should be three classes: "Novice", "Expert" and "Guru of Whoop-Ass". There are some really fast guys out there.

Until this last weekend, I had not really bashed into anything on the racetrack. (Well OK, the GROUND). It just seemed like a something to be scrupulously avoided. The thing is, these "Experts" don’t leave you any room to horn in and get around them. That means you’re stuck following the same bike around all day or making a passing space where there is just a splinter of one. There is only one way to practice this: install the brass cojones and start poking a tire into every unoccupied inch of track. The first thing I found is some people will give you the line if you are inside and nearly even with them, others just try to bash you out of the way like sumo wrestlers. There seems to be a correlation between the second type and white leathers, but that’s probably just coincidence.

 

Episode One: The Unicycle

I was growing impatient following one particular guy on my SV. I went for the pass on the inside of turn 7, which is a left followed by a quick right for turn eight. I was most of the way past him at the apex, but when I leaned it in for turn 8, I felt this odd nudge/scrape then suddenly a big bump and the back end of the bike launched straight up! It seemed like I was up there a while, because I had time to realize that it was going to be hard to make turn 8 with just the front tire and the ground. The only idea I had was to turn the bike like a leaned-over unicycle (I didn’t say it was a GOOD idea) and hope that gravity was kind. When the rear finally dropped, I got a violent shudder that almost flung me off, then the bike shot around the corner. I just grinned that "stupid but lucky" grin inside my helmet. It was fortunate that Pumpkin Lee and I had just installed an MB Motorsports steering damper, because that was the perfect recipe for a deadly tank slapper. When I got back to the pits, there was a big white smudge on my pipe where it had burned the hide off that wayward bovine. Don’t rightly know whose he was, but he’s got my brand on ‘im now.

 

Episode Two: The Assing

This time I was on the GS, trying to pass another GS on the outside of turn 6. This particular racer (let’s call him "The Oaf") bumped into me mid-corner and moved my line out to the edge. This I can understand, because I wasn’t really where he would look for or expect another bike. But then he looks over at me and just starts moving his line all the way out to the rumble strip. I am about even with his rear axel, and all I can see is this giant, pulpy white ass moving towards me. Up I go onto the rumble strip, and before I can decide what to do, the front tire hits a notch in the concrete and goes hysterical. The bike does a violent head shake and, of its own free will, gives the other GS a bash in the tail section. That bought me the room I needed to live, and as the marshmallow man was taking stock of his undershorts, I slipped by and left the scene.

 

Episode Three: The Impossible

I was once again on the GS, this time trying something I had been getting away with in the Novice class. The ol’ outbrake ‘em in turn 9 ploy. This is a very tight turn that most people slow way down for. I was in the middle of the classic out-brake procedure, when my intended victim looked over and decided to let off the brakes and keep things interesting. The result was we both went into the corner too fast, together, and me with a crappy inside line. I am not sure what happened, because the laws of physics seemed to be in a state of flux during the incident. Witnesses say I slid the rear tire, collided with the other bike now half a length behind me, bounced somehow off the rumble strip and ended up with the front tire a couple of feet in the air. When the tire came down, the front and rear couldn’t agree on which way to go and decided that a good tankslap was in order. Somehow, the careening bike avoided the bridge, the dirt, and the other bike, even though all I was doing was flailing at the bars in a confused manner. The guy I had passed went by me and I never saw him again. I think he was afraid of me.

What can be learned from all this? For one thing, you can smash into all kinds of things and still keep the bike upright. I was able (lucky?) to get out of some situations a better racer would avoid, and a sane, street-going person would never see. I managed to do this without much previous practice at bashing into stuff. I can only conclude that the bikes are much more stable than the average person believes, and are not inclined to fall down over minor mishaps like 80 mph tank slappers and riding on one wheel. Even with this new knowledge, I will endeavor to ensure that my next report’s theme will NOT be "Collision".

One Half-Baked, One Extra Crispy

July 13-15, 2001

For some reason, the weather gods are smiling on us this year. The God of Thunder handed down another perfect racing environment, and the whole gourd-oriented team was also favored by whichever deity is in charge of gravity (the mighty Groundaar?)

Practice went without any problems, except for my difficulty remembering which bike I was coming into turn 3 on. The SV is actually lighter than the GS 500, with twice as many brake rotors. When I forget what I'm riding, I end up nearly stopped at the #3 brake marker on the SV, or nearly in the weeds on the GS with a death grip on the seat cushion (a suction phenomenon).

Racing was another matter in terms of fortune, or in this case, the lack of it. In my first race on the GS 500, I was running the race of my life. I was riding valiantly and executing brilliant (for me) maneuvers to stay up with the lead group. During the 6th lap, I outbraked and passed 2 bikes at once (I was also chewing gum at the same time) and moved up to second. I had scarcely stopped enjoying that moment when the motor blew as I was exiting turn 9. It made a long, mournful poo-poo cushion sound, followed by the dreaded "three spoons in a blender" noise. The entropy god (Scramblor) had his way in my engine. I was fie upon't. I almost got rear-ended by both of my nemeses as I sputtered to a stop near turn 10. This was particularly disappointing because all the races aboard the SV were pitiful. There is still some problem with that engine (yeah, yeah, I thought I fixed it last week) because the thing has no power in the top 3 gears. As if to prove the point, I had one race where I was the only one in the front row of the grid (a bizarre experience in itself), so I got to drag race all of my competitors with a bit of a head start. I got a great launch in front of a field of lightly modified SV 650s and FZR 400s. By the time I got to turn one (1/2 mile down the front straight), I had 18 bikes in front of me. There was even one highly modified GS 500 that could pass me down the front straight. Once I realized I had no hope of making any infield pass stick down the front straight, I became less willing to insert the bike into every little opening. The results speak for themselves. I think I got a fifteenth in one race, just behind the ambulances and pit bikes. This just isn't right, so I have got to figure out what's up inside this flaccid engine before the announcer starts calling me "Backmarker Dave".

Summary

  1. Switching between two bikes confuses that part of the brain (the raceybellum) that runs things like brakes and shifting on autopilot. I have to spend more time thinking about what I am doing rather than just doing it, leaving less brainage available for plotting and scheming. This has the effect of making me slower on both bikes. "I have solved this problem for now" he said, pointing to the smoldering ruins of the once proud racebike. I don't want this to be the final solution.
  2. There is clearly some problem with the SV engine that must be tackled. Running at a huge power handicap with a giant orange slavering pumpkin sewn on my back makes me sure of two things: Everyone thinks I am even slower than I really am, and everyone is absolutely certain who it is they think is slow. I might be less conspicuous (and maybe faster) in a clown suit riding one of those really small tricycles. It would be easier to tear that bird-dogging pumpkin off my back than to sleuth out this elusive motor problem, but I've had enough practice being passed on the front straight. Leonard….fetch me my fixin' wrenches!

That which doesn't kill us...still bites

Heartland Park Raceway

August 18-19, 2001

Some race weeks are close to perfect, some are fraught with miscalculation, exothermic destruction and gravitational anomalies. This week was type "B". It started off innocently enough with our arrival at Heartland Park Raceway. Since none of us have ever been there before, we didn’t know what to expect, or where the "cool" guys set up their canopies. We figured the PA system might be like the one at Brainerd (ppsssshhh …crackle…..third mall of trid for underblight mumblrspike….pssshh"), so we set up in a spot near the track entrance, cleverly thinking we could watch the gate to see when races were being called. The mistake was, we set up 100 yards away from the electrical outlets.

Thus:

A) We had no electricity

B) Everyone around us brought their *#%@! gas generators, which NEVER STOPPED.

Imagine sitting in a lawn chair trying to converse, rest or even think, surrounded by half a dozen running lawnmowers. It’s like one of those hostage situations, where the police cut your power and blast crappy music at you until you go insane and surrender.

As it turns out, the PA, the restrooms and buildings, and all the facilities were much better than those at Brainerd. Even the ubiquitous racetrack bratwurst were beyond reproach. Everything at this track was wonderful, except for, well, the track. To say that it was bumpy would be something of an understatement. I think they must have paved over grapefruit-sized stones, medium-sized ravines, and slow moving animals when they made this place. There must have also been some major earthquakes in that area, judging by the cracks in the pavement. After the quake, the crack problem was "solved" (for the car racers) with a bevy of crack-filling tar guns, wielded by Larry, Moe and Captain Hazelwood. The net result of all this is a very technical track that is rough AND slippery. I understand that some people thrive on this sort of surface, just as some folks like to race in the rain or get spanked by people in black leather underwear. I have now raced on bumps and in rain, and I really can’t say I have adapted well to either. Nevermind about the spanking. I suppose it was one of those "growth experiences" that will probably sink later and make me a better rider, possibly when I’m 90.

My first encounter with the track was pure blundering. With practice, I learned to blunder faster, but I never really felt at home out there on either the GS 500 or the SV 650. The GS was set up too stiff for the "military proving grounds" type of riding I was doing. The front was skipping around on the bumps in the apexes of corners, and the rear was sliding out on the exits. I tried to manage all the invisible land mines, and started to feel OK in practice. As soon as the first race started, it all went out the window. In about lap 7, I lost the front in turn 5 (which we had previously named "Hell Corner"). This corner had a large deteriorating concrete patch, rather like a prostrate tombstone, right in the apex of the corner. It had my name on it. During one of my frantic flailings through there, I missed my line and ended up leaned over on that stony banana peel, and immediately low-sided into the weeds. It was an easy crash, with the only casualty being the flimsy Raask shift lever. I put another one on in time to blow the engine up in the next practice. ARRRRGH! I was not the only one who had troubles at Heartland. Pumpkin Jim crashed twice on a nest of tar snakes in turn 8.

The SV 650 felt really strange on this course. At Brainerd, it’s usually the back that slides out, if anything. At Heartland, the front pushed all over the track, and washed out completely a couple of times before miraculously coming back. It was like pushing a shopping cart at the beach. This was too freaky for me, and I didn’t know what to do about it. The crash on the GS made me paranoid about all the camouflaged instant low-side bombs lurking out there. To make matters worse, I was suffering the after-effects of the Chimichanga with E. coli sauce I ate the night before. I just resigned myself to riding around quietly, harassing no one, trying not to stir up too much gravity or gastrointestinal distress. I managed to bring it home in one piece, and got some not-too-crappy finishes (no pun intended).

 

Summary:

  1. At the next unknown track event we WILL set up near the electrical outlets, or in some toxic waste dump that no one else wants to be near so we can have some peace and quiet. Listening to that non-stop generator racket is like having burs in your underwear all weekend.
  2. Eating at dubious Mexican restaurants means having to worry about your underwear all weekend.
  3. Heartland Park is bumpy and slippery. It requires a very different set-up than Brainerd, or possibly a different mammal at the controls.
  4. What with all the crashes, blow-ups and paranoia, I really didn’t "enjoy" so much as "survive" the experience. It makes for good a calibration standard to help me appreciate those more perfect weekends.
