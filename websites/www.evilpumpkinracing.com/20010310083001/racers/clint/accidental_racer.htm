The Accidental Racer

 Setting the Hook

 The beginnings of this race weekend really began last winter.  At that time, my friends Dave (CRA #242) and Tate (CRA #256) had been racing GS500s in the CRA Ultralight SuperSport class for two years, while I watched, with maybe a bit of envy.  But I didn't have the time for it.  And it seemed that going racing left about the same size crater in your-life-as-you-know-it as becoming a drug addict.

 But late last winter, Tate said he had to miss the year's first race weekend, which is in May.  He was stuck with National Guard duty.  Hmmm.  Without really meaning it, I brurted that I ought to ride his race bike that weekend.  My thinking, what there was of it, went, "I'll just buy him a set of race tires, ride the thing around a bit, have a bunch of fun, what's to lose?  And besides, he'll never go for it anyway."

 Oops.  He did go for it, and soon after I started getting an inkling of what it really costs to get your carcass out on a racetrack.  First, I had to get signed up with the CRA and attend the new rider class.  That's a couple hundred.  I need some leathers, $425.  Racing Boots, $250.  Back protector, used, $60.  My street riding gloves won't do on the track,  $85.  Uhoh, my trusty X-9 will be more than five years old as of the first race weekend of the season, no good for racing, $150.  Tires for Tate's bike, $300.  Practice, race fees, food, lodging, gas for the race weekend, $500.

 Finally the weekend arrives, and the two big lessons learned on the track are:  (1) This is really Fun, and (2) I really suck at it.  Competent and maybe even fast on the street means nothing at all on the track.  Reading and understanding the lessons of Twist of the Wrist is nice, but you can't really put a lot of it in practice on the street.  While out sport riding, you can't go around picking nice late turn-in points, then snapping the bike over to full lean.  Not when there could be a dead raccoon, or a live deer, or an F150 right on that line you picked.  Well anyway, I knew wanted more of this Fun and less of this Sucking At It, so I had to get a race bike of my own.

 I quickly located and bought a GS500 which had been parked for two years but was "Race Ready".  Not pretty and it didn't run, but it had lots of good race components and spare parts.  I figured it would need a carb job, a battery, maybe some tires.  Well, I now know that the phrase "race ready" deserves its own wing in the pantheon of great lies.  After doing the carb job, I found that the battery was still good, but the motor spins over but won't start.  What's the deal?  There's spark, there's gas, oh - there's 75 lbs. of compression.

 I pried off the top end and brought it to Bill Bune (www.billbune.com) for a rebuild.  It ended up needing rings, valve guides, and intake valves.  When that was done and assembled, my inaugural ride up and down the street in front of my house revealed the rear end seemed COMPLETELY RIGID, and also made some horrible clattering noises.  The rigidity turned out to be a result of interference between the shock (a Fox Twin Clicker) and the swingarm, at the top of its travel.  The suspension would hang there until a sufficiently huge bump came along to break it loose.  Then it would lock up again until the next huge bump.  Also, the brake stay arm was an erector set refugee, poorly installed, the brake lever scraped the swingarm, and the canister rubbed against the caliper banjo bolt.  Okay.  At that point I realized I will never trust this thing on a track until I have removed every nut from every bolt on the chassis, and inspected and assembled it myself.  The June and July race weekends come and go, and it's not ready.

 Finally, with the September race date looming, I got the chassis done.  I put the fresh engine in Dave's GS500 street chassis, to get a couple hundred easy miles on it before subjecting it to racetrack torture.  The break in miles have to do it some good, and it's also nice to find out it doesn't leak and it runs pretty well.

 Friday - Practice Day

 Friday morning, practice day, the air temperature was barely out of the thirties.  Someone in the first (fast) practice immediately fell off, which got the session red flagged and required some track cleanup.  Eventually, the slow group's (that's me) turn finally came.  I suited up, all nervous and excited, head over to the track entrance, and...stupid bike fouled a plug.  I sputtered and backfired my way back to the Evil Pumpkin Patch, mooched a plug from Dave (lesson learned: Bring more spare everything!), and it seemed to run ok.  Next practice session I finally got on the track and, never having ridden this thing faster than 30 mph before, I take a few really easy laps.  I want to be convinced it's not out to kill me before I start pushing it.

 Several practice sessions later, I'm doing much better, the bike is running well, but it seems real nervous.  Is the shock ride height set wrong?  No.  Are the forks set to the right height in the triple clamps?  Yes.  Is the static sag correct?  Not too far off.  Dave and I swap GS500s for a couple laps, just for perspective, and Dave comes in and says my forks are way underdamped.  That makes sense, because I had started with ATF (which is supposed to be 7W) in there.  I was able to buy some 15W fork oil at the track from Mason Racin' Tires, and I set about replacing the fluid before the practice sessions ended for the day.  I was lucky and only missed two sessions, one which was redflagged after one lap and the other after two.  I rode the final practice of the day and the bike felt MUCH better.

 Saturday - The Sprint Races

 Saturday started off cold too, and of course somebody had to crash immediately in the first practice.  Now, I know I'm going to crash, and more than once, but what is it with these people who don't understand about cold tracks?  Are these the same guys who, in a couple months, are going to be plunging their pickups and snowmobiles through two-day-old half-inch-thick lake ice?

 Anyway, they crammed twenty sprint races into Saturday, in order to leave Sunday open for the endurance race.  I was in four of the sprint races, and:

  • I never came in last;

  • I stayed upright and on the track;

  • By the end of the day, my lap times were four seconds faster than in May;

  • My tachometer died, but otherwise the bike worked fine;

  • Most importantly, I started getting comfortable enough on the track that I could get my mind beyond self-preservation and start thinking about how to improve.

 The biggest disappointment of the day was I never did make it through turns one and two without backing out of the throttle a little.  I'd approach turn two, and it was like my spinal cord took over and FORCED that throttle hand to back off, just a little.  I think the problem was I wasn't really sure of the line I was taking.

 Sunday - The Endurance Race

 The parallel activity Saturday was planning the Sunday endurance race.  The planning went like this:

                Dave:  Are we going to run this endurance race tomorrow?

                Me:  Sure.  I guess.

                Tate:  Well, ok.

                Dave:  Whose bike?

                Me, Tate: {silence}

 So Sunday we signed up for it, with Dave's GS500 as the victim.  This should be a stimulating afternoon - all the different classes on the track at once, the openclassers rocketing by us down the front straight.  We even had a pit crew, Nancy (Tate's GF) running timing and Mike Dittrich (CRA #192) (whose bell was still reverberating after a nasty turn three lowside during practice Friday) at the wrenches.  After spending a bunch of time fumbling around getting our pit ready and discussing logistics, we finally took a look at prepping the bike.  Hmmm.  We should run Tate's tires, they're the newest, and maybe stick some fresh brake pads in there.  Of course, that turned out to be far more complicated that it should have been.  Tate installed the new brakes and ran a few practice laps to bed them in, but when he came in they were dragging severely.  We did get it all sorted out, with about three minutes to spare.

 A normal sprint race is five laps, which is maybe ten to twelve minutes of racing.  So a five hour endurance race is like running about twenty-five sprint races, with pretty much no serious maintenance the whole time.

We had no idea what all this implied.  How often should we gas up?   How often should we check the oil?  How will the tires last?  How long will the riders last?  We decided to rotate riders at twenty minute intervals, Tate first (because Dave hadn't found his boots yet).  We figured, twenty minutes of riding, forty minutes rest, piece o' cake.

 When Tate came in after his first shift, he said the bike was acting like it was running out of gas.  Unexpected.  So we gassed  it up, sent Dave out there, and figured Tate must have burned up lots of gas on those practice laps.  Or something.  That the bike only accepted 1.5 gallons (of a 3 gallon tank) was troubling though.

 When Dave came in, he said it still wasn't running right, it would miss and buck going through turns one and two.  Rideable but something wasn't right.  We gassed it up again, but it only took a gallon.  Something else was going on.  It was my turn to ride so off I went, while the rest of the team discussed what to do about the problem. 

 Dave was right.  Cranked over through turns one and two after BIR's long straight, 120 MPH with tires at the limit of traction, it began sputtering and bucking.  I Was Sure I Was Going To Die.  I thought, "Spark plugs.  This thing wants spark plugs."

 Finally, my shift was over.  While I was out the pit crew had decided to try a new air filter and check for gas line kinks.  We did all that plus threw in some new plugs, and sent Tate out.  After a couple laps he came in to tell us it was still no good, so we futzed with the gas lines some more and sent him out to circulate while we cannibalized the coils from my bike.

 The coils did the trick.  Or maybe it was the spark plug wires that went with them.  Anyway, that problem was solved.  But while all that was going on, the accumulated mileage had the back tire developing a bad case of "turn two syndrome", where the tread gets all beveled flat halfway to the tire's right edge.  How is this dangerous?  Well, turn three is a right-hander like turn two, only much tighter.   So as you lean into three you rock the bike up on the outer edge of that flat spot, which has you teetering on a very narrow contact patch.  Which is exactly what happened to Tate, about 3:15 into the race.  Tearing through three, that tire had had enough and just...spit him off.

 Although the bike had tumbled, Tate was able to ride it back the pits, just as we were figuring out he was late and starting to go after him.  He was ok but his race was over, the tank was dented and scratched, the tach was smashed,  and the bars were bent.  It took us about twenty minuted to get the bike track worthy, put my rear tire on it, get reteched, and head out.  It was up to Dave and I to complete the race.

 The rest of the race went pretty smooth, with Dave and I each getting two more shifts.  I went out for the first of my last two sessions determined to conquer the turn two thing, and I did.  I went through there wide open every lap for the rest of the race, screaming in my helmet the first few times, and eventually working it down to just a groan by the end of the race.

 We completed 98 laps in the five hours, ending up third in our class.  Hah!

Not Racing, but kinda close

I've just returned from a long weekend riding the Ozarks of northwest Arkansas and southern Missouri. We did the research, we thought it through, we asked around, and were sure we had it timed perfectly. Right after the edge went off the summer heat and humidity but before the leaves started coming off the trees. Still enough daylight for a good day of riding. Planned it like D-day. And we would have been right, if not for that huge blob of arctic air that flooded down over the entire middle of the country on Thursday night.  But still, there was much riding and much fun. Mistakes were made, and lessons were learned.

We started going to the Ozarks after we concluded the Deal's Gap area had become a little too choked with traffic, squids, and Gestapo for our tastes. Plus, for us it's a twelve hour drive instead of twenty hours. I was down there last spring, plus a spring and fall trip last year. This time it was me, on my ZX-11; Dave, on his new 929 (traded in his TL for it); Rich, proprietor of www.evilpumpkinracing.com, on his Gixxer 600 (just reverted from race trim); Chris, on his YZF600 (the year with the hideous fuchsia wheels); Derek, on his YZF750; and Andy, on his FZR600. Rich, Andy, and Derek had never been down there before so the rest of us got to be tour guides.

It seems that four days of riding the Ozarks is about right, so we generally load the bikes on the trailers after work Wednesday and head south, drive straight through and arrive in Harrison, AR at about 8:00 AM Thursday. We stay in Harrison because there are fun roads all around it, there is a dealership for each of the big 4, plus it has restaurants and Walmarts and stuff. We ride Thursday through Sunday, and head home on Monday.

So Thursday we checked in to the motel, took a 4 hour nap, and headed out right after noon for a quick 200 miler. We knew this might be our best day of riding, with that cold front on its way. So we headed south out of Harrison, eventually hooking up with AR 21. 21 is about 50 miles of fresh blacktop, lots of 90 MPH sweepers, with the forest cut back from the road far enough so you can see through the curves nicely. A perfect ZX-11 kind of road. Plus, since AR 21 doesn't go anywhere, there is no particular reason for cars to be on it. Down at Ludwig we cut over to AR 123 and began heading back to Harrison. 123 is even less traveled that 21, with tighter curves, but the pavement is that crushed gravel surface that is a little harder to read than asphalt. Still a great road.

Friday was A LOT colder than Thursday. 40s early, warming up to maybe the low 60s by afternoon. On the menu was MO state highway 125, the one Chris R. had relayed a recommendation about from the VTR list. And it truly was excellent. Fresh smooth clean black asphalt, no traffic, no side roads, very few driveways. One banked easy-to-read constant radius curve after another, for fifteen miles. Lots of roller coaster hills, too. Many of the curves immediately followed the crest of a hill, so you had to delay your turnin until you're sure gravity has you stuck securely to the pavement. After the first pass through there, we stopped, yanked off the helmets, and were all laughing like idiots. The roads east of 125 in MO were good fun, too. Lots more of that roller coaster stuff.

Later that afternoon, while stopping in Mountain Home, I discovered my water pump (the one I replaced 8K miles ago so it wouldn't fail in the middle of a trip) had begun leaking. This was about 4:00 PM, and, luckily, only 40 miles from home base. I knew I had to get a new one or I wouldn't be riding Saturday and Sunday. I immediately phoned the Kawi dealer in Harrison to get a pump overnighted out, bought some bottled water just in case, and limped back to Harrison. The rest of the groupcontinued south to AR 341, which is another of the great roads.

Saturday morning was below freezing, with a northerly wind. Well, I was stuck waiting for my water pump anyway. Nobody else was in any hurry to head out in that weather, but by about noon four of the group did, while Dave stayed with me on my water pump vigil. Finally at 12:30 the pump came, and by 1:45 Dave and I were on the road. But damn, it had only warmed up to the upper 40s, and even with the sun it was brutally cold. Luckily we hooked up with the rest of the group later in the day, so at least we could ride back to Harrison together.

After a couple of days clipping along at 80 or 90, passing on the double yellow, it all gets to be routine. So late in the afternoon Saturday we were heading back , maybe a little distracted by the cold and in a hurry to get there. I was second in line, behind Rich. We overtook a Burgundy Lumina, and Rich swooped around him. My turn: There was a short straight in front of us, and a driveway a ways ahead on the left side. But I could see the driveway clearly and it was empty. And I'd be by this Lumina well before he got there. Hey - the Lumina is going slower than I expected. No brake lights though. Still it was a clue, but it didn't click with me I swing out in the left lane, not really accelerating, just maintaining 85.

Just as I'm bearing down on him, the Lumina begins sidling into my lane. No signal, no brake light. I didn't know WTF he was doing, and there was no time to do anything but hammer my brakes. The ZX is squirming around, it seems like the whole thing is taking forever, my mind is shrieking "stop Stop STOP!!", the Lumina is RIGHT THERE in front of me, I can't see anything else. Then he begins braking too. I'm getting closer and closer to him. Stupid fading POS ZX-11 brakes! Finally, all at the same time, the ZX stops, the Lumina turns into the driveway, and...the left edge of his rear bumper snags my front wheel, yanking it to the left. FWOMP. I went over on the right. Just a static tipover. Too swamped with anger, humiliation, and relief to think clearly, I immediately picked the bike back up and restarted. I glanced over at the Lumina, which by this time had driven about 40 feet up the driveway and stopped. Finally he was aware something had happened, and he was sitting there watching me in his rearview mirror. I glared at him for a moment, decided nothing productive could come of talking with him, and blasted out of there. Spent the rest of the ride back kicking myself in the ass for letting the whole thing happen.

Total damages to the right side are scuffs on the fairing lower, the mirror, the front turn lens, and a few tiny dents on the muffler, which are not worth worrying about.

I got off cheap on that one. I figure my bodyman can make it all gone for $250.

So what did I do wrong? Well, for one thing, I forgot that the idiot in the car doesn't see you. Duh. And I shouldn't pass at such a high closing speed. And the driveway up the road, by itself, should have vetoed the whole pass until we were clear of it. I can be thankful that our group passes one at a time, so I didn't have to worry about getting rear ended when I was on the brakes, and I knew no one was in the way had I the opportunity to swoop around the right side of the Lumina.

Sunday morning the temp was 22 degrees, so we loaded up and came home. Which wasn't a big disappointment for me, as I was still kind of rattled from the Saturday incident.

The whole time we were down there, we saw exactly two other sport bikes, who were riding together. The other trips have been much the same. Nobody goes there to ride. We talk to the locals, tell them what we're doing, and they get this blank look. "So...you're not here for the Corvette Rally in Eureka? You're not just passing through? You're not fishing? You're not going to Branson? You're just riding around. Huh." The bike dealerships are full of ATVs and cruisers. The locals don't know what they have. Damned if I can figure it out.

We'll return next spring.
