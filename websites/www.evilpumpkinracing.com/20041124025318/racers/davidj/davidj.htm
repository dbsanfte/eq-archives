Victory at the Speed of Suck

David Johnson #242E

BIR September12-14, 2003

Young testosterone-laden pup-type racers find victory in...well...victory. Older, wiser, more mangled-type riders, such as I, find victory in other things.

  • Not breaking important structural mammal bits
  • Not crashing someone else’s bike
  • Not wasting money on unnecessary rain tires
  • Not forgetting your tire warmers, racing license, or armored battleshorts
  • Not losing your credit card, car keys, or pit bike
  • Surviving ANOTHER YEAR –YAY!

If you buy into this, (yes you do, shut up and embrace it) then it was a victorious race weekend. Here are the details:

 

Practice is Perfect

Practice is always fun. You get to scream around the track at whatever speed you want, endangering colorful cow hides and wearing out perfectly good vehicular hard parts, all while wantonly flaring off the precious, life-giving hydrocarbons that somebody’s grandchildren will wish they had some day (i.e., it is satisfying). Plus you get to try new secret race techniques, harass your friends, and size up the competition; all with no pressure or sense of urgency. It is the "calm blue ocean" form of racing. Much less stressful than the "red hot poker up the ga-zing" form that comes later.

 

GS Be a Beeatch

(Note: The following analogy only works for guys and lesbians. Sorry.)

Have you ever had one of those unpredictable psycho-nutzo girlfriends? You know the ones; ridiculously happy, charming, and devoted one minute, then the next minute they run off with Bubba, the married, ex-con, one-armed sewer technician. That’s what it is to race a GS 500. The one I am thinking of right now has been my own personal little demon-child for four years now. During practice on Friday and Saturday it was flawless; it didn’t use oil or leak anything, and it ran strong without a hint of trouble. OK, there was one hint. As I was getting off the track at the very end of the very last practice, it started having a case of the wheeze (where you turn the throttle wide open and you only get this weird, injured wookie sound). I attributed this to heat, low fuel, and imagination, since I could not get it to happen again in the pits. I didn’t have time to do anything about it anyway, what with the race happening in 15 minutes, thank you v ery much cruel hand of fate! I gave the little beast a full tank, some soothing words, and a brief rest. It fired up willingly at race time and carried me confidently to about turn one-and-a-half of the warm-up lap. Then it was wookie time. "Wooaag! Wooaaagg!" it said to me, letting me know it had once again tricked me into an untenable situation. I "woooaaged" all the way back to the pits at 12 mph, much to the delight of the corner workers I’m sure. I parked the back-stabbing traitormobile and turned my thoughts to my more faithful companion, Lil’ Miss SV. Maybe there’s one of those, what-cha-call, life lessons here.

 

Lightweight: The Everyman Class

When I got the SV 650 three years (and two SV 650s) ago, it was lonely out there. Now the whole team seems to have SVs, as do various friends, acquaintances, nemeses, assorted weirdoes, and possibly some lemurs and tree sloths. It must have been a good idea. I want credit. And hey! What took you so long? Yer’ not all tree sloths! My compatriot, Rich "The Great Pumpkin" Nelson, was doing especially well on his nice new ’03 model, what with all the fuel injection and Power Commander and extra horsepower and adjustable preload and ..... Dammit! This is going to cost me $8,000, isn’t it? CRAP! To make matters worse, Commander JenSpeed also nabbed an ’03 SV so he can harass me next year with all that fuel-injected cheaty power. Arrrgh! At least Tate and Billy are still playing with the same 2-year-old outdated machinery that I have, so I can continue to annoy them effectively. I gotta’ have something to look forward to!

 

Actual Races

The lightweight races were lots of fun, mostly because I was able to romp around safely and still hinder all my teammates. In the Lightweight Superbike race, I missed out on a golden opportunity to finish just in front of Tate, because my old feeb eyes failed to detect the #@#$%^&* white flag. When we came around turn ten, I let that goober go by so I could draft him down the front straight (this has a high annoyance factor and is therefore crucial). But here’s an Important Racing Tip: This is an ineffective strategy on the checkered flag lap! After this debacle, I decided to get my eyes checked. They were 20:70. The eye-people were horrified when I told them I was racing with these eyes. At least I think they were eye-people. They looked kind’a fuzzy. Might have been bears. But hey, I figured as long as you stay ahead of the moving blurry things, and stay on the fuzzy black flat thing, everything is OK, right?

The endurance race was a hoot! This is probably the kind of racing I am best suited for these days, what with my advanced cellular decomposition and victurophobia. All a person needs to do in this race is ENDURE. And I did. This means a reasonable, easy-on-the-sphincter pace, with a little throttle steering here, a little tiptoeing there, and lap times I can comfortably do all day. It would have made for a nice, relaxing sort of race, except 2/3 of the way through the thing, Bruce broke our bike. Actually he was minding his own business in turn two at a buck-thirty, when the chain flung it self at another rider in a fruitless attempt to take out the competition. This resulted in Bruce arriving at turn three straddling an unmotivated rolling poop. He searched his vast repertoire of useful racing strategies for this scenario and came up with bupkis. That’s when the B. Hewitt Inc. racing entourage leapt into action. It was quite an experience. I usually can’t find a single person to h elp in the pits, but Billy brought along Bob, Jesse, Fred, Wally, Amanda, Wilbur, Enos, Marty, Sandra, Liz, Felix, Nate, Frank, Alan, Beth, Dan, Linda, Traci, Sam, Jasper, Brent, Shawn, Karla, Millhouse, Noreen, Kelly, Mitch, Eric, Angela, Ward, Emily, Doug, Larry, Candice, Terry, Bilbo, Mindy, Cindy, Gus, Kathy, Ralph, Mike, Jean, Mark, Tony, Sven, Sheila, Greg, John, Paul, George, and Bingo (the dog). (Oh yeah...Thanks to everyone -Hope I didn’t leave anyone out) They all piled into a truck Clampett-style and blitzed out to where the bike was. They swarmed over the bike like crazed mountain men fixing a busted still.

 

Meanwhile I was standing around broiling in my leather jammies, wondering if/when the bike was going to show up, and in what condition. Thirty minutes later, after all my electrolytes had seeped into my undergarments, Bruce and the stricken vessel showed up at our pits. "What’s the story on the chain?" I screamed at him through my helmet. He proceeded to tell me the whole story that I already knew and I said, "No, no, no! What is the status of the chain? THE CHAIN?" He said "We put a new one on. Had to safety wire the master link. Hope it will hold. Felt a little weird on the way back. GOOD LUCK!" Oh lovely! There’s 42 pit crew guys standing there looking at me, waiting for me to jump onto what is probably a seventy horsepower chainsaw and twist the throttle. Fine! Let’s go! I hear they can do wonders these days reattaching severed body parts. I headed out onto the track and just went for it, figuring if I was on the throttle; the chain would fly off the bike and cut someone else in half. I was actually surprised when it never did. But now I had a new issue; the rear tire had long since stopped being what we seasoned racer-types refer to as "optimal," and was now in the "banana peel" phase of its existence. I had one episode where the back end came around so far I could actually see my own butt in front of me. At that point my higher brain functions stepped in, flushed all the testosterone and adrenaline into my appendix, and inserted the "calm blue ocean" program. I rode that puppy home like an ostrich on ice skates, and thereby lived. And it was good. We got 6th in Heavyweight Supersport against all the wundersquids on their 750s. I’ll bet there are some good stories in the teams that finished behind us!

Thanks to the wonderful folks at Sage Homes and JenSpeed, for enabling my dysfunctional affinity for stupid speedy things. Thanks also to Billy Hewitt for providing the bike we tormented for 5 hours, and to Bruce for not crashing at 130 mph when the chain let go. Good luck with getting that stain out of your racing undies. See ya’ll next year.

 

Results

Ultralight Supersport DNS (Ungrateful beeatch)

Lightweight Supersport 10th

Lightweight Superbike 11th

Endurance Race 6th ...in Heavyweight Supersport on an SV650? (Ask Billy)

Plus I got 70 (yes, that’s SEVENTY!) total points for the season. That is enough to get me into Dog Heaven. Won’t ol’ Rex be surprised to see me! Woof!

 

Nothing is Good

David Johnson #242E

BIR July 18-20, 2003

During this last race weekend, I kind’a felt like background noise. Everyone had more adventures than I did, which in some ways, is good.

  • Billy had his first crash (turn eight gravity gust)
  • Bruce crashed twice for the same reason (engine seized – probably that socket I dropped into his intake – shhh!)
  • Tate found new speed on his SV (probably due to his strict diet of aspartame and cheesy poofs)
  • Commander JenSpeed rode so hard trying to keep up with the class cheatywanks on his GS, he actually broke both handlebars off (yes, he is nuts). He did get into the 1:59’s; an astonishing speed for a GS, which shall heretofore be known as "The Speed of Jen."

Amidst all this hoopla, I rode around BIR quietly, trying to increase the velocity of a one-armed, half-blind, psychologically-scarred old feeb. Nothing too remarkable happened. And it was good.

 

Fixing the Feeb

To answer the burning question that nobody asked, yes, I am still recovering from moderate mangle-osis and brain murkiness caused by using my head and torso for high speed agricultural tillage at Mid-America. Which brings up another question: What the hell did they ever grow there?? Seaweed?? Does it ever dry out???? CRIPES!!! But I digress. The point is, I am practicing in every session I can (slow and medium) to clear the psycho-rubbish out of my brain pan and get the old feeb body used to the idea of flapping around on the bike. Some progress was made. Here’s how that went:

 

Meet Mr. Squid

Friday was another Edge Performance (www.edgeracing.net) track day; a chance for CRA racers and street squids of all skill(?) levels to mix it up on the track at high velocity. It sounds worse than it is, and it definitely gives you practice passing and dodging unpredictable riders. You might not expect someone to jump on the brakes for turn one, but maybe you should! The track day is what we get instead of our usual CRA practice because of the recent scarcity of corner workers on Fridays. I just can’t understand why there is not a morass of people who want to burn a vacation day, drive up to Brainerd, stand around in the sun/rain/sleet/mud/cosmic rays for 12 hours doing thankless, dangerous, filthy work for free, just so I can have fun. What the hell happened to everybody?? Seriously, I can’t believe they can get people to do this at all, and I am extremely grateful for the many fine services they provide. For instance:

  • Turtle shooing
  • Deer harassing
  • Marmot wrangling
  • Skunk annihilating
  • Gull intimidating
  • Giving the thumbs-up, even to goobers
  • Mid-track flotsam fetching
  • Oil-dry dispersing
  • Ecosystem participation (supplying vital blood to the area’s insect population)

Oh yeah, and they also flap fags at us and stuff. Yay corner workers!

 

Saturday Danger Fest

I was feeling alright, if a little parched, after doing four practices on Saturday morning. My Ultralight race was the first thing after lunch, so I stowed away some protein patties in the digest-o-mat and got psyched up. Or slept. I forget. Anyway, when the flag dropped, I was ready to rock! I got a great start on the ol’ GS and was happy all the way to turn one, where I realized (again) that everyone had more horsepower than even my special, elite Supersport motor. Suckage! Fortunately, there were a few of us honorable "old school" racers (i.e., too poor to cheat) at the back, and some fast novices. The racemasters decided to let experts and novices all go in one wave (yay!) and we had a grand time fighting over seventh place, or whatever the hell it was. But I learned something; these people are just as nuts as the ones up front! One racer, who shall remain nameless (let’s call him "Crazy NASCAR Todd #336"), really wanted the spot I was in. We kept trying to sim ultaneously inhabit the same space/time coordinates, despite important reasons why we shouldn’t. In one particular incident, CNT # 336 swerved over and made contact with my front tire just as we were entering turn one at a buck-twenny! The GS hated this idea, went into a violent headshake, and made me swallow my gum. This only made me more determined (I was saving that gum) so I glommed onto him like a remorseless remora. In the end, we crossed the line at the exact same time. I think he was running a cheater battery in his DB-COM though, because it gave him the win by 1/100th of a second or something. PROTEST!!

My second race of the day came really soon after the Ultralight event. I was still tired from the previous race, but I somehow got a freaky good start. I stayed ahead of my nemeses the Evil Tate (Team Chew More Tobacco) and his cohort Billy (Team Hippie Van) for a few laps. Then the stars aligned, my feeb arm flaked out, my brake pads shed their last useful molecule, and I went to study the weeds outside turn three at high warp. This was a relatively dreadful experience, but I had to get back on the track and pretend it was nothing so as not to show weakness in front of the other carnivores. Unfortunately, that little delay allowed me to meet up with Commander JenSpeed, who was riding up a class on his GS and annoying us all by going Lightweight speed on his Ultralight slugmobile. Damn him and his accursed skill! After all this ass-nipping and near-death drama, I was grateful to finish the race at all. My 9th place felt way better than Billy’s turn eight swamp romp. (Important racing t ip: Steer away from the cattails.)

 

Sunday Funday

By the time Sunday rolled around, I was feeling a little fustigated and more willing to ride with the intent of survival. Maybe Tate slipped some Pamprin into my beer or something. Despite my general lassitude, I ran all my practices, ate my vegetables like a good boy, and signed up for one race; the Lightweight Superbike. This time when the flag dropped, I got what we experienced racers call a "crud start," and ended up behind just about everybody. This contest was made even more interesting by a huge tailwind on the front straight that resulted in much faster than usual entry into turn one. That same helpful tailwind became a powerful, butt-clench-inducing crosswind in turn two right where we hit maximum lean angle, maximum speed, and maximum sphincter flutter. After a couple of these freaky trips through turn two with at least three forces of nature trying to kill me, I found myself a nice quiet spot. It was near the back of the expert pack, right behind teammate Billy. I decided to become his barnacle. I was having great fun harassing him by letting him plow the unstable air all the way down the front straight and through turns one and two, while I sat two inches off his tail at ¾ throttle relaxing and singing little songs to myself. Then I would pop up and get him in turn three, let him pass me back in turn ten, and do it all again. In the end, I got a chance to scrape him off on some traffic and make a dash for the finish. It worked! I felt like a successful, bloated tick, dropping off the dog just before the flea spray got there. I think I got more enjoyment out of that 11th place than any of the other races. Doctor Freud, what’s wrong with me?

 

Summary:
  1. Everyone on my team is soaking up the carnage so I don’t have to. Thanks.
  2. Nutball racers exist at all levels out there. Want to be safe? Stay home.
  3. Annoying my teammates on the track has some sort of fascination for me. Are there drugs for this?

 

Results:

Lightweight Supersport 9th

Lightweight Superbike 11th

Ultralight Supersport 6th

More of Some Things, Less of Others

David Johnson #242E

BIR, June20-22, 2003

I’m getting a little frustrated here. It has been six weeks since the last race, and I have spent the entire time acidulously trying to grow new cells for bodily repair. Unfortunately, the repair instructions must have gotten lost (i.e., my DNA is unreadable, probably from all the fumes I’ve inhaled over the years) so all I can make anymore are tons of neurotransmitters that stomp around in my brain reminding me what hurts. The doctors can’t fix anything; they only know how to surgically implant debt where none existed before. If Sage Homes (www.mysagehome.com) were fixing me, I’d be done by now. (Oooo, nice plug). So...once again, I was determined to race, despite the decrepitude that has engulfed me and the relative poverty I am currently enjoying. Yes, times are hard, but our beloved sponsor (that’s Sage Homes folks!) is helping us out with some costs. Yay them! This means racing is cheaper than it has ever been, and ya’ just can’t say no to that ! It is even cheaper than usual, because my distressed skeleton and resultant psychological trauma make me reluctant to work the controls with any great vigor, and thus I am much easier on tires and hard parts. And I am slow. Very slow. To make up for this I brought two bikes, the GS 500 and the SV 650, just because….well, why the hell wouldn’t you? The only way to get back to where I was a year ago (gawd, that’s depressing!) is to keep going out there.

 

MY PLAN

Fixing the Body They can’t. $7,000 please.

Yup. This is how they want to leave it. Hooray for modern medicine. Why didn’t they just spay me while they were at it? Horse doctors!

Fixing the Brain Where the hell is the "SYSTEM RESTORE" button on this thing? I need to go back to the last operating system that worked. I’ve discovered that after a few severe damage-type incidents, the "logical" intelli-brain has trouble convincing the "don’t jump off that cliff" instinct-o-brain that everything is OK. "Sure the ground is ripping along at a buck forty on my knee puck, but I’m sure it’s safe this time," says the front brain. "Yer mama!" says the hind brain. And so on. It doesn’t take much argument between brain units before you find yourself going slower. Add in a healthy dose of pain every time you move, and pretty soon focus is lost and going slow becomes easy. Hey, if I go slow enough, maybe I can go back in time and fix this mess.

Practice OK, Race not OK

I turns out I can practice and not look like a total cow plop, mostly because there is nothing to compare me to. In the races, however, there is no escaping reality and the harsh light of DB-COM. On the GS 500, I am 8 seconds off my best time, and I am 6 seconds off the pace on my SV. Those turtles that occasionally cross the track just scurry out of the way from my perspective. Solution: practice, practice, PRACTICE! I went out for all the slow and medium practices. It hurts, but it is good for the brain. And the drug companies. Demerol please.

 

THE RACES

Lousy Races with Memorable Moments

I only entered three races this time, hoping to gain a little ground without hitting the ground. (Terrible! ed.) I was all ready to impress the pit bunnies in the Ultralight Supersport race, which is, of course, the premier event of everyone’s weekend. Unfortunately, I was disqualified for equipment problems (yes, yes, very funny), and got ejected from the grid. Everyone needs that experience. Then you get a whole day of "What happened to you out there?" and "Hey man, where’s yer belly pan?" (Shut up, shut up, SHUT UP!!!). Jeesh! I have enough psychological scars. In the Lightweight Supersport race, I meandered around, not really bothering anyone. Then teammate Tate showed up, passed me and got me all stirred up. I chased him for a bit until we got to turn nine, where he tried to go 4-wide into the bus stop next to an orange-shirted newby. Front row seat as Tate gets run off and goes dirt mining – priceless! The Ultralight Superbike race was, as we experie nced racers say, a total wank. Everyone is now running those wretched $10 K Ducatis, or they have spent cubic dollars on their GS motors. I am still running my back-up endurance race motor, which also doubles as a low-powered aquarium bubbler. The result was I was not just last, but out of the draft by turn one in the first lap. This made for a lonely race. I think the class is wrecked.

 

THE OTHER STUFF

Don’t Need No Stinkin’ Cards! Last month, CRA gave all racers little cards instead of the usual wristbands. Doubtless because of my scathing review and general lambasting of this practice, they have now switched back to wristbands. To punish us for not getting on board with their little card idea, they found us the world’s itchiest wristbands. These prickly little parasites were certainly responsible for many crashes.

Dance of the Pit Bunnies

Six weeks ago, it was cold and lonely in our pits, with only occasional visits from girl critters wrapped up like wooly mammoths. This month was a different story. Not only was it hot, but an estrogen bomb went off in our garage, and a cloud of sugar-plum pit bunnies was released. It really dressed up the place. It’s not that Tate doesn’t look fetching in his sweaty spandex shorts, all covered with Dorito crumbs and oozing tobacco juice, but the ladies provided much needed feminine tomfoolery. There was the amazing Kelly, who showed up in her warm weather wear, thoughtfully providing some three-dimensional imagery to help the racers with their depth perception. Then there was the wonderful Jen; she who supplies the frou-frou coffee and must therefore be revered. And Billy surprised and delighted us all by showing up with a sensible girl. She’s like…..normal. What happened to the screeching-psycho-money-sponging-non-rent-paying-drug-sucking-beeatches? Our little Billy is growing up (sni ff!).

There was a Nip in the Air How can I put this politely? Our timekeeper, Mistress Kelly, has more pontoonage than the average pit bun, and way fewer inhibitions. The result is a scenario that you normally only get on pay-per-view. Naturally, as a respected member of the community, I tried to remain aloof. Ok, so I took a picture. OK TEN. I just wanted her to feel welcome. I should have brought several video cameras to ensure adequate welcoming. Oh, and some safety goggles.

In your face again! The illustrious Commander JenSpeed and his GS 500 continued to irritate the Ducati 620’s, EX 500’s and assorted cheatywanks in the Ultralight class, much to everyone’s delight. Reward him by going to his site (www.jenspeed.com) and buying yourself some armored racing battleshorts or something. Hey, the pit bunnies really dig those! Get the optional Schnitzel protector.

 

Summary
  1. Driving around the track at 0.7 race pace hurts like hell. Oxycodone helps, but it’s a bad idea while racing. OK. Pain it is.
  2. The concept of not racing is worse than the pain of racing. The concept of crashing my already mushy meatbag body into the ground AGAIN is somewhat unsettling, and requires massive beer inputs at the end of the day for recalibration. That’s right. I have a MEDICAL NEED, so it’s deductible.
  3. There were some noteworthy non-racing proceedings in our pit area, and in the surrounding countryside. Bruce and Commander Jenspeed claimed they were spooning. Did I hear that right? Yuck.
  4. The nice folks at JenSpeed and Sage homes are making it possible for me to race. Thanks to them for their technical and financial support. Thanks also to my other teammates for helping me with that EVIL shoulder-wrenching front stand and for shoehorning my busted ass into some leathers so I can race. Gads, what a feeb.

Fast Friends, Fresh Boobs, and Foolish One-Armed Racing

BIR, May 2-5, 2003

 

It sucks! I’m an old, mangled feeb and I can’t go racing. Or so I thought. After my latest debacle at MAM (April, 2003), I figured the season was gone. My collarbone and shoulder were no longer on speaking terms, and all the associated muscles were in a snit. Somehow, through willpower, luck, and copious amounts of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, I managed to get well enough to zip my own pants and drive a car. With this new triumph in my pocket, plus the promise of a newly packed boob-job on the team’s timekeeper, I was compelled to make the journey. My friends, family and dog were aghast, but it had to be. Besides the two important reasons listed above, I also wanted to be there for our first meeting with the team’s new sponsor Eric, from Sage Homes (www.mysagehome.com) If you want me to keep writing this blather, call him right now and order up that dream home you’ve been wanting. They can also fix up that hovel you’ve been living in. Or they ca n build you a hovel, if that’s what you really want. ANYWAY……

I knew there were going to be some compromises on this mission. First, I decided I would only bring the SV 650. Unlike the emotionally-troubled GS 500, it hasn’t thrown me off for… oh… say… almost ten months now, so it is currently in favor. Furthermore, I figured I could minimize the risk to my pulpy torso by riding a bike that actually has brakes. I also had to face the fact that all my racing buddies would sense my weakness and prey on me like a pack of rabid dingoes. So despite the little voice in my head insisting politely "Are you out of your #$%#&* mind!??!" I decided I would jump on the bike, flail at the controls with my atrophied Tyrannosaurus arms, and hope for the best.

Problem 1

One of life’s universal truths: People who have dislocated clavicles have no business putting on racing leathers. I have grown to hate these leathers since April. Imagine trying to delicately cram an overcooked turkey into a small leather handbag and you pretty much get the picture. Besides the fact that these leathers seem to have gotten smaller, they lack the sophisticated factory racer look they once had. There used to be a wonderful leather graphic of the evil pumpkin on the back with our web address. Now it looks like a pizza that somebody sat on with the caption "ww-e>i\pun{i^.c0/". At least the outside looks like the inside feels. I finally decided to just leave them on all day, and even considered sleeping in them. Besides, it would have come in handy if I crashed my bed during the night.

Problem 2

I imagined that I might not be able to take the braking forces generated in turns 3 and 10, since I am unable push a doorbell without getting a boisterous pain message. For once I was right. Grabbing a handful of brakes caused the bone to go "ping", and releasing the brakes produced a "pong" as everything snapped back into place. I found this distracting. This pretty much relegated me to practicing for next month, and allowing all of the glory and pit bunny adulation to fall upon my teammates. So I decided not to race, but instead to harass my buddies a little during practice (try to dingo me, will they!). This role is normally reserved for Bruce Magee, who sneaks up quietly then shrieks past on that loosely-contained grease fire that he rides (RS 125). I see now that this niche is very rewarding, because your teammates SHOULD learn how to go two-wide through the high speed sweepers, and deal with someone constantly poking a tire in at every corner.

Besides all this fun stuff, a bunch of weird things happened, which I will present in the form of a list.

Got card? CRA gave the racers little cards instead of wristbands this year. Now after you push your bike up the ramp to the narrow little inspection platform in the tech barn, you get to search your pockets while balancing the bike with your face. If you crash the bike in tech, do you have to be re-teched?

Safety first! I got honked at by enraged, truck-mounted BIR security forces for speeding on a sand road in the middle of the track’s infield on a Honda Spree (top speed 25 mph on pavement downhill with a tailwind).

"Don’t look at me!" A guy slapped me on the butt and told me how nice he thought it was. Eeeww.

Nothing like planning ahead… Another racer decided to buy my buddy’s race bike so he could use it in the next race. It was third call to grid at the time. (How fast can you peel numbers?)

"For the Love of Gawd!" Something beyond horrible must have happened in the men’s room. There was a dense, non-dissipating fug in there all day on Friday. The air was so thick, you could have carved it up and sold it to French people as cheese. I had to maintain until I got back to the hotel.

In your face! Despite the rule change that allows Ducati 620’s, EX 500’s and other wankermobiles into the Ultralight class, our hero and benefactor Mr. P. Jensen (www.jenspeed.com) finished first in three of his four races on a GS 500. So nyah!

 

Summary
  1. It is possible for a mess of disorganized mammal giblets to get zipped into a leather suit and go racing. There will be considerable caterwauling during the zipping process, however.
  2. It is worth going to BIR just for practice, because annoying your teammates on the track is vitally important.
  3. It is worth going to BIR just to watch all the weird stuff that happens. Beer seems to enhance the experience.
  4. Having a teammate wail on all the cheaty-wankish competition is almost as satisfying as doing it yourself. And safer.
  5. It was really cool to see a coherent, happy, sponsored team all in a row with matching stickers, hats, shirts, banners, and swarms of onlookers. And let’s not forget the tiny pink racing camisoles. Thanks again, Mr. Jenspeed.

How I Never Learn Anything and Still Love it: A Tragicomedy in Four Parts

MidAmerica Motorplex April 11-13 2003

 

It began much as every race weekend. Mad rushing to do all the things I had all winter to do, loading, driving, unloading, preparing new tales of boldness and victory to impress the pit bunnies. You know. The usual stuff. This year, however, we have the honor of being sponsored by Sage Homes (www.mysagehome.com), a high quality builder/remodeler and JenSpeed (www.jenspeed.com), a dealer in aftermarket motorcycle parts and accessories. Do you have a house or a motorcycle? Then stop reading this twaddle and go buy their stuff! Jeez.

This week’s theme: Don’t put stuff halfway together and assume "someone" will remember to adjust it later. Also, don’t fully assemble stuff and assume it will work just because the bolts are tightened. As the saying goes, when you make assumptions, you make an ass out of you and umption. wait….

First Catastrophe: I took the carburetors from the SV 650 apart at the end of last season, cleaned them, and stored them dry in a locked steel box with a dog guarding them. I foolishly ASSUMED that the Fuel Gum Fairies would be unable to work their devilish magic with such precautions in place. WRONG (insert gameshow buzzer sound). The newly refurbished fancy-schmancy SV race bike ran like a diesel turnip grinder. Four hours of fixing, cleaning, making up new words and missing practices later, the demonic muck had been expunged. Now I was ready!

Second Catastrophe. I jumped on the NEWLY newly refurbished SV and headed out for practice. Yahoo! First gear pulls strong….yes! Second gear is plenty good.,..oh yeah!…Wait…where is third? Where the @#$%^&*is the shifter for that matter??!?!?! Upon closer inspection, I see the dang thing hanging there like an overcooked fish stick. I limped back to the pits and found that Cardinal Rule #1 had been violated: NEVER assemble something half way and ASSUME someone will remember to adjust it later. The shifter location was never really finalized, so the lock nuts on the shift rod were never tightened. Someone would do it later. NO, NO, NO! This is wrong! Learn from my mistake. Make this a better world. So where does that leave us? Does anyone have a tap and die to fix the threads that stripped out on the shift rod parts? Maybe in some parallel dimension, where LEFT HANDED THREADS are common, but not here. So I had to teach myself how to resurrect threads from munge using stone knives, bearskins and one hacksaw blade. No problem.

Third Catastrophe I jumped on the NEWLY NEWLY newly refurbished SV and headed out for practice. Yahoo! First gear pulls strong….yes! Second gear is plenty good.,..oh yeah!…second gear…wait…..second gear….is all I have! ARRGH! I limped back to the pits and found that Cardinal Rule #2 had been violated. Never assemble something and just ASSUME it will work because all the bits are fastened. Everything was there, but one washer ended up on the wrong side of a heim joint and presto: instant crud! The shift rod was jammed against the adjusting nut. OK. Easy to fix. Deep breaths. Calm blue ocean.

Forth Catastrophe I had gotten in a few practices on the trusty GS 500 between bouts of cursing at the recalcitrant SV, and I was getting my old confidence back. There was still plenty to get figured out, because I have not had that much time at the MAM track. Most notable among the obstacles was the evil Turn Three, a tight 180 degree carousel thingy that tightens up at the end. Lots of folks go off there (looking in your direction, Mr. Magee). I thought I finally had the thing figured out, so I was getting on the gas earlier through there in an effort to smite my foes and further impress the pit bunnies. Instead of accomplishing this, I pushed the front at just about the worst time possible and ended up with a line that pointed me off the track. Seasoned veteran racers call this the "stupid" line. I decided I really did not want this line, and sought to make amends by not following it. When I got to the dirt at full lean angle, the line became more of a scribble. The bike, disa ppointed in me for my recent choices, handed me off to the ground. The ground at MAM is actually rather soft, so instead of sliding (generally preferred), I augered in. My shoulder hit first, which spun me and planted my head about a foot deep. Sixty to zero has a strange effect on the old brain pan. I wish I could describe it, but the 10 minutes following the get-off are lost in the ether somewhere (if found. please return to me care of Jenspeed Racing). I can say that Mercy Hospital in Council Bluffs is quite good and full of attentive nurses. After scrambling my DNA with X-rays for what seemed like hours, they concluded that my brain was fine (hah!), but I had a Grade 2 distal AC dislocation (i.e. my collar bone is thwanged off my shoulder at one end). Many thanks to my teammates and other cohorts who policed up all my stuff and took care of me in my mangled, narcotic blurred state. The Doc says if I "take it easy for a while" (well obviously) and take my Vioxx, I should gro w enough new cells to move again in about eight weeks. I look forward to not having to type with my nose.

 

Summary:

  1. I am greatly pleased to have new sponsors and team members.
  2. I should not violate my own rules about building/fixing race bikes.
  3. I am too old to crash, so no more of that! New cells don’t grow on trees ya’ know.
  4. Chemistry is my friend, gravity is my enemy.

Smooth Track, Bumpy Rotors

MidAmerica Motorplex

April 12-14, 2002

I was worried that our first outing at the newly constructed MidAmerica Motorplex would be a replay of last year’s debacle at Heartland Park (see: That which doesn't kill us...still bites) I need not have worried. The track there is flat and smooth as glass-weirdly smooth, in fact. It is made from some exotic new asphalt polymer that is as shiny as Jesse Ventura’s head. Despite that, it seems as sticky as anything I have been on (I haven’t been on Jesse’s head), and it is very easy on tires. I couldn’t even scuff up my knee pucks! The track layout is great, with all sorts of weird fishhooks, tight flip-flop esses, double apex toenail burners, and screaming-in-your-helmet carousels. Take a look at the track map at http://www.midamericamotorplex.com/. The penalty for going off the track is rather high at the moment, because the track is essentially floating on a giant, semi-solidified mud pie. Failure to negotiate a turn results in a sudden stop follow ed by a SPLORK sound as you auger in. Crashed bikes had to be CARRIED away, which was made even more difficult by the large percentage of the earth’s crust still clinging to them. Downed riders became hideous swampthings, frightening pets and small children as they shambled back to the pits. This is a transitory problem however, that should be solved when the grass grows and the land forgets that it was a cornfield. This place will be glorious once the amenities are finished. What do you need running water for anyway? Oh yeah. The mud.

The actual racing was, as is too often the case, fraught with bizarre parts failures of the sort that only come with racing. The first to go was my GS 500’s head gasket. That ungrateful hunk of metal and goo only lasted 1.3 race weekends. I was very fortunate to have a spare in the depths of Pumpkin Clint’s junk box. After standing around thinking the obligatory "I don’t wanna do THAT", we yanked the head off and made the swap. Back in business! It ran flawlessly after that. The SV 650 was not so fortunate. After a practice session on the first day, I noticed it had blown a fork seal BIGTIME. Usually a blown seal gets you some detectable munge on the fork tube, but this thing was slobbering like Pavlov’s own bulldog. Another lap and I would have gotten fork slime on the brakes or tires and gone kersplut. I was lucky, I guess,but the SV was done. The big new Suzuki dealership in Council Bluffs didn’t stock fork seals "cuz them things never go bad". The real winners of this weekend’s mech anical ruinage award were the GS brake rotors. These things went from stone-flat to satellite dish in one 8-lap race. We had three GS 500s running, and all three managed to warp their brake rotors. My rotor had only 1 race weekend on it before this. These are the $175 EBC cast iron "race" rotors. Apparently they are expendable like tires on the GS. No, wait – my tires actually outlasted this rotor. Maybe we can use some friendlier pads or sacrifice some chickens to the lords of thermodynamics. Or maybe we should just use the brakes less. We’d be faster, until we became mudskippers.

Summary:

  1. MidAmerica Motorplex is a wonderful track with lots of potential. Once they get the run off areas converted from cornfield-quicksand to lonesome prairie it will be among the best tracks I've seen.
  2. It is hard to predict what part of a race bike will fail, which is why I bring two. Even so, the perversity of the universe and its accursed laws of physics can strand you. The wily racer brings all the spares he/she can muster and cultivates good relations with (i.e. sucks up to) anyone who might help out with parts later.
  3. There is something up with these EBC brake rotors. I know the GS is hard on rotors because the brake pads are horrid broad flat things that aren't conducive to cooling, and there is only the ONE rotor to slow this careening cow down. Still, the stainless stock rotor lasted me 2.5 YEARS. A solution is needed.

Dry, Upright, and Sucked Through Two

(Webmaster-Maybe NOW we will get some hits!) June 21-23 BIR

Sometimes, just by chance, the laws of nature, gravity and thermodynamics, are nice to me. This is to say the race weekend went surprisingly well, despite great horrid components of disaster lurking everywhere and conspiring with the general contrariness of the universe. (i.e., I was lucky).

WEATHER

Everyone else in the state got hosed by torrential rains all weekend. The Twin Cities (130 miles south of BIR, where my igloo is) got four inches in one day. Even the crazy doom-saying street preachers went away, figuring they had at last been proven right. Meanwhile we were careening brazenly around on a dry track unaware that our basements were filling up like God's own power toilet. We did see the great slobbering rain clouds surrounding us like wolves at a campfire, but for some reason they stayed away. Maybe it was that garlic pizza I had.

RACES

I competed successfully (i.e., did not crash or blow up) in seven races, all of which went well, purified my soul, etc. In the Ultralight Sportsman race on Sunday, we had a good tailwind coming into turn two, so I was steaming in there better than usual on the GS 500. On top of that, I was shrieking along behind Bruce Magee on his RS125.

These little two strokes really cook down the front straight and through the big sweeping turns one and two. I was just able to stay in the draft of this two-wheeled peanut, so I got sucked into turn two much faster than ever before. WHEEEHOOO! I was getting buffeted as I tried to stay in a little pocket of dead air that was marginally smaller than me. The front was drifting and momentum was pulling me out to the edge of the track. Normally you can steer a GS around the track like a shopping cart, but this was ragged edge of doom stuff. Cool. I got some of my best GS lap times ever out of that. OK, it's not exactly the sound barrier, but there are not many GS 500s that have poked the 2 minute mark at BIR. It's one of them whatchacall milestone thingies.

MECHANICAL CONTRIVANCES

For the most part, the machines were well behaved. The SV barfed up a battery, but that is all. Important note: The SV 650 does not yet appear in any parts catalogs. You need to have one with you to even prove they exist. (I blame the millennium bug). Therefore the "YT12A-BS" battery it requires cannot be found in civilization. However, the "CYL9-BS, which only fits undersea mining robots, is readily available and bolts right in. Oh yeah, we flared off another couple of EBC brake rotors, but that's not even news anymore. They wear out faster than brake pads. Too bad they cost fifteen times as much.

Summary:

  1. I shouldn't need to say this, but IGNORE the weather forecast. Monsoons were predicted for the whole weekend, and we got diddley. Perfect racing weather. Unless they are predicting occasional to scattered nuclear missiles, get in the dang truck and go!
  2. If they ever write "BIR for Dummies", it will say "Nothing is more important for Ultralights than having the draft down the front straight and through turns one and two". Those little 125 oil-burning dervishes grabbed me and pulled me away from the rest of the 4-stroke trogs. It's better than free horsepower. I liked it.
  3. EBC should convert to making salad bowls, since all the metal they make ends up as a cone anyway. My stock rotors lasted much longer and might be the best alternative, even though they cost approximately the same as a new bike. I guess I could harvest a kidney or so to pay for all this.

Third Time’s Alarm

BIR July 26-28, 2002

What is it about the number three? Is it mystical or evil or something? Strike three, third time’s a charm, Three Dog Night, etc. It’s just weird. Any way, the Great Pumpkin R. Nelson had to stay home and wax his surfboard, so it was just me and co-pumpkins Clint and Tate at the races. It was just the THREE of us. This was our THIRD race weekend of the year and we were uncharacteristically ready for the THREE days of practice and racing. Little did I know I would soon be visited by the THREE SPIRITS OF DOOM! (Bah!)

Poltergeist Bike

The first indication that I was being stalked by doom (SBD) occurred in the first practice. I was on the SV650, so I was practicing with the middleweights. Just after turn ten, a 600 went past me on the outside. I was just moving over behind him to get a little tow down the front straight when his back tire spun up and a wild tank slapper ensued. I swerved away from the enraged machine, but it spit off its rider, hit the ground hard, snarled out a bushel of sparks, and started coming after me! Strange how a bike on its side can pick up speed and zoom off at some angle to the original direction of travel. By this time I was hard on the gas, but that maniacal sliding junk pile had the angle for an intercept course. It wanted to ram me into the concrete wall that runs along the whole front straight. How rude! I stayed on the gas, moved over to just inches (probably *three*) from the wall and tried to think about being lighter. I made it past just as that beast slammed into the wall, missi ng me by about a foot. Any other course of action, like getting on the brakes, swerving the other way, or even just getting off the gas would have resulted in me becoming road paste. Whew.

Is that Bike Possessed, or is its Rider Just a Goober?

Number two SBD incident occurred during the Lightweight Supersport race. I was having fun dicing with one of my usual SV nemeses. He would draft past me on the front straight and I would get him on the brakes in turn *three*. He must have been getting tired of being passed, because he kept going deeper into the turn on every lap. During one very late braking maneuver, I was about to make the turn, when some nutroll on a third SV tried to outbrake both of us! He thought he could use me as a brake marker I guess, but that didn’t work out so well for him. He went flying past just inches off my starboard bow doing a very nice but unprofitable stoppie, and preceded straight off the track for a little agriculture. Another fraction of a second and I would have been T-boned. Whew again.

Le Grande Splatage – Attack of the Spectral Banana Peel

The THIRD and most hideous incident occurred during the Lightweight Superbike race on Sunday. I had successfully completed the 13 (!!) lap Trophy Dash earlier that day, and had resolved to stay to the extreme inside of turn two for this next race because it was getting too bumpy-scary anywhere away from the edge. I got a great start, got out-motored by the usual cheaty motor guys, and settled into a reasonably brisk pace. OK, actually I was ripping along pretty good with about six guys in front of me and maybe eight behind. Smooth sailing. Then there was lap five. Nothing new or unusual, except in turn two the laws of physics took a brief hiatus, and I suddenly became a meat missile. I can’t really say what happened, even though my brain was not damaged. I was not off my line, I was not making any new steering inputs, I was drafting 6 guys who all had the same line, and I was being drafted by another bunch. For no particular reason, in the middle of the turn, at a buck forty-five, a little blob of evil ectoplasmic road slime dropped in from the netherworld and made my front end go away. The actual fall was about six inches down. It was the angular velocity that bothered me.

So….. (insert the usual question) "What’s it like to fall off at 140+ mph?" All the damage (to me) happened in the first millisecond. I remember saying "oooffff" when I hit, and my right forearm and both hands got quite a slap. Go outside and flail your arms on a brick building (don’t let anyone see you), then imagine that at track speed, and there you have it. Somehow, in the eerie slow motion tumble that followed, I remembered what my grandfather told me on my wedding night. "If you ever find yourself face down, it’s probably a good idea to flip over, ‘cause all the important stuff is in front" So I did. I actually had plenty of time to perfect my superman-mudskipper technique. It was a very surreal experience. As I was buzzing along like a warp-driven earthworm, I had the following thoughts:

  1. Hmmmm. This isn’t so bad……
  2. Oh….there’s my bike. Hey it’s flipping over. OOO! It’s doing it again! I hope it stays over there. Ooop! It’s gone now!
  3. I wish I could see where I am going. Seems to me there is a tire wall around here.
  4. Ahhh! Finally stopped…….. Ow.

When the tweetybirds cleared, I did not feel that I had hurt anything except my fingertips. I felt for sure that the gloves had burned through, but closer examination revealed they were abraded, but fully intact. (Spidi Carbo-Vent, $225 well spent). I sat up, and the ever-watchful corner dude Jason showed up and queried me about something. "Joo a gay?" I was fine, except I could not hear very well…Oh no! Brain damage! My cerebrospinal fluid must be leaking into my helmet! I…..oh yeah. Ear plugs. I took the helmet off, looked around and noticed the bike was gone. Ascended to bike heaven perhaps?

How’d I miss that? Oh, there it is. Behind the wall, up in that tree. Oh! There’s some more of it over in that tree! At this point the ambulance guys showed up and invited me in for tea. Actually, they were keen to get away from the virus-laden mosquitoes that thrive by the billions in the old tires that BIR lines the track with. By now my fingertips were all purple and fat and I was seeping a little blood out from under the nails. We pulled the leathers off to see what else there was. Actually, I had a hard time convincing the medics to help me, because they thought I was probably just a sack-o-guts ready to spill out onto the floor of their nice clean ambulance. All we found was a neat little spot on my forearm where it had met the track and accelerated to 140-something while the rest of me lagged behind. It was a Trident gum package-sized, shallow rectangle of missing skin with a nice deep bruise swelling up under it. No biggie. I have had much worse on a bicycle. So I went to visit the n ice medical lady, who fixed up the booboo and gave me a lolly. Meanwhile my friends went and swept up the bike. I’m sure they laughed when they found it hanging in a tree, and they probably took pictures.

The final insult came at home in my garage. I was trying to start the poor bedraggled thing with my puffy purple mitts, just to see if I still had a motor, when it became apparent that something was missing. In all of the excitement and angular momentum, the computer (being the only thing on the bike with a brain) decided it was time to leave. This fact was lost on the recovery crew, who were looking for big pieces, so the crafty little black box stayed to sleep with the raccoons. The trouble is they want something like $450 for a new one. To hell with that, I thought, in a rare moment of clarity, and drove the 130 miles to BIR to fetch the wayward box. Out at turn two, I followed the trail of broken plastic out to the woods, accompanied by a dense cloud of maraschino cherry-sized horseflies. I actually found the pricey little box sitting on top of the tire wall (6 feet of old tires that the rest of the bike had cleared in a single bound) where it had become friendly with some moss and goo. Whil e I was there, I stepped off the crash slide, just for yuck. Using the universally accepted one giant step = one yard, I determined that I slid 57 YARDS on pavement, then another 66 on the grass. It took a long time just to walk that far! I would personally like to thank the Italian cow from whom my leathers were made. Good job bossy!

Despite my brush with doom, I continue to walk the earth, eat pizzas and stuff, and write this inane blather. I suppose I need a weekend like this for contrast.

Summary:

  1. During a fall, it is generally good to slide feet first on your back (if you can). This may take a little doing, but it is worth it. A former star Pumpkin rider once went feet first/ face down and ended up with his helmet and sinuses packed with dirt, soil grubs, and untold quantities of bacteria that wanted to colonize him. Yuck.
  2. During a fall, it is best to slide in wet grass. I am not sure how you control this. For my escapade, the grass was wet but not a lake. This was just great for my easy sliding needs, plus I went a more respectable distance, which helps to impress the many pit bunnies.
  3. If you have important bits, like-oh-say a $500 computer on your bike, you might want to secure the sucker with something more robust than zip ties. It doesn’t matter if it is inside the tail section. Tail sections come off and go up in trees.
  4. Luck favors the fellow who buys good leathers (Spidi), armor (Bohn), gloves (Spidi), and boots (XPD). This stuff literally saved my hide. It was expensive, but I would rather give the money to Lockhart-Phillips than Skin Grafts-R-Us. Oddly enough the helmet was fine, which explains why I did not brain my damage.

Welcome to the Lebrea Tar Pits

MidAmerica Motorplex

August 23-25, 2002

I am happy to report that the demise of the MidAmerica Motorplex track surface is greatly exaggerated. There have been many reports that the track’s weird and wonderful glass-like surface had been torn up into a crumbly mess of bran flakes because of cars, improper curing, gamma rays, etc. We the Pumpkins, in order to form a more perfect race team, went forth to investigate. There were a couple of spots (notably turns 3 and 14) where the surface is rough, and there are a few semi-lumpy patches scattered about, but the surface is still mostly smooth, strangely sticky, and fun. Yay for the track.

The infield, however, has not progressed since our last visit (see "Smooth Track, Bumpy Rotors"). It has, in fact, become a nightmarish glop hazard. Anything that went off the track went straight to the Earth’s core and was retrievable only by a team of paleontologists with a winch truck. Just ask Tate "Tar Baby" Guckin. Really. E-MAIL him now. The good news is the track now has running water. The bad news is water is it is completely ineffective for removing this prehistoric muck. They need to install a high pressure hot benzene sprayer. It is not that they aren’t trying. Grass was planted in the infield this spring, but somehow, through the perversity of nature, they only managed to grow sparse agricultural weeds and pond scum. You really can’t blame the track proprietors for the mess. They are fighting intractable clay soil from hell, drought, torrential rains, tornados, and (literally) plagues of locusts. The poor corner workers, all dressed in their white finery, looked like mud wrestlers at the end of the day. I am not sure what makes them want to do this kind of work (counts as community service?), but I am very grateful.

As for my personal racing, I found that the specter of last month’s high speed exit was still haunting me just a tinch. The SV 650 was still parked (OK, more like loosely collected) in my garage at home. The GS 500 was performing well, but the whole "what if my tires let go again for no particular reason" thing was still creeping me out. This was just enough to keep me off the ragged edge of doom that I usually ride to keep up with all the crazed young sacks of testosterone. This business of being the wily veteran is fine until you fall off and it takes 9 years to heal. Curse those 20-something embryo racers and their overabundance of repair enzymes! I still have feeb-hand from my last fall (see "Third Time’s Alarm"), and it is affecting my jar opening skills. Oh, and my braking too. Because of my various enfeeblements, I signed up for just a few races to try and get my comfort level back. This worked only moderately well. I blundered through Saturday’s races, finishing alive, but impressing no one. In the Sportsman Superbike race on Sunday, I got a great launch, got through a few turns ahead of most of the gang, and felt pretty good. Breifly. When I got into turns 5 and 6 (a never-ending 180 degree left carousel) the front end pushed big-time and drifted me all the way out to the edge. I just managed to save it using my veteran wiles (i.e., I did nothing) and backed it off a bunch. All the assorted young crazies and other wily veterans went by as I was repacking my colon, and I just rode around until the flag flapped me home safely. I could not handle the thought of two destroyed race bikes or any more busted mammal bits. Went home early and watched Sponge Bob.

 

Summary:
  1. The MidAmerica Motorplex track is still smooth enough for the Ice Capades, or any other Capades that require a smooth, flat surface.
  2. MidAmerica motorplex might wish to consider opening a museum of prehistoric motorcycles in their infield.
  3. The psychology of racing, not to mention crashing, spending cubic money, and healing mangled appendages, gets a little heavy sometimes. Fortunately, I never took Psychology.
