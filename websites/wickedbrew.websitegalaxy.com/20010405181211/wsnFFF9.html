Guidelines for Joining a Group
Once you're in a group, there is nothing more exasperating than hearing, 'I just have to run and get some supplies, brb'. Unless you're with a group that you party with all the time, expect to take a little heat. If you're going to join a group, be ready. Have all the food and water you need to last you a few (real-time) hours. If you're lucky, a shaman or cleric can magically produce sustenance for you, but don't count on it - they usually need to conserve their mana for healing and fighting. Stay together. Norrath is simply enormous and getting lost is a very real possibility. Stop often and turn around - keep within visual sight of your group. Don't attack a critter unless all your spell casters are ready. Rely on the group leader for the mundane organizational stuff. You may wish to be part of a group that isn't within your level just for the fun of it. Gaining levels is definitely important, but even more so is the experiences you have with others. A month or two from now, you'll remember the battles, quests, and adventures you've had, not how you gained level 5.

Conclusion
For most people gaming is a hobby. We don't have the luxury of spending our 9-5 work or school day sitting in front of a computer playing games. We have responsibilities, obligations, commitments, other activities, significant others, families... you know, all that stuff that gets in our way of playing EQ. We don't all have the freedom to say 'Ok - every night at 6pm Pacific time I'll meet you outside of Blackburrow and we'll form a party'. More likely, we get home from doing whatever we've done all day (who knows what time that'll be), and we plop in front of ye ol' computer and expect to be entertained. So while we'd all rather be playing EQ 24/7, the reality is, it's a lower priority than getting good grades, earning money to pay the bills, eating, sex...well, most of the time anyway. In my opinion, this is the largest obstacle to multiplayer gaming. How do we form consistent relationships with others when our online time fluctuates so much? Additionally, how do we find others who are interested in the same things we are? And, how do we form groups that are beneficial for all involved? Hopefully this little guide will help. Check out this text, you might learn something that will make your stay in Norrath just a bit easier and more fun. We'll cover general group issues first, and then move into specific hints for individual classes while in groups. General Grouping Strategies
You have many people fighting with lots of health. Why should you ever let one person get mangled? Trade hits. Figure out how much health everyone has (ask if nothing else) and then try to spread the damage across the entire group. This way you have 2-6 people regenerating hitpoints (6 hps/tick) instead of one (1 hp/tick). Easy to understand. If you get messed up, back out and let someone else take the beats. Watch out for frenzied monsters... they'll concentrate on one thing until it's dead. ALWAYS back out before you get below 40% hps (2 bubbles). Otherwise there is a good chance you won't get out.
You have lots of people hitting the monster. Do it from all sides. There is a bonus for attacking a monsters backside. USE it.
If anybody realizes that the world is a bad place (ie: that Goblin Wizard was red and no one bothered to check it), and yells RUN!, everyone should BUG OUT, but do it in a controlled fashion. Most likely the person that yelled run is going to run as he calls the retreat which means that staying becomes an even worse prospect for you. People with more health stay back and take hits so those with less health can get away. If you die while holding of a monster so your group can escape, your bravery is most likely worth a medal, the Officer should report this to the guildmaster and it will then be considered by the Elders.After your escape, confer to decide if what you ran from is worth taking another try.
Class Specific Strategies: Fighter Types
My subdivisions are really sketchy and vague, and many classes fit on boundaries and borders between types. Adjust for your group composition and adapt to fill whatever roles you need to. (Fighter Types: Warriors, Monks, Rogues, Paladins, Rangers, Shadowknights, Bard) Most often, if you just follow general strategies you'll be fine. Fighters are straightforward. Fighters are the ones that are taking the primary damage, and hopefully returning the favor. It's your job to annoy the monster as much as possible to allow the others in the party to get in free hits. You have a lot of responsibility. You must keep watch on your health and everyone else's, especially in multiple enemy combat. Attack anything that is attacking your spellcasters, and taunt mobs that won't get off of more vulnerable characters. If you're a good tank, you'll have good armor and relatively high agility, so you'll take _much_ less damage than would another member of your party. However, you can't take _all_ the damage. Here's the part that is really hard. You must know -when- to keep the mobs on you, and when you can let other people take some hits. In a good group, no one leaves combat unscathed. This is because it's more efficient to have everyone healing (especially if you have a Bard in the group) than just one. So, tick off the monster, and then rotate the target, saving weaker members as needed.


Specifics:
Taunt: If you have taunt, practice it. It makes a monster think you've done more damage to it. Brief explaination of monster AI. If you hurt them, they don't like you. If someone heals the person their fighting, they don't like the healer, etc. Taunt effectively makes a monster hate you as if you'd done damage. This allows you to keep the monster's attention so it doesn't go beat on that spellcaster who just burned the monster for 50 damage. This is a _good_ thing (assuming your spellcasters aren't total nimrods and burn before you've taunted the mob). The ideal taunt is one that -just- keeps the monster on you, and if need be, you can let someone else take the darned thing. You do _not_ want to taunt a monster too much, because then it will stick to you until you die and nothing anyone else will do can get it off. This is called frenzy. It's guaranteed to get you killed if you're not careful. Bows: Every fighter than can use one (W,Ro,Ra,Sk) should have a bow. It allows you to back away from a monster and continue to deal damage. This is very useful in a dangerous fight. Also expensive unless you have a Magician along. Still is worth it. Once you are past about level 12 the expense of the arrows is well justified. The mobs begin to do much more damage and also begin to give more loot per mob. Soon spending a gold or two for ten arrows will seem like a neglible loss for such an invaluable skill. Lay on Hands: The ultimate party Paladin panic button. If you can't figure out how to use a 'complete healing' spell... well... this guide might be too advanced for you. Never blow your load on this one and heal someone while the healer of your group still has mana and is not being attacked by a mob. Unless the healer calls 'no mana' (or OOM for out of mana in shorthand), then you're the last line of defense. Harm Touch: Amazing when trying to take out a big nasty. Use as softening to really 'MEGATaunt' the monster. Harm touch really annoys monsters because of the big damage it does. Basically hold this one until you get a mob that you MUST get rid of fast . An example of this would be a mob that hops on when your party has already been severely damaged. Bards: Wow. Get one of these in your party. At L6 they become the best thing this side of sliced bread. Group healing is an amazing addition because when people back out (which they should do at around 80% in a Bard powered party) they come back at fu ll strength. Wow. Their other spells and skills also make them a very well rounded type of character. They have nothing that defines them as a class except for their amazing range of abilities (and the ability to use them indefinitely with no mana cost ). Monks: While unable to use weapons, these fighers make up for it by sheer number of attacks. At high levels, a monk can get off 3-5 attacks a round. EASILY. This makes them invaluable for tanking enemy spellcasters. As all of you casters know, if you're being hit constantly, you can't get a spell off. Monks, remember this advantage and use it to interrupt and beat the hell out of those wizards and shaman that are about to make crispy critters out of your party-members. Another _invaluable_ skill is Feign Death. Practice this one as much as you can. It allows you to immediately drop out of combat, either to switch mob target, to mend, or to wake up the clerics in the party (no cleric likes to see a party member crumple in combat, makes their heart flutter). Feign death makes the monk _the_ single best sacrificial lamb the party has. Fight going bad? Everyone but the monk take off. Monk, run the _other_ direction, and before you get too low, Feign death. You'll have given your party a good 20 second head start. This sort of advantage cannot be underestimated with the new bonuses to back/fleeing attacks. Rogues: Your best place to be in combat is _behind_ a monster. You should _always_ be behind the monster. Backstab is the single most effective melee combat in the game. If you have another fighter who can taunt effectively, you have a tremendous advan tage. If you have a mage who can attract attention with burn, you're equally set. (See Mage section below for tips). Class Specific Strategies: Burn Types
(Burn Types: Wizards, Magicians, Enchanters, Shaman, Druids, Clerics)

Rule #1: At this level (1 to 20) Burn is not a young caster's only version of melee.
Almost all of the casters (including the hybrids) have a way to deal direct damage. Direct damage is a good thing, but is so often misused that I've almost broken my monitor in frustration. Burn is for four things:
Attracting an enemies attention -either to start a fight, or to try to get if off of another party member (be careful with this one, cause you're next then). Finishing an enemy - Either one that is trying to escape, or it is much more efficient to use one last spell to knock it down rather than waiting a few rounds of combat for it to get killed in melee. Panic button - If suddenly the world is a bad place, everyone better be lighting up the enemy in question if the party has any chance of survival. Mid fight - Your warriors have been dealing a healthy amount of damage to the creature. It is now time to use a _little_ burn. Deal enough damage to use your burn abilities to make the combat go quicker but not so much that you A. draw the mob onto you or B. Burn up a ton of mana requiring the group to have a long downtime waiting for you to recover.
One more time: Burn is not a young caster's only version of melee.

Fire support does not mean 'always firing support'. You don't call in an airstrike in every situation. You save it for those instances when standard troops (ie: fighters) are not sufficient. Mana should be saved whenever possible, and then used whenever necessary. Remember that if you have no mana, the party should stop and wait until you do, and if you're being stupid and burning like a flame-thrower, you're just slowing the party down. (You'd be _out_ of my party in a big hurry.) At this point the question is "What _am_ I supposed to be doing?" during the times when the fighters are damaging the creature to the point where burn is effective. This is the time for your weak melee. Let the fighters land two hits and then step in. Your dagger or staff will never catch up to the fighters melee but will do a little damage. Every bit helps and it also allows you to take your share of the hits for more efficient group healing. If you want to be an effective party member, you save the burn for those occasions when it is needed, and otherwise, you attack with your puny melee and try to avoid getting gacked. If the monster decides it likes you, you back out of combat and wait unt il the fighters have it's full undivided attention. Then you continue to prick it as much as you can. When the monster runs, -then- you torch it, because that's more efficient than having your fighters chase it, possibly into more danger. (Root also wo rks well for this purpose, allowing the fighters to finish quickly). There are situations where a bit of judicious burn is necessary, but please err on the side of not burning rather than becoming a torch.Specifics:
Root: Wizards, Shaman, Druids, Clerics, Enchanters. If something is running, DON'T LET IT. Also can be used to stick a big ugly melee mob with no ranged attacks in place. Then the fighters arrow it to death while you fill it full of flames. (One use for a torch, but this is very inefficient killing.) Root is also resisted quite often. Its mana cost seems small at 30 but really the cost is somewhere around 45 or 50 depending on your level and ability with alteration because of these resists. If you can get a de-buff in at the beginning of combat, most of the time it's like burning the whole way through because the fighters are that much more effective. Much more mana efficient too. It is one of t he most subtle of all the spell types since its effects aren't immediately apparent but if you compare debuffed mobs to mobs at full strength the difference is amazing. Pets: Check for more PPI documents on Magicians and Necromancers, but generally consider them another fighter. Control them as such. If they get messed up, back them off so they can heal. In general pets are not as effective in a group if you are taki ng single or dual targets. Against multiple targets they are invaluable. Sicking them on an attacking creature you can put one to be dealt with later while you concentrate on the mobs that are actively attacking your living party members. Class Specific Strategies: Healer Types (Healer Types: Cleric, Druid, Shaman, Necromancer, Enchanter [what?! Read on]) Sure, healers can heal, but they have one more skill that is _so_ much more important that healing pales in comparison. What is this wonderful thing, you are thinking? Buffs. Those magical enhancements that make you faster, better, stronger, difficult to hit, and difficult to damage. The PRIME function of a caster of this type is to buff everyone in the party as much as possible. The secondary function is to keep tho se buffs from wearing off by keeping the people wearing them alive. Usually by casting healing spells to restore health. Clerics and Shaman have the best combat buffs. Enchanters have good buffs and excellent de-buffs (see Burn Types). Buff everyone and watch the monsters writhe. An example of good buff/class synergy is 'faster attack' buffs (Bard or Enchanter) on a Monk. Monks _already_ get 3-5 attacks a round. Increasing their speed, even by 50% moves that into th 4-7 attacks per 'round' range, making it nearly impossible for an enemy caster to get a spell off. Buffs on any fighter type is good. The more attacks one has, the more effective the buffs. Healing should only be used in dire emergencies. Again, mana efficiency is key. If someone drops below 60%, it's a good time to heal them. Also, healing during downtime can be useful. Healing becomes almost unnecessary when you have good defensive buffs on your tanks. The difference they make is amazing. Again just like de-buffs it is hard to see the difference they make at first but once you realize their power you will never let an yone go off without them. It will help you in your quest for adventure in EverQuest. Advanced Group Strategy Introduction Well, it seems that many people have read Intermediate Group Combat. If you haven't, you need to, as it the only 'prerequisite' for this document. In that document PPI covered the absolute basics of group combat, including analyzing the function of each class in a party and basic skills for each class to pay attention to. In this document, we're going to extend and move into more advanced territory, primarily to do with complex interactions between parties and enemy monsters. Some examples of things that can go seriously wrong while in a party: The party has two people scouting for enemies and suddenly, they _both_ yell "Incoming!!" and charge back with big yellow monsters on their tail. As a caster, you are meditating, and suddenly you see your parties health bars dropping... ALL of them. The party sees a group of monsters, and someone mentions an attack... and immediately, half of the party runs directly into the midst of the enemies and begins slashing indiscriminately. You are in charge bringing a monster back to an ambush, when... low and behold... the entire party has decided to head back to town for supplies, leaving you with a new friend and no party favors. You come back with the next kill hot on your tail only to find the rest of your group has engaged some wandering creature nearby without telling you, leaving you to hope they finish the other in time to save you from whatever you brought back. These are all serious group no-no's (just a few of many). Most often they occur because there is a lack of a basic party of any good group. Communication. Without good communication skills, your party is effectively acting as a bunch of solo characters who just happen to be fighting the same enemy (and that's if you're lucky). Most good groups have a designated leader, who acts as a ringleader in a circus, directing the actions of those around him, while avoiding the lions and elephants and gnolls... Gnolls!? However, sometimes groups will function perfectly well without a leader. This usually occurs when a group is comfortable with each other and knows their roles and what needs to be done. These groups will not usually have a leader per se but instead someone (usually a support character) who watches the battle from the outside and keeps everyone informed of what's going on. It is then this person's duties to back the group off so that they do not attract the attentions of wandering creatures that would endanger the group if they attacked. If you are in one of these groups, you're probably very familiar with a good majority of the rest of this document, but keep going, you just might learn something. We're going to cover basic communication skills, leader responsibilities, and then a related bonus section on multiple enemy combat that uses most of these ideas. Communication Without communication, the party is doomed. Those things mentioned in the Introduction _will_ happen, and they will not be fun. Even in parties with good communications, sometimes bad things happen. Mostly because someone either wasn't paying attention, or because someone forgot to mention some action they were taking that could affect the other members of the party. For example, they were going out 'fetching', luring a monster back to the group for an ambush. Groups without a leader can be much less fun to play in because without someone to give direction you are more often than not left standing around waiting for someone to figure out what should be done next. (Note: this is different from 'natural' groups who don't require a designated leader) While most online games have a barrier to communication, the keyboard, there are ways to work around this. Firstly, if you're working with a group of friends or some people you often group with, develop a shorthand that is easy to keep up with. Most of my groups have about five messages that are vitally important and also quick and easily abbreviated: "OOM" (Out of Mana): For a caster, this is a _huge_ important message. Always give warning for both low mana (LOM) and for complete empty tank. If the party doesn't know their healer has no more heals, or that their wizard has no more mana for root or burn... well... someone is going to die. Good healers and burn support characters will be able to read their mana bar and call exactly how many shots they have left. It is more important for the healers. For the burn support characters LOM should suffice. "INC" (Incoming): If a group is using the tentacle camping method (sitting in one place and reaching out and bringing monsters in), this is a key message, as it means that the designated fetch has found something and is coming in with it. A huge warning to prepare for combat (ie: stand up and stop meditating or healing). "[Name] Back out": Get out of melee with the mob. There are others that can take the damage. Sometimes people just don't realize they've just been mangled (lag or just plain blindness, I never miss 'YOU have been mangled for 50!' messages but that's just me). If the leader keeps an eye on health and directs combat movement, it greatly simplifies the jobs of other party members (though it adds to the leader's responsibilities). This command is the most often ignored. Many times it is because the person loses the message in the scrolling text and other times they just wand to get a couple more hits in. It is the party's job to be watching for the leader's commands and a lso the calls from other party members. Make sure you pay attention. If you think that you should get just one or two more hits in before stepping out DON'T. Your leader (if competent) has good reason to call you out. Step back immediately. If it is because you were getting low on health step back and sit down for 3 combat rounds at least. This gives the party's healers ample time to attend to you, and also makes the monster less likely to attack you when you rejoin combat. "MR?" (Mana Report): Just something that all casters should get in the habit of announcing. It allows the leader to effectively judge the relative 'power' the party has left, both in terms of healing and in terms of direct burn. A good leader always knows what the mana levels of party members is, and if he/she doesn't, ASK. "Rest": Pretty straightforward. The party is mangled and needs to camp out. Should only be mentioned when the party is not in any direct danger nor is sitting on somewhere that could become dangerous (ie: spawn point). Again, it is the leader's responsibility to find a safe place to call this. The Party Member Unless you are the leader of the party, it is your job to follow orders to the best of your abilities. Though that may sound like no fun, it truly can be cathartic to be 'The Tank' and know that you can and should throw yourself at the enemy. Or 'The Healer', who just watches health and makes sure no one goes negative. When a party member, your life is simplified greatly because you really have one or maybe two tasks to concentrate on. As long as everyone plays their part, the party will do very well. It is amazingly fun to be part of something larger; especially is that something larger is ripping through the beasts of the area like a scythe through wheat. Following a part: As long as your leader is competent, just relax and play the role you've been designated. Try not to do anything that would directly endanger the party, and use your skills to the best of your abilities. See the Grouping 101 guide for ideas and suggestions for each class. As a member, it is your duty to provide information to your party leader regarding your status. Casters, periodically report mana; warriors, mention that you are out of food and water, or that you need to rest. These things make the leaders job a little easier and are appreciated. Also, do not hesitate to inform the group about changing situations, including additional monsters, changes in health, or mana. It's never good to have additional monsters enter a fight, but it's worse if no one realizes they are there. Multiple Enemy Engagement Story time: Ok... your fetch has just announce that he/she has not one, not two, not three, but FOUR big yellow aggressive things (for example, Minotaurs). This is a bad situation, as your group has only taken one and occasionally two of the beasts. You can either panic and bug out, or you can try and fight the horrible odds. Let's follow an example party and see how they do against the monsters. This is told from Vogyd's perspective. Leader: Teffo, L11 Halfling Cleric (Jim) Party members: Zanith, L10 Human Paladin King, L10 Half Elf Bard Vogyd, L12 High Elf Magician (Drew) Adaemus, L11 Dwarf Warrior We had all rested up after a combat with 2 of the beasts, when we hear, "Oh... that's bad." from Zanith, our fetch. He had been scouting looking for a minotaur or two in the middle of the area where they spawn... when all of them spawned. He high-tailed it out of there with four minotaurs on him, and informed us as to our fate. "4 incoming." We briefly discussed our options, and then decided to try to smoke them. The Bard switched to a combat anthem, and we waited for the enemy to arrive. As soon as Zanith arrived, I dropped a Fire Shield on both him and Adaemus. With four enemies, that mana cost would easily be made up for in damage done to our tanks. Teffo began to heal Zanith, and then we began to melee the beasts. After a few seconds, we realized this was going to be close fight as both of our tanks were below 50% health, and I began searching for a monster with lowest hps. This would be the monster that was being attacked by our warriors. Most of the monsters were slightly damaged (from the Fire Shields) and eventually I found one at half life. I then burned it down the rest of the way. Teffo began to heal Zanith and Adaemus while fighting one of the monsters he had engaged long enough to draw its attack onto himself and away from the damaged warriors. King had also drawn one onto himself and resumed his attack on the next one targeted by the party. Quickly, the odds shift into our favor, as we have now a 5 vs. 3 advantage. Again, the warriors concentrate on one enemy, and I begin to fry the monster as it attempts to flee having dropped to about 20% of it's health total. While I am burning, the tanks are very low on hps, so Teffo backed out of melee to heal while the tanks switch to yet another enemy. After dispatching my runner, I step into combat to make sure that others have cover, as Zanith was near 20% (he backs out), and I take some hits from one of the mostly undamaged minotaurs (which quickly reduces my hps to half). Zanith is mostly restored by Teffo's spells and returns to combat. I back out, and dispatch another minotaur who had decided to try and leave the area (low on health) with a few spells. I am now extremely low on mana, so I save the rest in case of an emergency and step into melee range of the remaining beast. Adaemus and Zanith quickly maim the enemy and we cut it down from behind as it attempts to flee. Analysis: This little story demonstrates many key events in good party dynamics. Primarily, it shows that with good teamwork, a party can take out groups of dangerous (yellow!) monsters very effectively as long as certain rules are followed. Concentrating attacks on one enemy removes it from combat, lessening the damage done to the party as a whole. When engaging multiple enemies, make sure that they all are concentrated on players who can take the damage. Tanks. If a spellcaster becomes a target, the party is in big trouble as without the fire support bad things happen. Thus, in large combats, the spellcasters should stay out of combat as much as possible until things are less chaotic (ie: fewer enemies) and should NOT burn randomly. That is the surest way to draw an undamaged beast onto you. Bad mage. Spellcasters should endeavor to finish weaker monsters off quickly. This allows the warriors to get on to more important business. Also is much safer for the spellcaster (as mentioned in #2), as the monster is already really angry at the tanks who did most of the damage. Good communication. Zanith told the party what was going on well before bringing the enemies into range, allowing the party to have a good look at the situation before committing. Everyone responded to 'Back off' commands, and they also concentrated their attacks well on successive monsters. They all knew that I was finishing off the 'runners' so they could concentrate on the monsters in their face and not on the ones that would be coming back with friends after healing a bit. Overall, this fight was one of the best I have been in ever. Everyone did their part and there was very little confusion. Everyone knew what was happening and trusted the other members to do their part. I hope everyone gets a chance to experience this sort of coordinated action at some point in their EQ career. It's nearly indescribably cool. Pre-Fight Planning Any time the group is considering attacking a group of monsters, or is being attacked by a group, it is imperative that the group concentrate their attacks (as we saw above in the story). When the aggressor in a combat, the party has the advantage of pre-planning which of the monsters is the 'primary' target. There are a few logical ways to analyze the targeting order in a combat. Enemy spellcasters. These HAVE to die, or the party is hosed. Primarily, this is offensive casting enemies like wizards and necromancers. These are a parties worst nightmare in groups because sometimes you can't get to them and prevent them from casting spells. This also applies to those monsters with special attacks (stuns or drains and the like). They are also mush easier to take out because of their low hit points (hopefully!). Tactics: Have _your_ offensive caster take them out preemptively. Usually, starting a fight by having your caster burn on the enemy caster is effective because then the enemy caster is occupied. The only problem is that all of the enemy casters friends are on their way to kill your caster too. That's where the tanks step in front and occupy them. It's not too difficult to distract a mob if you hit it in the head with a sword. Thus, the fight is now divided, casters on casters, and tanks on tanks. Hopefully your tanks are taking out the enemies by concentrating their attacks. It is important at this stage to get a tank on the enemy spellcaster. Vital. Even having a tank with another enemy on his back while he attacks the caster is worth it. This is for one reason. Interruptions. If your tank can induce interrupts, and your caster is unaffected, the fight is over. The other caster is going to be eating sword and spells; that's never a good combination. An excellent tank for enemy spellcasters is a Monk. Lots of attacks means a higher chance of interruption. Also works with any higher level warriors types with dual wield or double attack (or both!). Weaklings. There are always a few lower level monsters in a group of enemies (at least you hope there are). These should be prime targets. Why? Because they have less health, and can be taken out quickly. Never underestimate the power of 2-3 green monsters will have on a fight with a blue one. The sheer quantity of hits can be deadly as well as the power of those attacks. Tactics: Either target them first, or have your spellcaster pick them off with concentrated fire. Damaged mobs. As mentioned previously and similarly to the Weaklings... the sooner they're dead, the less damage they'll do. Runners. This is the most dangerous type of enemy for a party in a dungeon. If one mob slips away after being damaged in combat the leader might as well call for a mass retreat while everyone can still make it out. In a dungeon,a damaged creature fleeing from combat will run deeper into the dungeon calling every monster it finds to attack the party that nearly killed it. If it does not call red creatures that would kill your party outright, it will call enough weaker creatures to overrun your party in a brutal fashion. This is called a train. You don't want it. Tactics: These creatures should be dealt with by the mages or one of the warriors if your party is lacking anyone who can cast burn. _Never_ let them go unless you want to see how ugly a dungeon can really get. Action. Attacking some creatures will lower your standing with other races and mobs. As Drew or the dwarven guards of Kaladim might tell you, killing Crag Aviaks will lower your faction standing with many groups. In general, if you con the creature (hit 'C' while it is targeted) and it says that it 'looks at you indifferently' you are best to leave it alone or you are going to get in trouble with some faction Group Leaders(Officers in Clan Skallagrim) Tips for Group Leaders (officers of Clan Skallagrim) Be a fair, conscientious leader. Usually, the leader should be nimble on the keyboard and keep the lines of communication open within the party. It is the leader's responsibility to make sure that there's an even rotation of looting. You can set up the loot sharing alphabetically, but don't count on everyone following it. It's easier to set up a rotation based on the order of the characters in your group window. Take a minute at the beginning to list the loot order. Now it's your responsibility to tell each individual when their turn to loot is, and who's next. This may seem like a bit of a burden, but it's really easy to do this, and your party members will appreciate it. Keep the Invite and Disband buttons handy. Be ready to target a character and Invite them in, even in the midst of a battle. You may come across someone who is having a hard time with a monster, and you want to help. If they ask for your assistance, join in and invite them into your group (even if you haven't formed on yet). This way, you'll make a friend, and if you're similarly leveled you'll share the experience. Zones. Currently, many times when the party crosses a zone, the grouping system loses track. Be prepared to disband and re-invite your party members after crossing a zone. This is likely on the list to get fixed during beta. Be aware of all your group's levels. They should be no more than 4 levels apart (although I'm not exactly sure where the breaks are for higher level characters). So a level 4 can group with a level 7 and still gain experience, but it would be better if all characters were within 1 or two levels. Just because you formed the party, doesn't mean you should guide the party towards monsters. If you're in a roaming pack, it is best to let the ranger types track your prey. But be careful if you have more than one ranger in your group - either choose one to lead, or have them take turns. It is not a good thing when more than one ranger brings back a high level creature and you have to battle them all at the same time. Just because you formed the group, doesn't necessarily make you the supreme being. Grouping in EverQuest is not a power struggle. If someone else has a better understanding of the area and local strategy, let them take the lead. Not everyone wants to be a group leader - it's a bit of a chore, but if you do a good job you'll gain respect. Furthermore, next time you're online you'll get messages from your former party members asking if they can join your group. The Leader Well... either you are one, you've been one, or you will be one. Regardless of how good or bad you are at playing EQ, it is likely that you will eventually be called upon to lead a party. If you are decent at it, you'll garner some respect and likely get to be leader more often. If you get the group smoked... well, let's just say that finding a group might be more of a problem than leading one. Experience makes a leader. The leader would have never gotten to that point if he had not made a few mistakes and learned from them along the way, though. Make sure if you do make a mistake to admit that you were wrong and try to make up for anything that may have happened as a result. The leader has many responsibilities, and actually doesn't have to be a front line combat type. I (Drew) play a Magician most often and have been designated leader quite often. I usually am no where near melee range (though I do mix it up as needed to save people who are hosed), and that helps me keep my mind clear while directing things. Just a personal preference, other leaders may have different experiences. The following items should be considered the leaders responsibilities: Finding the Enemies: Whether the party is a roving band, or is doing the tentacular thing, finding the enemies is the leaders prime responsibility. For roving bands, it's amazing how often the leader turns out to be the tallest member. Merely because it's easier to follow a tall leader who doesn't disappear into the weeds (*snicker* halflings *snicker*). Planning and Organization: One of the leader's jobs is to keep everyone organized and aware of the situation and their role in it. This is important both in and out of combat. Keep the party together and make sure everyone knows the score. A strong leader should be aware of your party member's abilities and limits. This includes a strong working knowledge of all of the spells and abilities of those members of your party. A good leader will direct the Bard to play the correct song for the situation, and have the Shaman cast specific spells to turn the odds, etc. If you are not very familiar with all of your member's skills, ask them and begin learning a little about classes aside from your own. The leader should be adept at directing actual attacks during combat, as well as indicating the order of combat for multiple enemies (see Multiple Enemy Engagement section below for ideas). This is especially important when dealing with dangerous monsters with special attacks, or those that have friends nearby. It also is important in planning escape routes should your party end up with more on their plate than they can handle. Loot order: Though this may seem trivial, it is vital that the leader keep a close eye on loot distribution. If a member is getting shorted, it's likely that he/she will depart for other places soon. Never good to lose a party member to those sorts of things. Autosplit (when working) takes the edge off of this problem but players still enjoy that birthday surprise type feeling of looting a mob. Even if you are not following an order just make sure everyone loots on now and then. A good leader will also keep their group hunting monsters that are both interesting and profitable. If you lead your group around killing things that you could have taken alone, people will bore quickly and leave the group to go fight by themselves. Group fighting is _the_ most efficient way to improve your character as well as being the most fun. A good leader will make sure this is extremely apparent. A bad leader can sour players' perception of grouping. Combat Coordination: This is where a good leader can make the difference between killing the enemies and getting the entire party killed. This is more of an art than a science and incorporates many skills. The main skill a good leader has is the ability to multi-task. This is because he/she must engage an enemy, while constantly surveying the situation for changes, and while directing other members of the party in actions. Often when I am leading a party I end up breaking my own rules of breaking off combat at 2 bubbles of health because my attention is rarely on my own hit points. Leading is very rewarding but also can be very risky. Your first priority is to keep you group together and alive and your second is to your own health. This does not mean you should be reckless, but good leaders will always take calculated risks for the party. A very important skill for any leader is to know when to fight and when to flee. As the leader in combat this is your most important duty. If you are with a good party they will not run until the leader calls for the run. This means they are placing their lives on your assessment of the situation. Do not expect to achieve this until you have proven yourself a strong leader. When the situation is getting ugly you are better to call the run earlier than later. Waiting can be fatal, and if you are going to escape a bad situation, you should attempt to reduce the 'downtime' the party will have recovering. Heck, if you called it too early, you can always make another attempt. While in larger combat, it is often good for a party leader to be familiar with and use the many 3rd person views to scan the area around the combat for possible dangers and to get a more 'removed', strategic view of combat. Some people prefer the overhead view, but I'm very much for the long distance shots of the combat, as they give ample viewing of the area around combat while still allowing a good view of combat. Respect: As a leader you must earn your group's respect. This is done by showing that you know what is going on are able to command the group to peak its efficiency. Respect will be gained in time through successful weathering of bad situations. It is in these situations that the leader is really put through their paces and when the group will become forever loyal or mistrusting and being ignoring commands. Don't ask people to do things that you would not do yourself. The battle described later in this document gained the leader some respect but if it had gone the other way he could have easily lost some of the loyalty of his group.
