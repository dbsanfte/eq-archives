Alyador is a constructed language. Or, rather, it is a language that I am in the process of constructing, purely for my own entertainment. It's still in a fairly early stage of development. It was previously called Mÿjador or Míjador, but I decided I wanted something a little nicer to my ear. The name is just one of the changes I have made recently.

Verbs

Verbs are regular at this time. Any irregularities are idiomatic. This isn't so much for the benefit of those who might be tempted to learn Alyador as it is sheer laziness on my part. Like everything else, it is subject to change.

There have been a lot of recent changes. For instance, the pluperfect as a separate form (-ad-) has been ditched in favor of a combination of past + perfect. The form that was pluperfect now represents a strong commitment to the truthfulness of the statement, as in "Olad valto _suwade_ nef!", "That guy is really kissing her/him!" (i.e., "I'm not imagining it!") . The negative of this ('Olad valto suwaddje nef') can mean either that there is no commitment to the truth (doubt) ('He wasn't really kissing her/him, he was kissing the back of his hand.') The new pluperfect form will be ga-al-. For example, "Peraxa gasuwale Yensari" ("Perasha had kissed Yensari"). This is also an example of how tenses/aspects/modes can be combined.

The infinitive form is the root + -al: ehal, 'to come'

The last element in most verb forms is the person marker: 1st, -a; 2nd, -o; 3rd, -e; 3rd-2, -efe. For example, ka dora, 'I speak'; fa gadora, 'we spoke'; do dordjo, 'you don't speak'. This is the only marking that is generally required for every verb.

Negation comes immediately before the person marker: fa ola, 'we go'; fa oldja, 'we don't go'. In those verbs where the verb stem ends in -dj, as in txudj- (txudjal, 'to prepare (to do something)'), an -a- is inserted between the stem and the negation: se gatxudjadje xexal, 'he didn't prepare to sweep'

Imperatives:
Odoro! Speak!
Odora! Let's Speak!
Odore! Let him/her/it/them speak!
Doru! Speak! (An alternate form, stronger than 'odoro' and perhaps high to low status)
Va dor aveyu! Please speak! (Idiomatic, used by those of a lower social status toward those of much higher station, relatively)
