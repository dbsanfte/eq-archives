TEAM EVIL PUMPKIN RACING

Gary's Lessons learned - so far

Rookie Indoctrination ("Pumpkin School")

My `95 Ducati 900SS/CRacer "Gogo" and I left at dawn Friday for the 3 hr drive to Brainerd International Raceway. 3 solid days of preparation, little sleep, and forgetting to eat left me 5lbs lighter and kind of dazed, but the slow buildup of adrenalin soon had me feeling ready to go. Suddenly, the cracked ribs didn't hurt anymore, the fractured thumb settled down to a dull ache, and the sunrise reflected on the Metrodome and skyline of Minneapolis was absolutely breathtaking! Was it Keiffer Sutherland who once uttered: "It's a good day to die."? Why that crossed my mind at that moment, I'll never know. Gogo was riding securely in the bed of my `69 Chevy C10 "Edgar", all the camping gear, leathers, etc. packed around her. I put the pedal down... 2.5 hrs later, after many miles of sleeping Minnesota lake country past beneath our wheels, we pulled into BIR at 7a.m. Just in time for registration and tech. inspection.

I rented a garage stall next to my Evil Pumpkin Racing teamates, unloaded the bike and the tools, and ran her through tech. Not bad, only I forgot to wire the oil galley plug, you know the one with the cylindrical mesh filter? Drilled and wired it in 10 minutes and got through tech. Next was "sound tech"... :( Those who have read my posts in the past know that my Termignoni High pipes set off car alarms on a regular basis. Sound tech measures your audio output at a distance of 20 inches, 45 degrees from the pipe outlet, for a maximum of 105db. I just knew I wasn't gonna pass. You pull up to the inspector, rev your motor to 1/2 redline, and raise your hand. I passed. Unbelievable! Must have something to do with the frequency, Kenneth >:-)

10 a.m. was classroom for an hour, followed by a ride around the track in a flatbed truck. Did I mention the adrenalin was building? Finally, after lunch, we suited up and went on-track in groups of 5 or 6 with an instructor. I was very fortunate here, as I was the only Ducati rider in the class. They assigned me my own personal instructor. (Thanks, Mark.) We went out at parade pace for a couple laps as they showed us the lines, then gradually picked up the pace. Then, all too soon the first session was over. The point of the exercise is to stay with your instructor, then ride ahead of him, and finally he releases you to ride your own pace. I felt really good out there. No problems keeping up, I wished he would go faster! Then during the second session, Mark pulled alongside, flashed me a thumbs up, and waved me on. "Hey!" thought I, they weren't gonna release us `till the 3rd session! COOL >:-) I proceeded to pick up speed down the straight, backed off a little before the notorious turn one. I know, I know. You're supposed to be WFO all the way through there, but I just let off momentarily to establish lean and line. It really is quite scary.

I improved my speed and line during the third session with no supervision, passing other groups of riders, YEEEHAA! Went into 10 really hot once and the rear finally slid. I held throttle and just straightened her up towards the wall, then leaned her back down the straight. That's O.K., I think I have a tire-iron back in the pits to pry my puckered ass from this seat >:-) The rear damping on the stock rear shock is still too stiff. Several times during hard braking into turns 3 and 9 it stuttered and skittered all over the place. One time, coming really hard into 9, it didn't happen. What the...? Oh, it's all the way off the ground. My first unintentional stoppie. cool. Now, let's smooth it out, shall we? So, I began braking a little earlier, a LOT smoother, and started going faster. When you aren't death-gripping the lever into the corner, you can look through and judge alot better, and carry more corner speed. I STILL couldn't hold it WFO through one! A couple times I really intended to, but got blocked by other riders even more timid. The last practice was over, they posted the grid for the "Rookie Race", and we'll carry on from here in Installment #2... (I gridded on POLE!)

It rained Saturday morning. Alot. Not only did it soak my tent, but the track was awash as well. Lots of riders went out to practice anyway, including my own Team Evil Pumpkin Racing mates, but I opted out. I don't have ANY real experience riding in the wet, having cut my moto-teeth in sunny Southern California, and the risk far outweighed the gain for me. Life has been far too exciting of late to invite disaster.

As race time approached, the rain let up and I began to hope. The CRA staff put trucks out on the track to dry out a racing line. At first they were splashing off HUGE amounts of standing water, which gradually decreased until it looked like we could have a race. I was gridded 7th row on the left, right next to a very intense young man on a (what else?) 600F2. I mean, talk about a "race face" ! Heavy mono-brow, knit fiercely over beady black snake-eyes. S-C-A-R-Y!!! >:(

On the warm-up lap, there were scattered puddles but these were easily missed. We went around without incident and regridded (next to "snake-eyes" again). The one minute board turned sideways, stirring up the hornets' nest around me. God, how they shriek! The flag dropped, and "snakeyes" slithered all over the place in a cloud of tire smoke, as Gogo and I launched with authority this time, moving into about 16th or so. Lots of bunching up at the entrance to one, I was surprised as I was prepared to enter MUCH faster than most riders in front of me. But I grabbed a handful of Brembo and watched it all sort out. Lotsa squids in this class. I know this sounds rather presumptious, coming from a rookie and all, but these guys were all over the place! I held my line through two, actually passing a couple of tangled calamari, and as we roared down on three I saw a major cluster- f*ck developing. Sure enough...one, two, three---FIVE riders down trying to occupy the apex at the same time. RED FLAG.

We waited about 15 minutes on the grid for the restart, seemed like days. Snakeyes was searching the ground for a dry spot to launch from, backing up and zigzagging around until he was way out of position. I thought the marshal would say something but no. Finally, they sent us out on another sighting lap, there was fluid (oil?) down on the outside of three which we avoided. We shuffled the grid to fill in for the crashed riders and the flag finally dropped. Another good start.

We carried good speed through one this time, probably 12th or so. I got stuffed in two by one rider, but got him back coming out of three. Gobbled up a few more in 4-9. Down the straight, however, I think 3 or 4 got back by towards the end there. And so it went. My lack of top end really hurts me here at Brainerd. I believe I would do much better at a tighter track. The next 4 laps are a blur. I was in a "zone", turning my fastest laps to date: 2'13s and 2'14s, very smooth and, for me, fast. There were places I could have pushed harder, but I've got a whole season for that. The fast guys, experts and some novices, were just plain GONE. WHITE FLAG-I didn't really want it to be over. Flying down the straight, I'd got a really good drive out of ten this time, I wasn't drafted past all the way down the straight.

But then...coming into one, I heard that now-familiar swarm `o bees sound on my right. Yeah, you guessed it, my old pal Snakeyes, pulling some last lap heroics trying to stuff me in the hairiest place on the track. He pulled an early apex and came drifting out WIDE, right into my line. Right into my front wheel. I moved left, he kept coming. Left some more, he's STILL drifting. Do I touch the brakes? No, I fear the front end will tuck. We're doing ~135 here and I have 3-2-1 NO inches of track left. Well, the grass looks flat for a long way out here-I've never tried motocross...

Did I mention it had rained?

I did about 200 yards of bronco-busting, tank slapping motocross. The fractured right thumb just wouldn't obey the brains command to hit the kill switch. My feet were thrown off the pegs right away, knees clamped to the tank, so the rear brake was out of reach. This wet grass wasn't slowing me down at all. Every bump had my throttle hand death-grip twisting the wick momentarily...no help at all. I began to think I would save it, as all I could see in front of me was a vast expanse of flat, grassy field. Then suddenly the tall grass parted to reveal a 4 foot drop into a drainage ditch! SPLAAAASSSHHH!!!

I landed on my head, went end-over-end a few times, and landed in the bushes. I couldn't breathe, my cracked ribcage was in a vice. I was croaking "AAaagh, aaargh, aaack" when I heard the slap slap slap of corner worker Nikes across the track. A head leaned in, upside down in my field of view through a broken face shield. "Are you O.K.?" Several sarcastic replys cycled through my tortured mind in a millisecond. Conjuring up the best of them, I uttered "UUnngickhh..cack!" or something to that effect. "Can you move your arms or legs for me?" I wiggled fingers, toes, hands, feet, and on down through the checklist. Nothing broken? Seems not. I put out my arm for a pull up, he obliged. Oh, oh, down again. Wobbly knees, don't ya know. Second try, up and hobbling back towards the track. Team mate Nina, also on a Duc, is riding by on the cool down lap. I give her a thumbs-up, she just shakes her head.

HEY! WHERE'S THE BIKE!? I finally ask. Oh, there it is. Tank askew, doesn't look damaged though. Windscreen broken, clipons bent (my knees, no doubt), can't tell much more as they hustle me across to the corner station. They won't let me take this damn, stifling helmet off! Can't breathe! Oh, well. Think I'd better sit down for awhile. Tomorrow: Thanks to CRA staff/corner workers/infirmary...lessons learned.

O.K. class. What did we learn from all this?

First of all, I had an INCREDIBLE time! The racetrack is the ONLY place to ride a Ducati the way it is designed to be ridden. I explored some limits out there, mostly my own. The few times I got the rear to slide controllably were a revelation! I scraped through my toe sliders and most of the way through the toes of my new race boots. Lesson: Add "Feet back on pegs." to my pre-corner mental checklist.

I also learned to have faith that the bikes' limits are probably higher than mine. Every time I asked for more lean, I got it. Every time I went into a corner at what I judged to be too hot, just leaning and rolling on the throttle got us through it, with room to spare. And the TORQUE! I love exiting corners at full throttle in second and having the front wheel lift off! I've done lots of these things to some extent on the street, but when you remove the law-anxiety factor it becomes so much more FUN!! >:)

What I didn't like: very little. I guess if I have to pick something it would be the fact that I'm competing against guys who don't have near the financial or emotional investment at risk that I do. I LOVE my "Gogo". She is my mechanical soul-mate. It really pains me to see her damaged like this. But the battle scars are well-earned, and it's all repairable. As for the actual competition, if CRA ever gets a BOTT-type class going where I'm racing against similar machines, maybe... but I won't race against the 600's again. When I read everything I wrote about this experience, I notice very little about the sheer thrill of competition. It's all about finding a faster way around the track. It's about improving MY riding technique and my lap times. It's about getting frus- trated when other riders come between me and the perfect racing line.

I think my future involvement will consist of paying the $40 fee for a whole weekends worth of practice, and then watching the other folks go out and beat up on each other in the races. This will probably piss off some racing purists out there, but if it does, I'll bet you make alot more money than I do and didn't have to work 2 full-time jobs for 4 months to come up with an adequate down payment on your Ducati. I'm also married. I didn't see a whole lot of married racers out there. Oh, there were some, but the majority were single and didn't have a spouse to answer to. As much as I have invested in the bike, my wife has WAY more invested in me. I have to respect the fact she doesn't share my belief that I am indestructible.

I have to thank the CRA corner workers for their prompt action getting me and Gogo picked up after our fall. Also, the infirmary staff for cracking dumb jokes and keeping my spirits up right after the incident. (Oh NO! DON'T say CRASH!!!) And I should even thank ol'"Snakeyes" for coming up to me afterwards and apologizing so sincerely. I couldn't even yell at him, because I can relate to that level of intensity. There was nothing but one more place in the middle of the pack to gain from passing me like that, but in the "red haze of battle" sometimes that's the only thing that matters. So I told him: "Hey, I guess that's racin'."...

Gary Charpentier
