BIR - Turn 5

5/12/1999 (My birthday no less)

Hi to all Evil Pumpkin fans…

This is the first installment of what I hope will be a long and prosperous addition to the EPR site.

First a little back ground on myself, (DoughBoyRacer@hotmail.com)

Ever since my father took me out for rides on his 350 Honda, when my mother said it was ok, after I could prove to her that I could touch the pillion pegs (Little did she know that it was one at a time, sorry Mom). I would beg to go for rides with him every chance I could, and dream of one day riding it myself. Something happened one day to cause my Dad to get rid of the bike and swear them off forever. The only chance for me to ride came at my cousin’s place outside of town (they had a dirt track in their back yard.) I would beg to go out there just to get a chance to ride the little 50 Honda mini trail. More often than not they would be out of gas or the bikes would be broken down. That was my only riding that I would do until three years ago, except for the tiny amount that I got to do in the empty lot across the street at home (thank you Lee A, Jessica, and Josh.)

Now on to the recent past, Dec 1995, the dream becomes reality, I purchase a CBR600F3 from a friend, I am now a sport bike owner. Now the trick of learning on the current king of the 600 super sport bikes. I felt that I was at an advantage in this category for I was a bit older than the rest of the squids that I was going to be riding with and felt I could keep myself under control for the most part. To make a long story not as long I did just fine in the learning department with help from my friends (thanks Mike, John, Ben, Corey, and Jim.) The only trouble I had was on the way home from Road America and the super bike races I got caught by one of the most common mistakes, looking back to see who was coming behind entering a corner…in the sand and down I went. Round one in the books.

I decided to take the Team Suzuki Endurance class with the group I ride with the next summer. Before that came to pass, all but Jim and I had dropped out for one reason or another. We went and had a great time, I learned a lot and became a much better rider. This first taste of on track experience got me going, the best time you can have on your bike. I rode the rest of the season practicing what I had learned, loosing the group I rode with, by them loosing interest in the sport. I still don’t know how, but girls will do that to you at their age I guess, which is better than the other way of loosing them to the sport. The ones that remained wanted to learn and improve. It was decided that we would sign up for the Keith Code School at Road America. We were very excited! Five of us registered to take the school, with Jim in the second level.

We also learned of the Team Absolute Riders Practice at the Dakota County Technical College ( DCTC ) track that you have probably read about in Rich’s section (if not, do so.) Jim and I decided to give it a go. Corey wanted to give it a try, but would not be riding for fear of downing his SJR replica Honda CBR600F3 ’96 edition. So off to the track we went, paid our 50.00 and got ready to go. I went out and had myself a good time leaning and learning, and then dragging my knees. Just as I started to have way too much fun I decided to pit before I wrecked. Jim went by as I took off my helmet, the next thing I knew everyone was looking to see who went down. Yep, GSX-R750, turn one, looked like Jim, rider up and it was Jim. One totaled bike and unhappy wife later we had one less to go to the Keith Code School. That ended bumping us out of the discount, which gave excuse to others to bail out and one thing lead to another, we weren’t going. L

Next I found myself on the Internet looking at the CRA web page. I decided to get involved as a corner worker to learn more about the possibilities of racing. Well, I got in touch with the staff and signed up with my friend Corey to corner work the CRA season. I also found this site and got in touch with Rich and the gang.

Now to the present, I bought Rich’s old race bike, a GS500E, to get into racing and learn on the best class for a beginner. Little did I know that I would learn a lot about racing prep and the trials of not being ready for the start of the season. All due to not having the money to get the bike in my hands until a week before the season due to a change in jobs (not having one for three months, Thanks Ag-Chem Inc.) I finally got my tax check back so I could pay Rich for the bike. I took the bike out to the practice at Dakota Co. the Monday before race weekend to get to know the bike and such (remember I have only been riding three years and all on the same bike.) Just as I thought everything was going to go as planned, I called Bill (chief of tech for the CRA) over to have a once over of the bike. This way I would have time to fix any shortcomings that had been overlooked in the past that would cause a delay at the track on Friday. As it turns out there was nothing technically wrong with the set up but he did point out that I should clean up the "old" oil that was on the motor so I could detect an oil leak should one turn up. Little did he know this had come from a new oil leak in the base gasket. Needles to say, my night was over. I talked to John Ryti as to what I should do, he said, "Ah…just pull the engine and bring it over, I can take care of it.". Done deal, engine pulled, torn apart, fixed, and replaced in time to get to the track. One hurdle met.

Friday morning arrives early and off to the riders meeting. Next, off to our pit for a bit of breakfast and chat with the teammates. As 10:00 rolled around I ran off to the new rider meeting to learn more about what to do or, really what not to do. But first we got our test from the class room session returned and found out that we had one of the worst classes in CRA history. Next, a little bit about flags and on track procedure to get us rolling, all of which, as a corner worker, I already knew. After the briefing in the racers lounge we took to the track in the back of pick-up trucks to tour the track with some expert racers to point out a few things. As happens every new rider class tour it was ended with the corner workers at lunch yelling "give me an S" "…Q" "…U" "…I" "…D" SQUID, SQUID, SQUID… Well, I had been a part of giving that cheer so I knew it was coming.

Now on to the part of the day we had all been waiting for, the on track experience following an expert rider. All goes well as nobody goes down the first session. After that while we waited for our next chance to get out on the track I notice a dirty spot on my boot but think nothing of it at the time, big mistake. Out on the track for our next session I find it increasingly hard to shift without my boot slipping on the lever. Not wanting to tack my attention from the track, I carry on. When the session ends and we head for the pits I look down to see what I had been hopping would not be the case, a boot misted with, you guessed… oil. It seems that the head gasket did not reseal properly, end of track time for the day. As the day rolls on and the number of "newbies" falling down goes through the roof ( 14 in all ), Marc calls a meeting to inform us that there would be no new rider race and that we would not be let out on the track any more that day. He was trying to save some of us for warmer weather so that we could get our racing license. Good news to me due to my troubles. That meant that I had the rest of the night and the next morning to get a fix for my leak. Saturday morning arrives cool and clear but with temperatures rising, good news for the new riders as Marc has decided to try to get the race in just before lunch.

In trying to fix my problem we had removed the air box and carburetors and had been contemplating taking the motor out completely, to get it ready to be fixed correctly. After deciding that was more than I wanted to get into at the track, Corey and I decide to try a simple fix, re-torque the bolts. We put the bike back together and head out for a practice session with the "slow" (relative term) group to see how we had done. Pull out on to the track as the bike begins to sputter. I limp it around and finally had to pull off in turn 5. I got the bike going again only to see a full track with no breaks in sight. To my surprise the corner captain decided to give it a shot but first gave me a few words of wisdom (I am not sure if he was doing it because I was a pumpkin or because in the heat of the moment you can easily forget.) He tapped me on the back and said "remember cold, wet, sandy tires, take it easy when you get back on and even in turn 6." He was unaware that I had worked all last year as a corner worker and had learned that lesson at the expense of other riders. Anyway, words well spoken and I appreciated them greatly. Well back to getting back on, as I waited and waited for a space to get back out I saw what was the biggest gap for a while so perked up my attention and waited for the signal. As I waited, the gap seemed to get smaller than I would like for my first attempt at getting back on the track from the grass. With this in mind it is easy to predict what happens next. As I head for the tar knowing I don’t have as much time as I would like, I roll on the throttle a little too firmly, only to have the back end come out and start around on me quickly. I have had this happen before so I don’t get too excited but can just imagine what the captain and his crew where thinking, "Squid." I never looked back to see because it was all I could do to limp back to the pits. Once there the job of trying to figure out what had gone wrong commenced.

I started by asking those in the know. I said it was like a fuel flow problem, the response I got was, "it’s all done with vacuums so put it on prime and see if that helps." The next chance to try would be the warm up lap of the newbie "race". I made it out on the track and everything seemed fine. As I was about to pass a slower type bike, my bike sputtered. So I pull in behind so as not to interrupt his day, and try to get around later. That’s not going to happen as the bike completely dies going into turn 4. Well I guess it’s back to pulling apart the beast I said to Corey. As we struggled with that, I decided to take some time to relax and give Corey a break. Tearing into an engine was not what he wanted to do all weekend, nor I, and there would be no more track time for us anyway. In the mean time Corey heard over the PA while I was "out of the office" that there was a New rider meeting called, so off I went. It seems that we were not done getting lectured too as someone in the second wave of the race jumped the start causing the rest of the field to do the same… not good. Well after that was all straitened out by Marc, he handed out the new rider licenses and congratulated us for getting through it and low and behold I got one with my name on it.

Getting back to the bike we started to pull it apart and as the air box was removed I spotted the trouble, the "T" between the carburetors was up not down and pinching the fuel line that ran into it. Like I thought, a fuel flow problem. With that flipped over the thing ran like a top again. Some things are better screwed up sooner than later, when it really matters.

On to Sunday to try to get in some laps and see if the oil thing had stopped. No such luck so I pulled in and we packed it in for the weekend. We spent the rest of the day running around helping Rich, Todd P. and others while putting some good miles on the scooter (thanks Rich.)

I left out a good portion of the events getting to the track but that would only drag it out even longer and if you haven’t quit reading this by now.

I have started to get the motor rebuilt with the help of John, so hopefully we can avoid this sort of thing next race weekend. This time a broken motor, hopefully next time not a broken bike. Wish me luck, and I hope to see you at the track. Good riding and keep the rubber side down…
