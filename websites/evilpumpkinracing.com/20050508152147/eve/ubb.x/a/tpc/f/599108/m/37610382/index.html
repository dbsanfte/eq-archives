Tate Guckin #256E

BIR, April 30 - May 2, 2004

As a person goes through life, experience generally creates a continually expanding reservoir of knowledge and wisdom. Experience also makes a person realize how much they don’t know, which sparks a real desire to learn. A cycle of learning, realization, and more learning repeats and expands as we get older. Over time, an understanding of people, places, events and best practices emerges that can have a profoundly positive effect on a person’s behavior (tolerance, good will, etc.) and technical performance (techniques, processes, etc). But there are, of course, exceptions – George W, Mike Tyson, and the Evil Pumpkin/JenSpeed Racing Team, to name just a few. I can’t pick on George W, since I don’t want my picture to suddenly appear in a deck of cards, and I’d better leave Mike alone, for obvious reasons. So, I’ll just focus on the loosely assembled group of social misfits known as the JenSpeed Racing Team.

My JenSpeed Racing Teamates

Training to Race

We had already learned the basic lesson of "We are too slow" during previous years of racing motorcycles. Watching faster racers on the same equipment triggered the realization that we need to actually learn something more about how to race motorcycles if we are ever going to be any faster. Sure, some people would tell you that you just have to "put your head down and ride harder", but thankfully after a couple years of watching Gary McCoy in MotoGP, I learned there might be more to it than that.

Everyone’s hero-Gary McCoy

with his head down

So when Commander "Sponge Bob" Jensen suggested that we all attend the Kevin Schwantz Suzuki School at Road Atlanta, a JenSpeed contingent quickly sold off any worldly possessions that weren’t directly related to motorcycles in an emergency fund-raiser and set out for an assault on Road Atlanta. "I’m going to learn how to go faster," I told myself.

Our long-time friend, Kevin Schwantz

It was absolutely huge fun at the school. I’ll never forgot following Kevin Schwantz around Road Atlanta for a few laps, with Jamie James following me (and they were nice enough not to let me actually hear them laughing at me!). The instructors were very approachable, and spent a good bit of time on a variety of racing techniques and topics. And what did I learn? I have to learn a lot more to go any faster. Two days of instruction is only enough time to teach me what I need to work on. For example:

  • Downshifting while braking – The instructors were able to smoothly drop 3 gears in about 2 seconds, while under hard braking. Parking lot practice sessions pretty much proved that I’m a ham-fisted spaz at the controls. But I’m going to continue practicing to learn Jamie James’ technique of "jus a blitle sliply mrchf clutch ta gatr slippin, steprm dn tyrng n blp n smooooth as buda". His southern drawl might sound a bit odd to us Yanks, but I got his point.
  • Lines through the turns – Apparently I ride around the track like most soccer moms drive mini-vans: weaving randomly, erratically timed braking, etc. But there is really an amazing amount of information to try to keep conscious of. I found myself unable to accurately be thinking about brake points, turn in markers, apex markers, exit points, etc. Oh, and be sure to be looking way ahead on the track while simultaneously trying to finding that 2nd skid mark on the track after the tar strip that looks like a goat before turn 1 to use as a turn in point, the apex marker is … It’s obvious to me that to be a fast racer, it takes more mental abilities than anything else. The cross street at the end of my road (which I refer to as "turn 1") will be a great place to practice race lines while looking ahead since it’s a popular hiding spot for the local police.

An approximation of my driving focus while racing

 
  • Relax – I consider myself to be an expert at relaxation, but it’s a constant battle on the track to stay relaxed. My arms lock up, I squeeze the hand grips waaaay too tightly, and the bike does not turn well at all when I ride like that. I wonder if there’s a chemical solution I could employ to loosen up? I’ll have to search through my stockpile of pain medications…

Perhaps I can create a "relaxation tonic"?

Training to Crash

Dedicated as we are to our race team obligations, all of us attended the next opportunity to hone our motorcycle skills at the JenSpeed Training Weekend, conducted at the secret training facility in southern Minnesota. Another opportunity to learn! Unfortunately, without any real adult supervision, that turned into a free-for-all race of dirt bike double jumps, motocross, wheelies, pistol marksmanship, and – yes – crashes. Dr. Dave managed to mess up his "good" shoulder (if you can really call any of his parts ‘good’). Bruce managed to drive over himself with a go-cart and really mess up one of his arms (you don’t suppose the Tequila had anything to do with it, do you Bruce?). I pulled a groin muscle trying to not crash during my huge skid in the "through the garage" portion of our SuperMotard race on XR100’s, and my KTM got planted in the mud 4 times during the semi final round of the Multi-Disciplinary Race (thanks Dave and Brian). Now, I know you’re thinking that thi s doesn’t sound like behavior of learned, worldly men. But you have to realize that we have actually improved our behavior at the training events. We did not get a visit from 2 Deputy Sheriffs and 1 Police Officer this time. I haven’t even been referred to as ‘immature’ by anyone other than women in years (e.g., ‘come on, no one else will ever see these pictures!’).

Racing

With one race school, and one JenSpeed Training event under my belt I was now ready for the first CRA race of the year. I also had new bodywork on my bike to help with the high speed drafting down BIR’s front straight. However, despite years of racing experience teaching me NOT to ignore any mechanical anomaly with the bike, I somehow consciously suppressed the facts that 1) there was a puddle of brake fluid on my garage floor, and 2) my bodywork looked awfully close to my front tire. Sure enough, I spend a good portion of Friday’s practice fixing my rear brake caliper and moving the bodywork mounts to prevent the front tire from rubbing on the lower faring during hard braking. It was really stupid not to address those issues earlier. With the bike now ready for Saturday’s races, I was ready.

Slow. That basically sums up my racing. I couldn’t make the bike turn well. I wasn’t looking ahead. I was not downshifting smoothly while braking. During the Lightweight Supersport race I was running about 5 seconds per lap slower than last year’s pace. And as if I wasn’t having enough problems, the lone Ducati rider in our class ("Crazy Todd") must have been poised off on the side of the track, laying in wait for a victim. Upon seeing me coming towards Turn 7, he pounced like a lion on the already weakened gazelle. At just about the apex of the turn, I caught a glimpse of the front wheel of my attacker coming from off the track right at me. He hit me but somehow I didn’t crash! Could it be that I actually learned something from the KSSS during the discussion about managing panic? A couple of laps later, I had the convenient excuse of my clip-on handlebar pulling loose, so I couldn’t finish the race (more managed panic there!). It was probably just as well that I didn’t finish, since my biggest accomplishments thus far in the race were successfully managing panic situations – and how long could that possibly hold out?

Back in the pits, Cdr. Sponge Bob thought Todd hitting me was hilarious from his vantage point immediately behind me, and after the race simply said "Now you know what I was dealing with all last year!" The only damage from Todd hitting me was a bruise on my leg. Nonetheless, I could not let his behavior go unpunished, despite his apology. I tend to favor the more unconventional forms of payback. Hmmm…I actually have an idea already for Mr. Crazy…

Don’t mess with me, Todd!

When I checked my clip-on, it wasn’t all that loose! I was just pulling on the bars so hard I pulled it towards me. That clued me in to my basic problem of not being relaxed and loose on the cycle. Then Dr. Dave came limping back into the pits. Apparently he’d crashed – again. And injured his shoulder – again. And has some lame excuse about it not being his fault – again. Someone needs to help him out by sending him some new excuses. And Vioxx. Send Vioxx. At least I wasn’t the only one not learning.

Rich and Paul were riding quite fast. Paul is always fast because he is nuts, but what the Hell got into Rich? My suspicions were verified when I carefully inspected "The Great Pumpkin’s" motorcycle. Most of us have a piece of duct tape on our tanks with our grid position written on it, but Rich has something quite different on his tank:

Seems like this is outside the Supersport rules to me. I’m going to protest that sneaky Pumpkin at the next race!

My only other race of the weekend was the 17-lap trophy dash. Still in pursuit of learning to race a motorcycle better, I spent all my attention on trying to relax my arms and my grip on the bars, and on trying to look ahead. I think I finished last place during the race, but that wasn’t the point of those 17 laps. I had to practice relaxing while traveling at 100+ MPH. I had limited success – approximately half my turns were done correctly. The good news is that during those turns that I was properly relaxed, I was going faster. Generally, the RPMs were 200 to 500 higher at corner exits than they were when I was rigid. I need to continue to practice this until it becomes natural for me.

Going downhill?

I’m starting to suspect that I am not getting smarter like I ought to be, and possibly the other members of the team are similarly afflicted. We do dumb things, and we’re getting better at it. Previous learning is somehow suppressed, and our social behaviors continue to deteriorate. Dr. Dave admits that maybe he just "hates all people", and he has the means to implement his "Bacillus cloud" plan.

I’m forgetting several important race lessons previously learned (which is affecting my GI tract); Bruce continues to crash and injure himself in odd ways; Rich has taken to abusing the laws of physics in order to go fast; Billy stopped bringing much-needed pit babes to the races in his "shaggin’ wagon" so we have no one to stare at creepily; and Paul … well … Paul is doomed. Although he thinks he’s doing well, he’s been hanging around us for a couple years now. Sure, at last muster his was the only wife still present, plus he has a job, and he’s pretty fast on a motorcycle. But let’s be realistic here – what will happen to all his successes if he chooses to continue to hang around the rest of us?
