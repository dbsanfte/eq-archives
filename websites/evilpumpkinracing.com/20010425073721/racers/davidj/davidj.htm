Tales of Fire and Metal Shavings

My friend Tate and I have been Pumpkin supplicants for a year now (i.e. we hang out with them), and Rich suggested we might get into this prestigious group if race reports were written. Here it comes!

Last month's race was mostly a bunch of cursing at carburetors and camshafts. This month's was similar. The main problem is some elusive oiling malady that causes #2 exhaust cam bearing to become assorted metal filings. I had rushed to get the engine back together after the first race of the year, and had hoped I had solved the problem. I made 3 laps in practice, took it apart, and found the bearing surface had, once again, fled to the nether regions of my oil pan in tiny bits. Now, one must understand that there are just so many races in a lifetime, and I was not about to be eclipsed from this one by a mere engine destruct. I called former Pumpkin Chris Morken, who has lost his way (i.e. quit racing) and offered to buy his race bike….delivered. One giant sack of money later, I was racerboy again. Pumpkin associate Ned gets credit for the midnight delivery. Thanks, dude!

Tate and I got up early to prepare the bike for Saturday practice. I bustled about while everyone else rearranged our pit to resemble a REAL team effort. The effect was stupendous, of course. We put master tuner Steve to work pushing a broom, which seemed harmless enough at the time. When practice time came, my newly acquired bike would not make it down pit road. I hobbled it back to the barn and began the wondering process. We fixed a bunch of obvious problems, and it still misfired above 8,000 rpm. Just as practice was ending, Tate says, "is it getting enough air"? My brain box lit up. I knew what the problem was. The fastidious Steve had helpfully placed a rag in my airbox to keep out the dust he was sweeping up. I saw him do it, but somehow it didn't register. Lesson: Rags make poor air filters.

Now I have a dilemma. I have a racebike I have never ridden, with 1-year-old stale racing tires, and the next time out will be a real race. Ultralight Supersport. Nothing to do but go. When the flag dropped, I got a good launch and was first into turn one. I was astounded. At about turn 3, it started to rain. Lovely. I haven't raced in rain very much. Somehow, I stayed on the track and took third.

On Sunday, I practiced every possible moment. When the Ultralight Superbike race came I was pumped! I started well, but got into a 120-mph traffic jam in turn 2. After 2 laps of wrangling, I was buzzing along a few inches off maniac Tate's rear tire, in about 4th place. Suddenly in turn 3, I lost power. I coasted to a stop at the turn 4 corner worker's station. "Out of gas?" they giggled. I looked down and pointed to the stream of high-energy plasma and melted ignition coil sauce exiting my engine cover. It would have been a long push back to the pits but for my friend "Trophyboy" Shane Keavenly and his truck, who were conveniently sitting there. It turns out the ignition rotor bolt loosened, the rotor went a little eccentric, and got personal with the pickup coils. This arc-welded a big piece of the aluminum rotor, turned the coils molten, then caused the bolt to shear off in the crankshaft, taking the locating pin with it. Inside the ignition cover, it must have looked like a blender full of really, really hot margarita makings. Thus ended my glorious racing weekend, since I had neglected to pack a machine shop. Can't wait 'til next time.

Carnage 0, Me 1 ( July 16th-18th )

Gadzooks! I actually made it through the whole weekend without blowing anything up! (It’s a first for this season). The whole weekend went very well, and I think I had more fun than I was supposed to.

The first good news was that the practice sessions were changed by CRA so that the Ultralights and Lightweights were not grouped with the 600 and 750 novices. This is a great boon to mankind, because those bikes strafe past you on the straight between turns ten and one, and then we (the little fish) end up passing them in the tight infield. Then you do it again. And again. ARRRGH! You can’t enjoy dicing with your fellows when you ‘re worried about some heavyweight running up your tailpipe. Now it’s fixed. FIXED! I actually had time to figure some things out, and got used to the idea that tire sliding is OK.

The races on Saturday were awesome. There is a group of 4 riders that seem to contend for the lead in every race this season. Besides the lucky # 242 (me) who seems to have found mechanical bliss, there is fellow Pumpkin Tate, Rob (a friendly competitor) and #118, who is …….grumpy. I ended up 4th in the Supersport Sprint race with these guys, due mostly to the fact that I went up on the red striped thingy in turn 4 and then did a little dirt tracking before rejoining the fray. Lesson: When you ride off the track, it slows you WAY down. Also, you may need to change your racing shorts. It’s a 6 lap race, so there is no time to be bungling around in the grass. The Trophy Dash, a 14 lap race, is supposed to be more “relaxed”. In racer lingo, the term “relaxed” here means “just like a sprint race, only longer”. It was INTENSE! The four of us each had the lead for several laps. I was fortunate enough to take the lead late in the race, and scraped off Tate and Rob on some lapped traffic. Before they could close the gap, it was checker time. I have my first first. The big lesson this weekend: Getting comfortable with the bike sliding and wobbling and headshaking through corners, and not being intimidated by aggressive grumpuses ( the plural form of grumpus) bumping you in the turns. It’s definitely not commuting or joyriding with Betty Lou on the back. New mindset. Grrrr!

Star for a Day

Jason Pridmore's Star School was my chance to ride the "big bike" (my street-going TL 1000S) on the Brainerd racetrack, and get some race-learnin' from the veteran Jason. I have had the TL on the track at Road America for the CLASS school, taught by Jason's famous Dad Reg, and it was just more fun than the human brain should be allowed to have. I was really looking forward to riding this power wagon on the track where I race my GS 500. Observation on lap 1: The TL is DEFINITELY not like the GS 500. Thwacking the throttle open on corner exits gets you a leaned-over, tire-sliding wheelie. I think I swallowed my tongue when that happened. The whole morning was spent erasing GS 500 files from my track brain so that I could live.

The school itself was great. Like Rego's school, it is split into "fast" and "otherwise" groups, who alternate classroom and track sessions. Jason's "fast" group is more race-oriented than the CLASS school, and there is much discussion of race strategy, lines through corners, riding techniques, and other squidly stuff. Our class was blessed with other first class instructors, notably Lance Holst, motojournalist and cycle knowledgebank, and Josh Hayes, the wonderdude who took 4th in the AMA 600 race the previous day on a 2nd tier (not factory sponsored) machine. These guys all do things differently, and we were privy to their discussions of what worked and didn't work for them on MY track. Very interesting. The icing on that cake was that I got to watch them do what they said live the day before, then saw it again on the Speedvision broadcast of the race.

So what do you learn there? It's different for everyone. Bring your questions and ASK THEM. Before every classroom session, I was able to walk right up to Jason, and ask him about my latest quandary. I got mostly very practical answers, but some of the answers can be quite advanced (I thought). ME: "How do I keep from running wide if I'm too hot into a tight, greater than 90 degree corner, like turn 3?" HIM: "You can slip the clutch and let the revs come up so you slide the rear until it points you up-track" ME: "Uh….sure. I'll go practice that". Most of the advice is more do-able by mortal men, and I found Jason and all the instructors very approachable.

Bottom Line: The Star school is like Reg's CLASS, but more race-oriented in the fast section. Jason travels with his own handpicked instructors, unlike Keith Code, who hires some local track heroes to fill in. The Star School gives you 20 minutes ON, 20 minutes Off the track for the whole day, while the Suzuki Team Hammer school splits track sessions with other groups so you get less time. For the money, this can't be beat.

 

It's the little things.....

May 5-7, 2000

The race weekend was shaping up nicely at Brainerd.  It was perfect racing weather, the bike was running well, and I felt pretty good, except being a little rusty from all that laying around during winter.  Friday practice was going well.  I had been concerned about my tires (Michelin Pilot Race) because they looked funny on the too-small rims (I use the 150-size rear on the stock bicycle-size GS 500 rim).  The Metzler 150's I had been using did not seem to be so distorted, and indeed I used to get the tires cooked all the way to the edges on front and back.  The new Michelin rear was scrunched together by the rim and the edge was just about parallel to the sidewall. It looked like the only way I could get to the edge of that would be during a crash. 

My impressions were confirmed by the first laps around the track.  In the pits, it was clear I was getting all the way to the edge in front, but was only getting tire crumblies (a tire industry technical term) about an inch away from the edge of the rear. Despite that, the bike handled better than it did with the Metzlers, so I just went with it.  

Now the only thing wrong with my cutting edge race machine was the accursed rubber grip on the throttle.  Back in the gloom of winter, Pumpkin acolyte Clint (who was helping me put this beast together) had slorked the grip on using some contact cement.  Same stuff we always used, I thought.  This time, however, it had somehow mysteriously changed into the non-hardening mucous from hell.  As I made my way around the track, the grip slowly slipped further and further off of the bar.  Stupid thing!   So I got back to the pits, attacked the goo with the magic of chemistry (brake cleaner), and spent half an hour cleaning the grip and bar.  Then I safety wired the grip onto the bar. 

Hah! ..... No good.  It slipped again in the next practice.  The evil goo had infested the rubber and made it like Teflon?.  I cleaned it AGAIN, and found some heavier wire (which can be twisted  tighter) and put about 5 loops around the rubber demon.  It was approximately at this time that a new variable entered the picture.  Todd, the Pumpkin who rides an R1 in the unlimited class (everyone's hero) had brought along some friends.  One of these was a 17 year-old bit 'o crumpet named Brandy who was wearing a rather vexatious tube top.  Very topographical.  Not that I noticed.  However, as I was completing my repairs, this critter and some others decided it would be fun to have a tube-top pulling, ice down the shirt, wet splashy water fight thingy of the sort you normally have to get on pay-per-view.  Not that I noticed.  Shortly after that was the call to grid for the Supersport race.  I suited up, and off I went to the gate.

Suddenly I noticed something.  It was pain.  Right there in the all important throttle hand.  SOMEHOW I had neglected to clip and bend a couple of the safety wires around the grip, and now there were wires poking holes in my glove. No time to fix it.  Gotta' go now.  So I put my hand in a safe spot, and resolved to keep it there for the whole race.  I managed to do that for almost the whole warm-up lap.  So the flag waves, and off we go!  I got a good start, and diced with two other riders for the lead.  Despite the constant poking I was getting I managed to pass a packet of riders on the brakes going into turn 9, and I never saw them again.  I placed first in that race, probably because I was in a hurry to get back to the pits.  For some reason.

The next race was the Trophy Dash.  This is a relatively grueling 17-lap affair.  I was up for it though.  The flag dropped, I got a good start and horned my way into the lead by lap two.  Everything was going great.  But wait! What the hell is this???  The grip was oozing off again, due to the heat off my hot little mitts and also because this would be THE WORST POSSIBLE TIME for it to do that.  So now I am frantically trying to stuff the sumbich back on between every corner, but the slime only works in one direction (laws of physics, where are you??)  I did this for about 8 laps.  Finally, the thing is about 3/4 of the way off.  I come up to turn 4 (sharp left) and do my usual thing.  Suddenly I realize that the flange part of the grip is stuck on my glove and I am steaming into this corner with the throttle on!!  No way I am going to make this, so I stand it up, do a bucky over the rumble strip, and head off into the weeds at high warp.  I am flying towards the turn 5 corner workers, who scatter to watch my impending grass biting from a safe distance.  One plucky corner mistress stands her ground, using the time-tested arm flapping technique to stop my charge.  It works, and I stop just before the drainage ditch.  Now I have to wait for the whole world to go past (about 30 seconds) before getting back on the track.  I managed a sixth out of that fiasco.

Now I have had enough.  I get back to the pits, exorcise the handgrip-from-hell, and seek to buy a new, less diabolical one from the tire guru.  No luck. But he DOES have some Three-Bond grip adhesive.  Highly recommended.  It tamed that vulcanized hellspawn into a useable grip.  I will never use anything else (for grips).  Now I am ready for tomorrow.

Tomorrow comes, and practice goes off with relatively minor hitches. 

  1. My throttle cable breaks, but I have a new one in my racing tub!  What are the odds?? 
  2. The "quick disconnect fittings that you buy for fuel line from Lockhart-Phillips are actually lab gizmos.  They were never meant to see race gas.  Net result: They swell up and become "no reconnect" fittings.   It took two guys and some spit to plug my gas line back together.  Plenty of guys and spit on hand though. 

So now its race time again.  Sportsman Class: a confusing mix of obsolete 600's, two strokes, and generally weird machines.  I got a great launch. Super launch, in fact.  It was so super, I had to flail around a bit to get my feet onto the pegs.  Then I realized I didn't seem to have a peg on the right side.  In my flapping around, I had kicked the peg and folded it up. These are Raask rearsets we are talking about.  I was not especially impressed with how they worked in the first place (the shifter and brake lever hardware is kooky), but why do you need folding pegs with NO RETURN SPRING on a race bike?

So I am madly trying to get the damn thing down by feel, while steering towards turn one at 100 mph.  I don't really dare look down, do I?  I sure as hell don't want to be in turn one with no peg.  So after burning half my boot off on the muffler, and after everyone and their slow cousin goes by me, I get it folded down.  I bumble through that race, passed a few people and never really figured out where I finished.  Horse poo.

Now comes the Ultralight Superbike race.  I am jazzed for this one.  Make up for that last crud-run.  So I am waiting at the gate for the warm-up lap, and a CRA guy comes and tells me my grid has changed (everyone's had), and now I am in 5-E or somesuch.  This is because they moved the GP 125's from the back of the grid (a silly place to put the faster bikes) to the front of the grid where they belong.  No problem.  I did, however, fail to take into account that this put an extra WAVE in front of me.  I had already told my race-brain that there was one wave ahead of me.  To further complicate things, there has ALWAYS been a CRA guy or two in front of us holding up a clipboard if there is a wave leaving ahead of us. 

After the first flag dropped, these guys scattered.  Needless to say, when the second flag dropped, I was gone, as were some of my cohorts.  I was first into turn one!  First into turn two!  First into .......why isn't anyone chasing me?  So what was to be the big race got to be essentially a practice.   I did catch some of the experts and passed them just to make them feel bad.

To Summarize:

  1. The Michelin "Race" (not Race Soft") tires worked well, despite being mounted on smallish rims.
  2. Never use anything but Three-Bond grip adhesive for your grips. In particular, don't use slippery goo.
  3. Young biscuits in the pits may be distracting.
  4. Fuel "Quick-Disconnects" aren't.
  5. Raask rearsets with folding footpegs and no return springs are a menace to navigation.  Maybe Woodcraft sets would work better (they have solid-mount pegs).
  6. Race officials will play mind-games (inadvertent or not) to see if you are paying attention.
  7. New variables in work their way into the race situation. 
  8. If you are assuming too many things, you might be about to futz things up.

Asking for it.  June 23-25, 2000

Nature abhors order.  Chaos is a lower energy state.  What this means is, if you organize your underwear drawer, your sock drawer will instantly become a mess.  "Conservation of entropy" it sez in the textbooks.  If you organize your underwear AND your socks, your dresser will probably spontaneously combust  (so don't try this at home).  The same principle applies to racing.

If it were possible to look up the term "asking for it," it would say "See David Johnson, Evil Pumpkin Racing, CRA # 242".  Ya' see, I finally grew tired of my bike looking like a dented up Easter egg with wheels, so I got a new tail section, got a decent used fender, had the tank straightened and made the whole mess be one color.  I also pasted on some stickers from one of our sponsors, "Hi-Side Racing"  (if this were a movie, that would be called "foreshadowing").  Another factor that really tipped the scales was my stylish new Spidi leathers, which I just received from Lockhart-Phillips.  Naturally, I needed a new helmet too.  I was stylin'.  Briefly.  You will be happy to know that the Universe returned to chaotic equilibrium in turn 9 during practice on Friday afternoon.

The deal was, I have had good luck passing people on the brakes there (it is the slowest corner on the track) and I had been getting away with bouncing off the rumble strip on the outside when I occasionally overcooked it.  This time I went just over the top and sunk into the soft dirt next to the concrete strip.  This made an effective edge trap, and the inevitable high-side splat occurred.  Net result:

  1. Tank dented and scratched.
  2. Tail section scratched.
  3. Leathers scratched.
  4. Helmet scratched.
  5. Raask rearset shifter spinked off, mounting plate bent.
  6. Several million perfectly good human rump cells converted to miscellaneous organic debris

Despite this impressive list of carnage, the universe decided to make things a little more random in my arena, just for good measure.  For starters, the bike never ran right all weekend.  There was some carburetion malady that I could not sleuth out.  Not that I didn't try. I disassembled it so many times I will be removing that accursed airbox in my dreams for a month.  The weird thing was, the jetting didn't seem to matter.  No matter what I changed, the thing never made decent power, and it sputtered and bucked all the way through turns one and two (icky when leaned over at 100+ mph).  The only thing that helped was removing the air filter.  Bizarre!  The bike ran fine during the last race weekend with the identical parts, except for the addition of a Pingle petcock.  I think the deal was that the float height was just a little off, and the extra pressure from the "Pass More Gas" Pingle made it overflow into the carb throat.   This theory was supported by the fact that when I later went to start the bike at home, it was hydraulically locked by a cylinder full of gas!  It had oozed past the needle valve all the way home on the trailer and just filled 'er up.  When I went to start it, made a "clack" sound, as if a valve had gone to visit Mr. Piston.  Once I realized the engine was floating in gas, I decided to fix it.  Here are the steps you need to follow if this ever happens to you:

  1. Remove spark plugs and get those sparky wires away from everything gassy
  2. Move bike OUTSIDE
  3. Stand in FRONT of handlebars
  4. Push starter button
  5. Marvel at how far gas shoots out of the cylinder

Nevermind which of these steps were determined to be necessary AFTER the starter button was pressed.

Summary:

  1. Spidi leathers are comfy, stylish, and relatively protective of the squishy mammal inhabitants thereof.  Go buy some at Lockhart-Phillips and tell them I said so.
  2. Raask (Novella) rearsets would not stand up to being scolded by a gray squirrel (Sciurus carolinensis) at a distance of 20 yards, much less a crash at racing speeds.  When I went back to turn 9 to get my lever fragment, the ground was littered with pieces of Raask.  Hey.  I invented a new insult, as in "What a piece of Raask"!
  3. Race gas is a little more caustic on skin than the pump stuff.  And its blue.
  4. You can't win races when your bike runs poorly and you are deficient in rump cells.

Pumpkin Attrition - July 14-16

Considering my recent past, I never thought I would be writing this sentence, but NOTHING horrendous happened to me or the bike during this past race weekend. Well, OK. I had to switch carbs after the first practice, because I was STILL having problems in turns one and two with the bucking and wheezing thing it was doing last race weekend. The thing is, how do you diagnose a problem that only occurs at 10,000 rpm in top gear? I switched to some carbs that had been thoroughly sleuthed out and the problem went (mostly) away. This is much better than any of the other Pumpkins fared.

In order of occurrence:

  1. Rich (the Great Pumpkin) stayed home to sort his refrigerator magnets
  2. Jim (new Pumpkin-waif-adoptee) had a shift shaft seal fail and leak oil
  3. Mark crashed in turn 8 while looking at scenery. I believe it was a comely wood nymph.
  4. Tate kept saying "It won't blow itself"! His bike heard him and promptly blew itself (head gasket)
  5. Justin missed his race because he needed ice cream.
  6. Brian roasted his ignition exactly as I did 1 year ago (see "Tales of Fire and Metal Shavings")

By Sunday, I was left standing in a virtually empty pit. Tumbleweeds were blowing through. The wind made a low, mournful sound. No wait. That was Tate. As for racing, I was consistently 4th or 5th all weekend. I can not keep up with or even stay in the draft of the 5 fastest guys down the front straight, which at BIR is half of the track. Either they have had some serious motor work done, or there is a fat marmot living in my airbox. The bike did feel lean on the top end, so I think more carb twiddling is in order. I also had trouble with my shifter not doing what I want when I want. Piece of Raask.

Summary:

  1. I am STILL grossly unimpressed by the kooky, fragile Raask rearsets. As they say in Minnesota: ISH!  
  2. I need a marmot exterminator, a new motor, or a diet to loose 50 pounds of unsightly muscle and bone.
  3. RE: all the mechanical difficulties, racing is hard on parts and it is difficult to prepare for all potential problems. Just like the brown recluse spider in the outhouse of life, you think everything is fine until something bites you in the ass.

Need More Fun? Get More Bikes! September 15-17, 2000

I must say this last race weekend was the best ever. Perfect weather, a full pit o' happy Pumpkins, and my avoidance of the usual last minute attack of psittacosis, or whatever the local plague-of-the-month is. Steve Gorski, Chairman of the vast and powerful Evil Pumpkins East organization, drove out from New Jersey with his entourage for a visit. This was all a nice backdrop, but the main reason for the splendor of the weekend was GREAT BIG helpings of track time! I have been scrambling all year to get my new SV 650 race bike ready for this season. Now I have the GS 500 and the SV 650 to race. They run in different classes (ultralight and lightweight, respectively) and practice in different sessions.

Net result? I get to ride until my brain is gelatinized. The gelatinized state is blissful. The new bike is everything the GS 500 isn't. Fast. Modern. Stable. It lunges out of corners with a Chewbacca-esque "GRONK!" that only a V-twin can make. By comparison, the GS seems feeble. You can brazenly thwack it open mid-corner with nary a wobble, because nothing has really changed as far as power is concerned. And the sound…. like some unskilled musician blowing a trombone into the toilet: FWAAAB… GURGLE...PIFF…FWAAB! Having said this, I do not want to give up the GS. It has the simplicity and "charm" of Soviet farm machinery. Cheaper to run. Safer to crash. Easier to fix and scrounge parts for. The very same machine has been produced since 1989, with new model year updates consisting of different colored stripes. Riding this wheezer teaches you more about drafting and conservation of momentum (i.e. corner speed) than the big shrieking sauropods ever could. We've been through alot together. The SV is an addition, not a replacement. So how do you get into the lightweight class?

Follow this simple procedure:

  1. Scrape together all of your worldly liquid assets
  2. Dispense them

OK. If you want to know specifically how I did it:

  1. Buy a new SV 650 for $5200 at Larson's cycle in Cambridge, MN, or pay the take-it-or-leave-it full retail "special " $5749 + dealer set up + gouging fee at Hitching Post in the Twin Cities
  2. Call Lockhart-Phillips and Hi-Side Racing and give them whatever money you have left, then heat up the GM Mastercard (thinking to yourself "I'll be saving big on that new Chevy Hoverbox in 2047")
  3. Purchase and install Race Tech emulators and springs, Goodridge braided steel brakelines, Vortex clip-ons, Factory jet kit, M-4 Pipe, Michelin Pilot "race" tires, Auto Meter tach, RK 520 chain and Vortex sprockets, Daytona digital water temp gauge, Shark Skinz bodywork, Fox shock, Woodcraft rearsets, MB frame sliders, assorted screws, nuts, bolts, clamps, Water Wetter, brake fluid, oil, gas, and some racing air for the tires
  4. Purchase a Dremel tool and a grinder, then remove all the metal bits (i.e. the frame) that are in the way of the bodywork
  5. Stop wasting time and get to work. No, you DO NOT have time to take a whiz!

Assemble the whole mess of perfectly crafted (haw, haw, haw) aftermarket parts using the detailed and accurate (haw, haw, haw) instructions that they all come with. Example: "Mount the flimsy bracket to the motorcycle by drilling a 3/32" hole into the solid aluminum part of the frame. Insert the too large 1/8" rivets (not provided), which would never expand in a straight-sided hole in solid metal anyway, and then try to imagine why this would ever work". I did develop a special technique for working with the fiberglass bodywork. Take a plastic tub big enough to hold the largest piece, fill it with cold water, then every ten minutes during assembly, submerge your head and scream like a burning hyena.

As for the actual racing, it was mostly uneventful fun (if careening around BIR at a buk-sumthin' can be called uneventful). I did miss one practice because I dreamed I had put gas in the bike. Dang thing won't run on dream gas. Low octane. I got plenty of track time though, thanks to my gaggle of motorbikes. I was able to run six races on Saturday, which left me happily rubberized. I placed between 4th and 7th in these endeavors, depending on skill, luck, the actions of other pesky motorized mammals, and whether or not some sort of crud flew into my eye in the middle of a race. Here's a fun exercise: Try judging corner speed with only one eye open sometime. It is grossly unnerving. I almost had to break out the seeing-eye whippet.

On Sunday, Pumpkins Clint, Tate, and I ran the 5-hour endurance race on my GS. If you ever doubted that these bikes are tough, imagine 5 solid hours of screaming around on a track where you really are at full throttle more than half of the time. The heat coming off these things is probably blinding infrared satellites in orbit. We didn't actually make it for a full 5 hours. Tate (who shall henceforth be referred to as "Crashboy") crashed the faithful GS in turn 3 and caused some ugly but non-lethal carnage to the bike. There was some minor Tate damage as well, but he always grows back. We got the GS right back on the track and finished the race on the plucky unit. Now I am trying to imagine how to get the BIR core samples out of the tops of my fork tubes.

Summary:

  1. A simple equation: Two bikes are twice the fun of one bike. Save your money (food is for suckers) and get another one. If you eat less, you will be faster on the bike anyway.
  2. Installing aftermarket parts requires patience, some custom fab skills and tools, and takes about five times as long as it would take to install factory parts. It sucks to do this kind of work in a rush. You should start now.
  3. People who are due for a crash will be riding YOUR bike. They are all named "Crashboy".
