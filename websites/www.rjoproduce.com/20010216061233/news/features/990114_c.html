Last Option For Frozen Oranges: Juice

Growers try to cover some costs by turning to a 'salvage' operation

By Robert Rodriguez, The Fresno Bee
(exerpted from The Fresno Bee January 14, 1999)

 

Freez-damaged oranges can be processed into juice -- such as at this plant in Lindsay -- but the returns are far less profitable than selling fresh oranges.
Photos by Kurt Hegre - The Fresno Bee

LINDSAY  -   Every day, diesel trucks loaded down with 25 tons of citrus rum- ble into Phillip LoBue's juice-processing plant.

Unlike most of the Central Valley's citrus packinghouses idled by the '98 freeze, LoBue's Valley Foods Inc. is running nonstop. For nearly two weeks, the company's 35-person crew has been working 24 hours a day, seven days a week, squeezing freeze-damaged citrus into juice concentrate.

"We've had to tell our suppliers that they are limited as to how much they can deliver," LoBue said. "Because we just can't handle it all."

What's happening is what citrus industry officials said was inevitable. Growers are finding that most of their fruit is too badly damaged to be sold as ready-to-eat oranges.

Statewide, more than half the state's $1.5 billion citrus crop is said to be damaged - a majority of it grown in the Central Valley. Damage estimates for oranges alone is set at $473 million.

With that kind of situation, growers are turning to one of their only options: selling their oranges for juice.

Although county agricultural officials don't have an estimate of how much of the state's crop is being juiced, the volume has increased significantly since the pre-Christmas freeze. And juice processors, for the moment, are not concerned about finding enough markets for their product.

Sunkist Growers, the largest marketing cooperative with 6,500 growers in California and Arizona, has two juice processing plants - in Tipton and Ontario - that have been running 24-hour shifts for more than a week.

Sunkist sells its juice concentrate on the industrial market and for a variety of uses.

"This is by no means a lucrative option," said Claire Peters, Sunkist spokeswoman. "But at least we have a place for the oranges to go once they are off the tree."

The price for oranges made into juice averages $35 a ton as compared with a 40-pound carton of oranges that can sell for as much as $25.

Although juice sells for a lower price, growers can at least recover their costs for stripping the fruit off the tree - considered a must by most growers. Oranges left on the tree to decan can lead to disease.

"This is all strictly a salvage operation for nearly everyone involved," LoBue said. "There is just no way this can make up for what we have lost in the fresh market."

There is also little chance that California's navel juice will have much impact on juice prices overall. Florida supplies 90% of the Valencia oranges squeezed for juice, while California supplies an equal percentage of navel, or eating oranges.

Even with the 22% drop in Florida's crop, the state's major juice producers say they aren't expected to need California's navel juice.

"We do buy some from California, but it would on be Vanlencias," said Kristine Nickels, spokeswoman for Tropicana, the nations No. 1 juice maker. "We would not be buying California navels."

Overall, the nation's citrus crop is expected to be down 27% from last year's crop, largely as a result of bad weather.

Where California's juice goes is to the "private lavel" juice makers. LoBue's buyers include grocery stores, such as Ralph's Markets and Safeway, which produce juice under their own labels.

Despite the apparent flood of California juice, LoBue does not expect a glut in the market, at least not in the near future. Not all the damaged oranges can be made into juice, LoBue said. Heavily damaged fruit is already beginning to dry.

"We call that a powder puss, and we can't use it," he said.

What is usable can be stored as juice concentrated for up to a year in LoBue's cold storage. The room, lined with six 85,000-gallon floor-to-ceiling tanks, is kept at 18 degrees.

Like other juice processors, LoBue tries to use as much of the orange as possible. The rindes, scraped mechanically to remove the pulp, are sold for a variety of uses from marmalade to cattle feed.

LoBue says cattle growers are getting a good price - about $8 a ton - for the vitamin-rich rinds. "But even that price is falling as more product becomes available."
