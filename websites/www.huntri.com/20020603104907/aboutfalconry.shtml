All About Falconry
By James Gwiazdzinski

Falconry is the art of taking game and/or quarry with a trained bird of prey. Falconry is not pet keeping nor is it a passing fancy. It requires many hours of training and dedication. It is a 365-day hobby that many times becomes a passion and obsession. At the end of the hunting season, the bird that was flown during the hunting season must now be fed day-to-day and maintained in superior health.

With that said, I also have to mention that falconry is an extremely exciting method of hunting - bordering art, allowing a falconer an intimate understanding of, and bond with a hawk or falcon. The history of falconry is very rich and can be dated back at least 4,000 years, most likely evolving in the Far East. During the early years of falconry, the birds were used to help put food on the table, as opposed to the time of kings and queens who considered falconry more of a sport than a means of providing food. The invention of firearms was probably the biggest reason that falconry was lost in obscurity. Here in the United States, falconry continues to grow in popularity and though still obscure, is practiced with vigor by American falconers.

Falconry is the most regulated hunting sport regarding hunting. The United States Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) authorizes and regulates falconry, setting the minimum standards for individual states, such as Rhode Island, to implement.

Upon further reading you will find that the requirements to get a falconry license are very stringent. Many of the rules and regulations came from the input of falconers themselves when the regulations were being formed by the USFWS. What these standards do is protect the birds used for falconry purposes and make it so only the serious and committed individuals are able to obtain a falconry license. Here in Rhode Island, there are currently only three licensed falconers. Nationwide, as of 1991 there were only 3,738 licensed falconers. While the numbers may have grown since 1991, the amount of falconers compared to the overall population, is still extremely small.

So you want to be a falconer
-What it takes-

While there is a great deal more to be listed and written down, I will try and highlight the a fairly extensive list of requirements of what it takes to become a falconer. In order to get started, a potential falconer has to first find a sponsor willing to take you under his/her wing (sorry for the pun), during the first two years you are an apprentice. A quick note: It would be extremely important for a potential falconer to first join other falconers when they are going out hunting with their birds. Not only will you see the amount of effort that goes into the sport, but you will also be able to single out a falconer that might agree to sponsor you. There are three levels of falconry classes: apprentice - which is a two-year duration, general - which is a five-year duration, and finally master falconer.

The higher the level, the more birds a falconer is allowed to posses as well as a greater variety of different species of bird. For instance, an apprentice level falconer cannot fly a falcon until his third year (general level), and even at this time, many falconers continue to fly the bird they had during their apprentice years in order to get more experience with the sport and handling a raptor before moving onto another and often more difficult challenge.

Upon finding a sponsor, the next step is taking the state issued falconry exam, which requires a score of 80 percent or better. This can be done by contacting the Fish and Wildlife Division and asking for a test date. The test consists of fill-in answers, written answers, and multiple choice questions. Questions range from biomedicine, identification, health, laws and regulations, to incubation periods of the eggs of different bird species and other biological information. (It should be known that if the test is failed the first time there is a 90-day waiting period before the test can be retaken). After (hopefully) passing the test it is time to put your hammer and saw to use and build a facility known as a mews. No, this has nothing to do with the Mews Tavern in Wakefield - great selection of beer by the way. The mews is the outdoor holding area for the bird and should have dimensions measuring at least 8'x8'x8'. During this "building" process, equipment is being purchased and made in order to have the variety of falconry equipment ready when the bird has been trapped. One item that is extremely important is a scale to weigh the bird. Scales can be expensive, but they are essential for the successful falconer. Behavior and the weight of the bird are synonymous. An overweight bird will not be fit and unmotivated to hunt. This does not mean that the birds used are "starved" as some ill-informed individuals have speculated, simply, a "hunting" weight must be established. Once the mews has been constructed to federal and/or state specifications, a state fish and wildlife official will not only inspect the mews, but also make sure the proper equipment has been obtained. In addition to a scale, there are leather items such as bewits and/or anklets, jesses, leashes, perches, and food which must all be at the falconers dispense before a bird is legally trapped.

Finally, when the fish and wildlife has given the "okay" it is time to trap a bird, which would and should be done with the sponsor. This too requires a permit issued from the fish and wildlife division, without this permit you are not allowed to trap a bird. It is only the immature or passage bird that is allowed to be legally trapped.

There is a mortality rate of 70 percent or higher with birds of prey. The premise behind only immature birds being trapped is a mature bird or "haggard" should be left alone since it has beaten the odds and made it through its first year. The bird is therefore a "strong" specimen and should be allowed to breed and maintain a healthy raptor resource. Once a bird has been trapped - welcome to falconry. Prepare for long hours of training and manning your new bird. However, once the bird seems ready for hunting with a falconer, more challenges lie ahead in the hunting fields and woods. I've avoided a considerable amount of information on training and manning aspects of falconry here because there is too much information to put down without writing a book. But the long hours and fortitude has its rewards. Being able to witness your red-tail chase down a rabbit or corkscrew around a tree to get to a squirrel is worth all the work and long hours. It's like the Discovery channel live. I cannot stress this enough - what must be remembered, is falconry is actively hunting with a bird of prey. To do anything else is not falconry and one should not get involved in the sport if the "hunting" element of falconry is something that you cannot accept. It is also not fair to the bird you are employing to not be able to do what it knows best - and that is catching game - they are hunters, period. Also, a falconer should get out at least 3 times a week in order to have a well fit and successful bird.

Birds Used for Falconry

There are a variety of birds used for falconry. From the immense Golden Eagle flown by falconers out west, to North America's smallest falcon the American kestrel. One of the most popular birds used is also very abundant in Rhode Island - the red-tailed hawk. There is also the Harris' Hawk, which is an extremely social bird from the southwest and very popular among falconers. Their only draw back is their low tolerance to cold weather. Some of the more difficult birds to train would be the Northern Goshawk, the Copper's hawk, and the sharp-shinned hawk, all are known as accipiters. These birds are extremely quick and can be flown from the fist, but are prone to be moody and aggressive to the falconer if not properly handled by an experienced falconer. Anything from pheasant, duck, rabbit, squirrel, small birds, and some other quarry can be hunted by any of the birds listed above. It's simply matching the predator to the prey.

Falcons are also used and watching a falcon drop into a "stoop" from 800 to 1,000 feet is an incredible sight. Falcons can be flown on duck, pheasant, dove, various grouse out west, and a variety of other game depending on the falcon used and the location of the falconer. A kestrel can be flown on starling and sparrows, while the huge gyrfalcon can be flown on grouse and sometimes geese.

One of the more popular falcons flown is the Peregrine falcon, which is not trapped from the wild but rather, bought from a breeder. Yet another falcon used is the Prairie falcon, (refer to photo). This bird is much more temperamental than the Peregrine falcon and can be difficult to train. However, the prairie falcon is a very courageous bird and will tail-chase prey into cover, something a peregrine most often will not do. The Great Horned Owl is also used as a falconry bird, but training this species is very time consuming and requires not only a lot of time but also a lot of patience. There are many other birds used, some exotic and from other parts of the world, like an African Crowned Eagle which is used by very experienced falconers in Africa, and some that can be found sitting on a light post on the side of a highway like a red-tailed hawk. The list is simply too long to try and mention all the birds used in falconry. However, I have tried to list some of the more popular species flown by falconers across America and other parts of the world.

Some interesting facts:

  • The female is larger than the male in the bird of prey world.
  • Goshawks, especially the larger female will aggressively defend her nest and young.
  • Falcons such as the peregrine are capable of a stoop close to 200 mph.
  • Falconers are one of the main reasons the peregrine falcon was delisted off the endangered species list through such organizations as the Peregrine Fund.
  • For the first time since 1951, peregrine falcon chicks were born, raised, and fledged in Rhode Island within downtown Providence.
I wish you luck in your future falconry endeavors.
