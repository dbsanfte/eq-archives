Narrative to accompany the series of charts pertaining to the RI Turkey hunting season:
Brian C. Tefft, Principal Wildlife Biologist 6-15-00

Chart 1: Applicants for turkey permits are now at an all time high. This chart shows how early in our turkey program the number of permits exceeded the number of applicants. During the period 1992 through 1994 applications roughly equaled permits available. The number of permits available has increased since then steadily up to 1000 permits available by lottery. In 2000, the lottery resulted in 328 applicants who did not receive a permit.

Chart 2: This chart shows how the Rhode Island spring turkey harvest has risen rapidly beginning in 1992 to there being 147 birds taken in the 2000 season. The number of permits issued also rose steadily and is closely correlated with harvest.

Chart 3: This chart shows the success rates of Rhode Island turkey hunters. Successes rates of 20% or higher are commonly referenced as a benchmark for quality hunting in turkey hunting in states with established seasons. Rhode Island hunter success rates are just now starting to reach near the twenty- percent level the highest level was 16% in 1995 and 15% in the last two seasons. We have reached the 15% success level in only the last five years.

Chart 4: Interference rates among hunters are another important statistic. High rates of interference diminish the quality of the turkey hunting experience. Hunter education and better distribution of turkeys on private lands has helped keep interference rates low. Adding more permits can adversely influence hunt quality and interference rates.

Chart 5: A benchmark before the establishment of a fall hunting season is the spring harvest per square mile. Rhode Island management of turkey has chosen to promote quality spring turkey hunting. States with long established seasons, including West Virginia, establish a spring harvest of 1 bird per square mile of forested habitat as a minimum before a fall season is established in a particular county. The RI harvest per square mile of forested habitat was less than 0.4 bird and below the recommended goal for having a fall season.

Chart 6 and Chart 7: These charts compare two population indices, the brood index and the gobble index, with the annual harvest. Harvest has risen steadily since 1993 and is correlated with the rise in the gobble index. Harvest in the spring often reflects the previous summers production with high poult counts in any year accounting for more jakes in the next spring harvest. Several successive years of poor production can be reflected in poor spring harvest in future seasons.
