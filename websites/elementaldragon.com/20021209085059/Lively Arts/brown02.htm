Review

Ronald K. Brown Performance a Moving Experience
for Stanford Audience

Michael W. Phelan

And the crowd went wild. Stanford Lively Arts' presentation of Ronald K. Brown's Walking Out the Dark on October 26th was such a powerful experience that some members of the enthusiastically receptive audience admitted in a post-performance discussion that they had been moved to tears.

Brown's eclectic choreography blends modern dance vocabulary with influences from west Africa, Cuba, the Caribbean, Guinea and North American street dance. The result is a powerful, driving force that, if it doesn't move you to tears, is guaranteed to move your feet. A visibly austere work, Walking Out the Dark was performed by only four dancers, including Brown. The backdrops were simple monochromatic blends, and the lighting mere coordinated spot lights. Against this visually passive environment, Brown and his dancers worked their dynamic magic to the beat of forceful African music. These exotic rhythms, however, were punctuated by Brown's poetry and the serious opening piece, against which the vibrant, celebratory segments of the work shone.

The brief poetry recited by Brown, solo on a nearly-darkened stage, referred to the "hard truth" and distance between people who love each other. The truth, Brown explained in the post-performance discussion, refers to the anger between people who love each other, anger that keeps people in pain that feels like grief. When we are caught up in our egos, Brown elaborated, we do not understand that God is with us. When that happens, we are in exile and cannot reach across the chasm to the people we love. What he wants from his choreography, Brown says, is for the audience to breach this chasm of aloneness and feel what the dancers are feeling.

The opening segment of Walking Out the Dark depicts the four dancers in an initiation ceremony, derived from the culture of Burkina Farso, in which the initiated are buried in earth. The dancers express the initiates' fear and panic at being buried, and their relief as they eventually let go of their fear and trust that they will be all right. At this point of acceptance, the dancers are showered with a rainbow earthen rain, out of which they will arise and be reborn as adult members of their tribe.

Brown acknowledged that this aspect of the work was inspired in part by the longing of his siblings for each other after the death of their mother. Their longing for comfort from each other was a reaching out across the chasm of aloneness.

When asked what the traditional African dimension provides his work that he cannot get from modern western choreography, Brown answered that while Alvin Ailey opened the door for African American choreographers, Ailey had to legitimize his work by basing it upon a modern western foundation. Because of Ailey, Brown says he is freed to create work without the western basis, work with a sense of purpose that he wants to share across the chasm with the audience.
