Bolshoi's Swan Lake at Cal Performances
Inspires with Elegance, Power, and Enigmatic Russian Soul

Michael W. Phelan

On your list of top ten monuments to see in your life (Pyramids, Great Wall, Grand Canyon), include the Bolshoi Ballet's Swan Lake, preferably near the top of the list. The Bolshoi's production of this classic is a monument of both human and natural proportions, for their superb technical prowess as well as the aesthetic spell these muses cast on the soul.

Following the choreography of Yuri Grigorovich, Cal Performances' Sunday matinee lead roles were danced by Anna Antonicheva as Odette/Odile and Sergei Filin as Prince Siegfried. The elegant and powerful Antonicheva was equally convincing as the hapless, bewitched Odette and the forceful, bewitching Odile. Filin was substantially impressive with his power and drama as well as his superb technical precision.

The sensational cast included Ilya Ryzhakov as the demonic and manipulative Evil Genius. His diabolical influence on Prince Siegfried was almost palpable. As The Fool, Denis Medvedev wowed the audience with his dynamic leaps and tours.

Among the especially impressive for me were Elena Andrienko and Marianna Ryzhkina in the roles of Friend to the Prince in the festivities of Act One, Scene One. They danced as lightly and elegantly as though on air. Their synchronization and grace were beautiful, executing perfectly synchronized Arabesque allongée. I could have watched them all night. Notable among the brides-to-be were Inna Petrova as the lead Russina dancer. She and her followers seemed to convey a special joy and well-deserved pride in dancing their native Russian scene. Maria Alexandrova, as the Spanish dancer, conveyed a powerful grace and radiance. When dancing together the brides-to-be were exceptional. At one point, the five of them descended on one foot in unison so delicately they did not make a sound.

The set, by Simon Virsaladze, was dazzling in the bright, festival scenes, and entrancing in the dark, swan scenes. When The Evil Genius first revealed to the Prince the dark world—the suddenly illuminated backdrop of swan dancers behind a scree, their raised, limber arms writhing in unison—I actually felt goose bumps. Such was the magic worked here.

If there is a Dance of the Four Swans Hall of Fame, surely its hallowed ranks include Olga Zhurba, Svetlana Gnedova, Svetlana Pavlova and Daria Gurevich, who transfixed the audience with their precision, grace and spirit.

Anna Antonicheva as Odette captivated the audience with her spellbinding elegance, grace, power and spirit, not to mention her breathtaking multiple pirouette relevée en dehors executed with near-perfect precision. I could have watched her all night, every night.

But to me, the real stars of the performance were the Bolshoi's corps de ballet. It was as though they were without ego, displaying only a oneness with each other, executing each movement with the precision, grace and form essential to the story. Moving sublimely not so much in unison, but with the enigmatic Russian soul, the corps reveals the Bolshoi to be greater than the sum of its parts, their eloquent limbs and backs attuned to a monumental force of nature known only to the likes of Prince Siegfried and Yuri Grigorovich.
