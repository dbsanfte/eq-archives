Stick to the Dickens

The green, decaying, blurred world of Great Expectations is evocative of a weird dream. Set in the seawater-infused slums of Florida's Gulf Coast and the sooty artists' lofts of New York City, the film drips with surrealism like an overladen raincloud. But it is not the dreamlike world of a Dickens novel; director Alfonso Cuaron creates a bizarre reality of his own.

Cuaron's interpretation of the classic novel seems not so much based on Dickens but on the Great Expectations Cliffs Notes; the original story threads through the movie like a vague suggestion. What Cuaron is really interested in is creating a mood, setting up eerie, mystical scenes that drift by like a series of paintings. The film's main character, Finn - renamed from Dickens' Pip - is an artist, and Cuaron revels in this idea to such an extent that he seems to have borrowed Finn's charcoals and paints while filming. The entire movie appears to be painted from a single palette of greens and grays.

Though the film's mood is artistic and surreal, it is not a familiar fantasyland. In the first scene, the young Finn stands knee-deep in the ocean, surrounded by squawking gulls, sketching the fish that flash by his feet with grubby colored pencils. Suddenly, he is pulled underwater by Robert DeNiro - is this Cape Fear? Why a little boy in a huge shallow bay would be targeted by an escaped convict is never explained; the film invites the viewer to suspend reality. But as the story progresses, it becomes more difficult to conjure up genuine emotions or motives in the characters, mostly because reality has been suspended to such a degree that the entire plot doesn't make much sense.

Great Expectations follows Finn through his childhood days in Florida as he drifts from home - a poor fisherman's shack - to the overgrown decay of mansion owned by Ms. Dinsmoor, the wealthiest woman in town. Dinsmoor, played with campy glee by Anne Bancroft, is a certifiable loon who was left at the altar by her fiance years ago and hasn't cleaned house since. She invites Finn to visit weekly; her motive is to bait Finn with her bewitching young niece, Estella, whom she has raised to be an emotionless heartbreaker. (Why anyone would send their daughter to live apparently alone with a crazy recluse is also never explained.) Unsurprisingly, Finn swallows the hook, and the movie tails Finn and Estella (played as adults by Ethan Hawke and Gwyneth Paltrow) through twenty years of unrequited lust.

But all the glorious cinematography and artsy mood Cuaron can muster doesn't light a flame in the Finn-Estella romance. Hawke walks through the film in a daze, his character more reactive than active; he doesn't inspire much of a cheering section when spurned by his lady love. It doesn't help that his character is the center of inexplicable plot developments - it's hard to relate to a hero who finds himself a successful artist in New York City for no apparent reason. Paltrow serves her purpose as the gorgeous and cold Estella, heartbreaking and unpredictable, yet she too inspires no feeling because she appears to have none herself. Thus, the exchanges between Hawke and Paltrow wander in a confused emotional purgatory, trying to suggest deep meaning and passion that just isn't there.

The only truly erotic scene in the movie is the Titanic revisited - Estella shows up at Finn's apartment, strips, and poses for a portrait. This scene also inspires the best performance in the movie - not by an actor, but by Italian painter Francisco Clemente, the artist whose work acts as Finn's. His expressionistic sketches of Paltrow are a visual treat, as are the huge paintings Finn completes for his first gallery show.

Though the dreamlike aura of the movie is initially captivating, its lack of emotion and conflict is its undoing. Dickens' rich and convoluted themes are distilled into a few paragraphs and spoken aloud by Finn, explaining the tragedy of his situation. Cuaron would do better to show the audience why Finn's situation is tragic rather than having him tell the audience that it is. Cuaron's idea to bring Dickens' plot into the present is a good one, but it loses in translation the spirit of Dickens' old London setting and his equally spirited characters.

Ultimately, Great Expectations is very much like Paltrow's Estella - visually beautiful yet very aware of its own beauty, distracted and devoid of feeling. Cuaron's weird dream is, in the end, just weird.
