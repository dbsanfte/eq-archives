Heavy on talent, light on quality: Barry Levinson's Sphere

It would be easy to overlook Sphere's shortcomings were it not for a few minor details. These minor details are Dustin Hoffman, Sharon Stone, Samuel L. Jackson, Michael Crichton, and Barry Levinson.

What is truly amazing about the movie are not its inevitably huge special effects, gripping performances by Hollywood's favorite actors, or the nail-biting claustrophobic suspense created by a veteran director and famed best-selling novelist. The most amazing thing about Sphere is that it has none of these things. Despite its all-star cast and crew, Sphere is a big clunker of a movie that sinks faster than one of its deep-sea submarines.

For one thing, we've seen this all before. The plot of Sphere unfolds like so many sci-fi movies and television shows from years past: Isn't this from The Abyss? Was this scene in Alien? Didn't they already do this in The X-Files? An alien spacecraft has been discovered on the ocean floor. It looks like it's been there for 300 years, and there's still something alive inside. Enter Hoffman, Stone, Jackson, and Liev Schreiber, as a psychologist, biochemist, mathematician, and astrophysicist respectively, sent in as the alien welcome wagon to make contact with whatever is lurking in Davy Jones' locker.

The first twenty minutes of the film scramble to set up the plot at such a fast pace that the story itself almost seems irrelevant - hurry up, we've got to get these actors underwater because we spent millions of dollars on creating an underwater military base. After some assorted briefing and de-briefing that all-too-obviously foreshadows dangers to come - how long one can survive without a diving suit on, and so forth - the four Ph.D's are plunging towards the ocean floor.

Hoffman, as psychologist Norman Goodman, gives a typically appealing performance, though he most often must make do with screaming for help rather than delivering dialogue. Jackson is disappointingly sidelined as mathematician Harry Adams, relegated to spewing one-liners for the first portion of the movie until his character's consciousness is preempted by an alien force. Stone plays biochemist Beth Halperin with tough-chick bravado, despite the fact that she looks as if she's modelling the new spring line of Clinique makeup. And the supporting cast… well, let's just say that in the tradition of sci-fi thrillers, not many of them are around long enough to leave a lasting impression.

The plot becomes an increasingly muddled mess as disaster plagues the submerged team, as the film becomes a sequence of gasping-for-breath, near-death (or downright deadly) encounters all caused by an unknown and seemingly unembodied force connected in some way to the giant golden globe found in the submerged spacecraft. The golden "sphere" garners the award of the least well-done special effect in the movie; anyone who has used Adobe Photoshop has seen its computerized surface texture before. But is the sphere an alien entity causing these disasters? Or is it - all in their heads?

It is in attempting to achieve this ambiguity that the script seems to be so confused. It is not a bad plot device - we've seen it done on The Twilight Zone and The X-Files with extreme success - but in this case, it's just poorly executed. It would have helped the movie immensely to have Mulder and Scully show up and explain things; without any characters who seem reasonable or trustworthy, the audience ultimately ends up uncomfortably perplexed.

Sphere proves that an all-star cast, an Oscar-winning director, and a story from Michael Crichton do not add up to guaranteed success. In this case, they're an equation for mediocrity.
