shoreline ampitheater, 7/19/98

Phish shows are linked in my mind like a daisy chain. Each show is a little pocket in time that exists independently from the rest of my life; in this void I can only remember what transpired in the pockets before. Time doesn't pass in these pockets - the parking lot vendors and patchwork kids live there forever, disappearing like Brigadoon when a show is over.

At a Phish show you're part of the away team exploring a strange planet. You blend in with the natives surprisingly well, but are always conscious that you're only a visitor, and are not moving into orbit permanently around the Phish planet like that guy who sold you a veggie burrito. You stand in line and chat with some of the calico-clad natives and hope that they don't see the "Just Passing Through" scrawled on your forehead.

There was a time when I wanted to move here permanently. I'm always afraid that any show will seize me in its Vulcan mind-meld and suck me into the enclave of Phish vagabonds, that as Trey reaches a transcendent peak my brain will snap and I'll quit my job the next day. It's a mixture of disappointment and relief when this doesn't happen. It doesn't happen at this show.

This is one of those shows where they lose me. It's like any other long-term relationship - you blame yourself at first. "Is it me? It must be me." But you start to remember the links at the beginning of the daisy chain, when you and the band went willingly together to strange and crazy places, where you forgot yourself and knew only the music and its unity with your soaring thoughts. This time, they're not going anywhere you're interested in exploring. It's not for lack of ability on their part; the band is as tight as you've ever seen. There's no purpose. They careen head-on, louder, faster, louder, faster, into nowhere. Repeatedly.

As their talent and control has improved, so has their manipulation of the audience. They hold a tiny pattern of notes and hammer at it, making slight modulations until it becomes something else. They break into triumphant funk with such intensity that your body dances by itself. But your mind keeps waiting for the big golden ring on the carousel, and it never shows up.

There are the expected transcendent moments. Page's solo during "Jesus Left Chicago" is like champagne and city lights. "Piper" descends like a submarine into peaceful underwater depths. "You Enjoy Myself" is the usual mixture of divinity and Dr.Seuss. Mid-second-set, I am impressed. The band can exert perfect control over hurtling comets of noise. They seem to delight in the complex manipulations of sound and emotion, can go from tranquil to thunderous like a Porsche goes from zero to sixty.

But I'm not looking for an experimental study in musical theory. It's the golly-gee, genuine happiness that I've found with the band in the past that brings me back every time. As they've gotten increasingly occupied with intentionally exploring their sound, they seem to have lost the innocence they had when they first discovered the bubbling stream in "Reba" and the mountain's summit in "Tweezer Reprise." Back then, they were just happy to have arrived anywhere at all, and happy that we were all with them, and had made it this far. They weren't just tour guides; they were friends.

And they still are friends. Trey can flash his shit-eating grin and start to bounce and I'm with him every step of the way. Shoreline still brought me to strange and crazy places. They'll always have something new to show me, and I'm learning to look harder for that elusive golden ring. And the next time I thread a new flower into the daisy chain, the patchwork kids will appear, and there will be puppies and burritos and Fishman will wear his purple mumu. These days, that's all I need.
