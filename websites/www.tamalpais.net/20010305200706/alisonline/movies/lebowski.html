The Big Lebowski: Bowling, nihilists, and White Russians

There are certain things you can expect from a Coen brothers movie. It's likely that the film will be ten degrees removed from reality, and ten degrees in a pretty odd direction. There's a good chance that the characters will be outstandingly strange, and will share some witty and often wonderful dialogue. A botched kidnap plot is a strong possibility. The words "bizarre sequence of events" will describe the plot fairly well.

In living up to these criteria, "The Big Lebowski" doesn't disappoint. The latest offering from writer-director-producer team Ethan and Joel Coen, "The Big Lebowski" has many of the ingredients that make Coen films so entertaining, a formula that doesn't always guarantee success but ensures a memorable viewing experience. Though "The Big Lebowski" never achieves the comic genius of "Raising Arizona" or the pure serendipity of the Oscar-nominated "Fargo," it's still a worthwhile addition to the Coen library of innovative and enjoyable filmmaking.

"The Big Lebowski" is the story of Jeffrey Lebowski, alias the Dude (Jeff Bridges), an easygoing Angeleno stoner who spends most of his days drifting between his Venice apartment and the local bowling alley. The Dude's mellow schedule is disrupted when two thugs break into his apartment, mistaking him for another Jeffrey Lebowski - a filthy-rich tycoon whose trophy wife Bunny owes money all over town. The Dude obviously won't pay up, one of the thugs urinates on his rug, and suddenly his life is hopelessly entangled with an eccentric cast of characters who all want a piece of the Big Lebowski's pie.

Bridges is effortlessly appealing as the Dude, shuffling around in dirty shorts and flip-flops and sporting an impressively real beer gut. The Dude's shaggy, space-cadet manner is offset perfectly by his best pal and bowling buddy, Walter Sobchak, played by John Goodman. Goodman virtually lights up the screen as Walter, a belligerent Vietnam vet who sees nothing abnormal about whipping out a firearm during a bowling dispute. Rounding out their bowling team is Donny (Steve Buscemi), three steps behind any conversation and perpetually abused by loose cannon Walter.

Through the Dude's bumbling escapades we meet a cast of trademark Coen lunatics. There's a trio of nihilist Germans in black turtlenecks and combat boots, a landlord who delights in modern dance, and a porn king who has naked trampoline parties at his Malibu pad, for starters. But John Tuturro brings down the house as rival bowler Jesus Quintana, a convicted sex offender who swivels and thrusts his way around the bowling alley in a delicious lavender jumpsuit.

And amazingly, we love these weirdos. The Coens don't treat them in a negative, derogatory way; wackos like Walter and Jesus are portrayed with good-natured affection and sympathy. We can understand these people - or at least appreciate them.

This oddball ensemble contributes to some inspired situations, but ultimately the movie's plot wanders around as aimlessly as the Dude himself; it comes off as a sequence of strung-together scenarios rather than a unified whole. Though some attempt is made to give it coherence by the narration and occasional appearance of a lone-cowboy character, "The Stranger," the gunslinger's arrival is merely confusing at best. Adding to the confusion are fantasy sequences that occur whenever the Dude receives a knock on the head; these '40s-style musical numbers are a funny idea but often break up whatever rhythm the script has created.

Thankfully, "The Big Lebowski" diverges from past Coen movies in one pleasant respect: it doesn't degenerate into a bloodbath at the end, a la "Raising Arizona" or "Fargo." However, it lacks a certain luster that made both of those movies so great: a strong leading actor. Bridges is pleasing, but ultimately not interesting enough to carry the movie on his own charisma; both "Raising Arizona" and "Fargo" owe much to their respective stars, Nicholas Cage and Frances McDormand, who won an Oscar for her "Fargo" performance.

What makes the movie worthwhile, and what will probably make it a video classic in future years, are the performances of its supporting cast. "The Big Lebowski" doesn't bowl a strike, but it's got at least eight pins down. And one is teetering.
