A three hour tour: James Cameron's Titanic pleasure cruise

It would be easy to draw sweeping parallels between the ill-fated ocean liner Titanic and James Cameron's film about the disaster. Both are epic events of gargantuan proportion, lavish and indulgent, opulent and thrilling. I'm sure every film critic who has seen the movie has been itching to say something like, "But alas, the film Titanic sunk like the ship itself." So I tried my best to dislike the film. But Titanic's stupendous effects and rollercoaster rush were about as hard not to like as a Hershey bar, and after three and a half entertaining hours, I had to admit that Cameron's Titanic was a pleasure cruise.

The movie does share some similarities with the ship. They're both extraordinarily long. The pace of Cameron's film is as indulgent and luxurious as the Titanic's grand promenades and dining rooms. Cameron's camerawork seems eager to show off every penny of the reported $200 million that went into the making of the film, a budget that included a 90 percent scale recreation of half of the ship. Though the film's attention to detail is one of its greatest assets, it does contribute to its unhurried pace. Look - they've re-created the china pattern. Want to see it again? And again?

But given the obvious effort that went into the making of the movie, it's easy to grant Cameron a little extravagance here and there. Because the film is often downright spectacular. The underwater footage of the Titanic wreck, the thunderous sound effects of water exploding through walls, and the cinematic wizardry of making that huge ship sink are unforgettable on the big screen. It's a real movie-lover's movie, complete with a damsel in distress, a free-spirited young hero, and a generous measure of disaster and destruction.

Oh yes - there are actors in this movie. Leonardo diCaprio and Kate Winslet are surprisingly appealing as the lovestruck couple around which the movie's plot revolves. DiCaprio manages to make the plucky Jack Dawson believable despite having to deliver lines like "When you got nothin', you got nothin' to lose." Winslet is equally plucky as Rose, a society girl trapped into marrying the evil Cal (Billy Zane) for his millions. Perhaps most memorable about Winslet's performance is the expanse of creamy cleavage she exposes in every scene in gorgeous dresses of the period. The camera often seems to take as much delight in the beauty of its young stars as it does in the beauty of Cameron's sets.

The delight that Cameron seems to have taken in making the movie is what gives the film its greatest appeal. Titanic never pretends to be more than it is: an extremely entertaining flick meant to be an enjoyable spectacle. It's true eye candy - young lovers kissing at sunset on the bow of the ship; huge pistons pumping in the sweaty, coal-fired lower decks; thousands of bodies floating under the stars with ice crystals in their hair.

What Titanic sacrifices in favor of pure audience satisfaction, however, is any sense of creativity or surprise. Of course, the filmmakers are working against some fairly public knowledge - it's not like it's a shock that the Titanic is going to sink. And since Cameron chooses to tell the story through the elderly Rose's flashbacks, it's already established at the movie's start that she will survive. But the movie never takes a single unpredictable turn - the storyline glides toward its inevitable conclusion like a certain ship that's too large to turn quickly. Heroine Rose appears to have the glance of death during the movie's drawn-out climax; anyone she makes meaningful eye contact with while the ship is sinking is certain to meet a watery demise.

Cameron seems so eager to please that he often undermines the impact of the story's plot by introducing superfluous sidebars. It doesn't seem like enough that he's chosen to film one of the greatest disasters of all time; he has to supplement the action with a few crowd-pleasing distractions, just in case anyone were to get bored with the spectacular destruction of the largest ship ever built. Thus, Jack and Rose find themselves fleeing through the flooded dining room while jilted lover Cal chases them with a gun. Did it possibly occur to Cal that Jack and Rose might die due to the North Atlantic ocean swallowing the entire ship?

And then there's the cliched "classist" subplot, thrown in for good measure to round out the classic "good vs. evil" conflict. To make the storyline go down in easily digestible nuggets, Cameron gives moviegoers a sweeping generalization of kind, downtrodden poor folks locked in lower decks by the evil, wealthy passengers in First Class. One especially eye-rolling scene involves a poor woman putting her two adorable moppet children to bed for the last time because diabolical rich people have taken all of the lifeboats.

Yet for every scene that is a groaning cliché, there is one that is shockingly poignant. The band playing to the passengers as they board the lifeboats; the preacher who has time to deliver a final sermon while the rear of the ship bobs like a cork before sinking; the elderly couple who lie on a bed embracing as the water level rises around them. During an event as ponderous as the sinking of the Titanic, the scenes that are more subtle carry greater weight.

Had Cameron let the film's tragedy speak for itself, Titanic might have been a truly fantastic movie. But his efforts to entertain are ultimately effective. After all, everybody likes candy.
