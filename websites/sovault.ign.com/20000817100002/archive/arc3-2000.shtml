Wednesday, April 26, 2000

Vault Network Enlightens Dark Zion

As the online persistent world category continues to grow and evolve, one of the most exciting aspects to watch is the way each new project reveals different design ideas and influences. For this reason, it can be pretty difficult knowing that a potentially exciting project is in progress, but still waiting patiently until its developer is ready to unveil it. A bit earlier today, Wombat Games formally announced Dark Zion, a game we’ve been waiting to learn about for some time, and one that seems certain to attract considerable interest from the online gaming community.

At a time when game makers with experience creating online worlds are at a premium, Wombat’s co-founders include Rick Delaschmit, former Lead Programmer of Ultima Online, and Jason Spangler who held the same position on the Ultima Online: The Second Age expansion. In addition, the team’s Creative Consultant is the internationally acclaimed New York Times best-selling author Tad Williams.

To bring the online gaming community the best possible coverage of this exciting new title, the IGN Vault Network announces the opening of our latest dedicated site, Dark Zion Vault. And as the highlight of our launch, we are pleased to present the first-ever exclusive and very extensive interview with the game’s principals at Wombat Games. Here’s a brief summary of the game to pique your interest:
Dark Zion is a player-driven, player-controlled persistent online world. Players will have an unprecedented level of control over their environment; beyond building a character, a player will have the opportunity to build a business, a town, an army, even an entire society. There will be no arbitrary quests or super-imposed events; the story is the success or failure of the world at large.
Other features at Dark Zion Vault will include game information, news, concept art, screenshots, humor, reader and community participation features, humor and much more. New features and content will begin to appear in the very near future.

We invite all members of the community who are interested in the future of online gaming to stop in and check out the coming excitement from Wombat Games and best-selling author Tad Williams at our brand new Dark Zion Vault. Enjoy!

Wednesday, April 12, 2000

Triumverate Of Posts From Clint

Resources:
Cities are tied to resources. The collection of resources is done with the construction of a city. There will not be any resource "collection" vehicles. Each terrain has the ability to generate specific resources that the players can build cities and collect.
Micromanagement:
One of the goals in Sovereign was to minimize the overall micromanagement that is required to maintain an empire. Due to the scale of the game we had to simplify certain areas that players are used to having total control over. We want the game to appeal to the masses and making the game an intricate system that only the most diehard gamer could use would prohibit maximum exposure.
Unit Sharing:
I have been playing RTS games for a long time and I haven't found a single person yet that I could trust enough to run my game in a manner that I feel "safe" with. On that thought, there are no plans to allow multiple people to share a single empire. Players do have the option to turn over control of units and or cities to other players but the key concepts that needs to be remembered is that the player that owns the units will always have total control over the "shared" items. This means that the owner can at anytime override the control of the "sharing" player. I am interested in this concept and look forward to the beta to see it in action.

-Clint Worley
Producer/ Designer Sovereign
Verant Interactive Studios

Nukes And Consequences

If you haven't noticed yet, our Sovereign devboard is the place to be if you want to chat with the lead developer of Sovereign, Clint Worley. Here he responds to some concerns about nuclear weapons:
From the get go, I wanted nuclear weapons in Sovereign. I understand the initial impression that they give to players, they won't be balanced, players will use them as fast as they can build them. Well my goal as a game designer is to make the nukes powerful and threatening but with a dire consequence that will make the player seriously consider the use of the weapon. Your empire will react accordingly to the production, stockpiling and use of nuclear weapons. The bottom line is that people as a whole DO NOT LIKE NUCLEAR WEAPONS! Make them, use them, its up to you, but you also have to deal with the consequences. You may destroy another player’s city with a nuke but your people hating their leader might lose your city.

Artificial Intelligence

This is a sensitive topic in Sovereign, as an AI will be in charge of your empire for lengthy and potentially critical stretches of game time. Here are some thoughts from lead designer Clint Worley:
I agree with the concepts that you are speaking on. The problem is that we all love RTS games and the A.I. in RTS games has come a long way in the past few years. There is still a long way to go when it comes to realistic A.I. that acts and reacts like a human counterpart. Usually the issue that surrounds A.I. in RTS games is that you want it to be more complicated but still keep a simple and straightforward interaction between the player and the game. You don’t want to complicate the gameplay with A.I. settings, you want the user to have fun and the units to act as natural as possible. The A.I. in Sovereign recognizes potential threats and assigns priority when attacking. This should help to solve your complaints with traditional RTS A.I.

-Clint Worley
Producer/ Designer Sovereign
Verant interactive Studios

Two From Clint

Clint took some time to answer a couple of questions over on the Sony boards this morning:
Experience:
There are many ways to gain and also lose experience in Sovereign. Each individual unit is capable of gaining experience and the experience that units, cities, skills and technologies accumulate all go towards the total experience tied to that character. The experience that a character gains is used to research new skills and technologies.

Leaving a game:
Players will be able to "pull" a character out of a game when they would like. We offer many types of gameplay to the players. We strongly urge players to play in games that they feel they can commit the time to playing in. If you are a more casual gamer, it would not be in your best interest to register into an unlimited time game.
We do keep history logs on the player’s characters and that is one of the ways that Sovereign is persistent.

-Clint Worley
Producer/ Designer Sovereign
Verant Interactive Studios

Units can be healed in cities. There is a possibility in the future that we include "medic" units that can do field repairs. At this point we make the user return to a city and repair there.

-Clint Worley
Producer/ Designer Sovereign
Verant Interactive Studios

History Of RTS

Incite Magazine has published a game by game retrospective of the RTS genre, beginning with a look at Command HQ from 1990 and continuing through last year's Homeworld. Comments from notables like Chris Taylor are sprinkled throughout the article. Here's their teaser:
Strategy gaming was once the realm of the sedate; those who chose to ponder their moves over a cold beer, plan the precise strategy that would bring them victory, reap the cerebral satisfaction of outwitting a worthy opponent. The previous millenium's final decade changed all that. Like a gust of fresh air into a stuffy room, real-time games blew into the staid parlor of strategy, changing it forever.

Although certainly not THE most popular genre of the '90s — that honor would go to adventure games (believe it or not) — it has certainly been one of the most influential. Remove real-time strategy games from PC Data's decade-long top 20 (complied since 1993) and you're left with two strategy titles: Sim City 2000 and Sim City Classic. And in case you haven't noticed, they are real-time themselves, albeit without combat.
It's easy to see after reading this article that Sovereign will be a landmark game in the RTS genre.

Saturday, April 1, 2000

The Economist Class

Those interested in engagement without bullets will be pleased to know that Trade and Economy were actually developed before combat, as Clint explains on our Sovereign devboard:
We decided early in the game development to focus on the trading and economy model of the game. Our goal was to have fun playing the game without combat. We figured that a game that is fun WITHOUT combat would be a blast with. Due to the focus on trade and the economy we have built a solid foundation to build the rest of the game on. That is why we have been focusing on combat lately, it has the biggest visual payoff and we don't want to let anyone down that makes it out to L.A. this year.

-Clint Worley
Producer/ Designer Sovereign
Verant Interactive Studios
