We have pre-loaded our my.ign.com user database with your old Vault network username/password. However, we had to prefix your username with "vn_". Monday, December 17, 2001

Which guy are you talking about?

Froech started a thread on the official forums today asking how may people a person can deal with at one time before a well oiled campaign turns into chaos.
OK, here's the question... and I guess it will be for the rest of the month, since after next Monday, we'll be off until next year. Just as an FYI, I have to attend a service in NoVA, so I'll be away from the boards for a while, with no way of accessing, so y'all be nice now.

Question: How many people can you effectively team with, or against?

We've discussed this briefly before, but I'm really interested now, due to some discussions we've been having on the team about other games.

Is there a limit to how much information you can process? What is that limit? For instance, in a "raid" in EQ, how many people can effectively participate, without total communication breakdown?

In other games, that feature large masses of people attacking other large masses of people (Camelot! Shhh! It's only a model!), what is the effective bandwidth of information you can process?

I tend to play a lot of a text based game, when I have time, and there have been in the past lots of invasions of masses of critters to kill. Once you got about 6 or 10 people in a room, trying to coordinate attacks got hairy, but part of that was a result of the method by which the information was imparted (textually, rather than graphically).

But even with a better visual interface, I still think that coordinating 20 or 30 people into an attack, each controlling on the order of 100 units (the prior discussed unit cap), it could get hairy. There obviously will be a lot of communication going on, coordination of troop movements, etc... but at some point, aren't we going to hit information overload?

And when we do, where is that point? Is it 30? 50? 250 people?

The side issue is how sure will you be of proper coordination. My experience online is that it is harder than not to get people to stick to a single objective. As game players, we all tend to believe that our ideas are better than other's, and to move into that direction. Most of us don't have military training, to follow orders blindly, especially if we don't understand the strategy, or see another easier short term goal that benefits us directly.

So, what do you think? How many of you expect to have guilds that are raiding other guild planets, struggling for control of the planet? How many compatriots / commanders do you expect to fight with, or against, simultaneously?

I'm truly interested in your discussion... mostly because I'm not convinced of my own position here. So, tell me what you think, please!

Froech

More on WoMD

Froech had more to say on Weapons of Mass Destruction on the official forum today.
From a real world perspective, I'd agree that notification doesn't make much sense (although I do like the background radiation concept), but looking at it from the side of gameplay, it would be hard to not provide some sort of warning.

Kinda like if you were playing in EQ, and hunting snakes, and suddenly the snake, which has been hitting you for 2 or 3 damage every bite, suddenly hits you for 10,000 hit points of poison damage.

Now, snakes do have poison, so you don't want to take away that aspect (WoMD need to exist), but how fun would it be not to know that the snake is going to bite you with posin sacks. You'd either want to identify the snakes capable of it as some different kind of snake (poison snake, vs garden snake), or show some sort of visual representation (red vs green) so you know to stay away from that kind of snake. So, take that over to a game (and remember, we *are* talking about a game here), we need to provide some method by which you're aware of a WoMD.

Otherwise, there just won't be a game, it will be just a race, won by the first person who gets WoMD.

Not fun, in my book.

Froech

Saturday, November 10, 2001

She can't take this much longer!

Froech had the following to say about a discussion that's begun on the Official Sovereign Message Boards on the difficulty of keeping the bandwidth used between Sovereign's client/server within reasonable levels.
Nice to see you all discussing this. I'd agree that state transition might be the best answer. At least in terms of keeping bandwidth down, it seems that limiting the amount of information you have to send makes sense.

On the other hand, Larry has a point too... if you keep too much information client side, doesn't it expose it to the hackers? (not really a rhetorical question, the answer is yes!). Which rules out that answer (or does it?)

But as to the keeping track of only half the globe, I'd suggest it may not be that easy. What if Dach is on the other side of the world... but I'm busy fighting Larry, and Dach's units are shooting just over the horizon at my units just on this side of the horizon...

Or, what if I have units on both sides of the world? Say, I put a unit every 10 degrees around the equator? Don't you have to feed me the information around each unit? How much bandwidth can you dedicate to a single user?

Which brings up the other side of things... how much bandwidth can your server handle. Won't that define how many people can be on a world at the same time? And if you want to be fairly quick at sending updates, how much bandwidth can you use before you start getting lag? Sure, you'll have to design around internet lag, but at some point, you're going to have to say that you have a budget of X bits per second that you can dedicate to a user. But, you can get a bit slippery with that number, can't you? That's an average bit rate, isn't it? What about bursting? How often can you burst information? At what rate and for how long? Can game design work within those restrictions, or do we need to make changes? Hey, what are we paying for bandwidth? Not just in terms of processing budget, but real dollars? Will we still make money if we charge less than $1000 per hour/month/time period of your choosing?

You guys have the right idea of the problems, I think. This is all pretty easy stuff for a single player game. Where it gets a wee bit interesting is when you try to do it for several thousand users simultaneously (and no, we're not changing this to a single player game!)

Froech

Saturday, November 3, 2001

If we told you, we'd have to kill you.

After a month's silence Froech posted on the official forums again today. The following was a reply he made about being away, and the reasons for a lack of public information on Sovereign.
Well, allow me to add some additional information to your current direction.

Option 3. Many developers have noticed that when they post direction/plans/systems for public content, they sometimes end up in other games. Those other games may be further in the development process, and be released before you might release your own product.

One of the problems I think we all have with this incestuous circle of game developer and fans is that we tend to goad each other into an ever cyclic action/reaction of release and discussion. Sometimes, this is ideal, sometimes it isn't. We feel it is important enough to dedicate team members (like me) to the process, along with management oversight on what can and can't be released.

As I've said before, this company is in business. That means profit. Yes, we want to do cool games. Yes, we want to play our own games, but at the end of the day, we're still in competition for your dollars with every other online game company out there.

We walk a tight rope every day between the idea that we want to have as open a design process as possible, to involve you as much as possible in the ideas, concepts, direction that this game has, and the opposing concept of other people taking our ideas and beating us to market.

To be truthful, I've seen it happen before. It didn't make me happy. The game developer I worked with came up with a pretty damn unique idea, and we pitched the game concept to another company for backing. They decided not to spend the investment money on our project, but their next project that came out a year later had that same unique idea implemented in exactly the same method we had demonstrated to them under a supposed "NDA" type of agreement.

Do we, as developers, wish we didn't have this limitation? I think we all feel that way, not just here, but I think across the entire industry. Does that stop people from looking at other games in development for ideas? I'm afraid not. I wish it did.

You guys want more info. We want to give you more information, too. But at this point, the plain and simple fact is that it doesn't make sense for us, from a business perspective.

Now, the opposite side of that statement is that when it becomes intelligent for us to bombard you with information, we will absolutely be doing so. I promise you that.

WarOrk, I can assure you that none of the statements you made in your post have any basis in reality.

Now, as for where I've been... my most sincere apologies to you all. My other tasks on the team have swamped me, and I've been dealing with some personal issues in my private life that haven't given me any spare time that I normally would devote to you all.

We're moving on with this project. I'm quite pleased with what I'm seeing, and I'm sure once we're able to share this with you, you will be too. I know I've said that before... and I'm sure I'll say it again. Because I believe it.

Froech

Friday, October 12, 2001

Brad McQuaid Leaves SOE

Sony Online Entertainment announced today in a press release that Brad McQuaid, one of the masterminds behind the wildly popular Everquest, has decided to leave SOE to explore other opportunities in the massively multiplayer market. It's sad to see Mr. McQuaid go but I'm sure this is not the last we've heard from him.
SAN DIEGO, CA - October 12, 2001 - Sony Online Entertainment Inc. (SOE), announced today that Brad McQuaid, one of the original creators of EverQuest, will be leaving the company after five years of service. Mr. McQuaid was instrumental in the launch and success of the world's largest subscription-based online game and was a key spokesman in the online community that nurtured EverQuest's growth.

"After five years of building the EverQuest franchise for Sony Online Entertainment, it's time for me to explore other opportunities in the creative development of massively multiplayer games," said Brad McQuaid. "I wish SOE the greatest success with all future releases and I have the greatest confidence in the ability of the development staff that we've assembled over the past few years to continue expanding the EverQuest experience."

"Brad's contribution to gaming philosophy that evolved into EverQuest has been recognized throughout the gaming world as one of the premier accomplishments of the past decade in the business," states John Smedley, Chief Operating Officer, Sony Online Entertainment. "It's been a pleasure working with Brad for the past few years as a colleague and a friend and I'm certain that he will continue to contribute innovative and exciting game technology in the future."

"Brad's passion for excellence in all things EverQuest has made a material impact in the success of this groundbreaking title," said Kelly Flock, President, Sony Online Entertainment. "I know he will continue to be a force in this emerging market and all of us at Sony Online Entertainment look forward to the possibility of working with him again in the future."

John Smedley, Chief Operating Officer and a partner in the conception and production of EverQuest, will assume Brad McQuaid's responsibilities.

Sony Online Entertainment Inc. (SOE), the online gaming division of Sony Pictures Digital Entertainment, is a worldwide leader in massively multiplayer online gaming. Headquartered in San Diego, California, SOE develops, publishes and distributes entertainment for the PC and online markets. With more than 10 million registered users, SOE's award-winning website, The Station® (www.station.com) hosts a variety of entertaining games and player communities spanning all genres. In addition to blockbuster hits EverQuest® and Jeopardy! Online, SOE has an array of cutting-edge online games in development such as PlanetSide™, Star Wars® Galaxies™ and Sovereign™.
